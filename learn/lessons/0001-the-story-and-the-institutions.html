Why would a stronger rival bring another buyer in?

Start with the decision to investigate a company. Then follow the information that helps a buyer make that decision.

Study in short sectionsNo auction theory neededPaper: Sections 1 and 2
A company is for sale. One buyer is ready to bid. Another is deciding whether to spend money preparing a bid. Usually, a stronger rival makes that spending less attractive. This paper explains how the rival can also make the company's stock price more useful to the second buyer. Sometimes that extra information brings the second buyer into the sale.

1. Meet the company and its two possible buyers

We will use the same fictional company throughout the course. Call it Target. Its shares trade on a stock exchange, and its owners are considering selling the company.

Buyer A has already investigated Target and is ready to bid. The paper calls A the incumbent. Here that word means the buyer already prepared to participate. It does not mean Target's current owner or manager.

Buyer B is interested but has not yet done the work required to submit a bid. The paper calls B the challenger. There is only one Buyer B. Its eventual investigation cost can be low or high; these are two possible circumstances for the same buyer.

The company can be worth different amounts to A and B. Perhaps B can sell Target's products through its own distribution network. If the two businesses fit well, buying Target is valuable to B. If the fit is poor, its value to B is much lower.

By a buyer's value, we mean what owning Target is worth to that buyer before paying the purchase price. Value and purchase price are different. If ownership is worth 10 and the buyer pays 3, its acquisition profit is 7 before investigation costs.

All our monetary numbers are measured per Target share, as in the paper. A preparation cost of 1 means total preparation spending divided by the number of Target shares equals 1. It is not a claim that investigating a company costs one pound in total.

2. What is B deciding?

B must hire advisers, check information, arrange the transaction, and establish what it can offer. We call this work preparation. Due diligence is part of it.

In the model, paying for preparation does two things. It tells B its exact acquisition value, and it makes B able to submit an executable bid. If B declines, it cannot skip the work and bid its current best guess.

B knows what the preparation will cost before deciding. It does not yet know whether its acquisition value is high or low. Its decision is therefore based on an average over those possibilities.

$$\text{Prepare if expected acquisition profit}\ \ge\ \text{preparation cost}.$$

Acquisition profit already subtracts the price B expects to pay if it wins. We then compare that profit with the separate cost of getting ready to bid.

For a small arithmetic example, suppose B expects an acquisition profit of 8 if the fit is good and 0 if the fit is poor. If each outcome is equally likely, its expected profit is

$$\tfrac12\times8+\tfrac12\times0=4.$$

Preparation costing 6 would not be worthwhile. Now suppose useful evidence raises the probability of a good fit to 80%. The expected profit becomes

$$0.8\times8+0.2\times0=6.4.$$

B now prepares. The investigation did not become cheaper. B received evidence that made paying for it more attractive. These rounded numbers illustrate the decision only; later we calculate the paper's exact profits.

Check the decision

B expects a profit of 8 in a good fit and 0 in a poor fit. Preparation costs 6. What changes when the probability of a good fit rises from 50% to 80%?
Yes. The expected benefit rises from 4 to 6.4, while the cost remains 6.
Compare expected profit with cost. The average profit changes from 4 to 6.4. Neither the cost nor the requirement to prepare changes.

3. Why does a stronger A normally discourage B?

The model uses a second-price auction. Each prepared buyer bids its value. The higher bid wins, but the winner pays the other buyer's bid, subject to a minimum price set by the seller. We will prove why truthful bidding works in Lesson 2.

Hold B's value at 10. If A bids 2, B wins and pays 2, keeping 8. If A bids 7, B still wins, but keeps only 3. If B instead has value 1 and A bids 2, B loses and earns zero from the auction.

So a stronger A reduces what B expects to earn. If B has to pay for preparation first, that makes preparation less attractive. The paper keeps this effect. It will prove that expected acquisition profit falls as A strengthens, whenever B's information is held fixed.

The phrase held fixed matters. We compare buyers who assign the same probability to a good fit. Later, the stock market will change that probability.

4. Target's shareholders receive a different payoff

B cares about the value it keeps after paying for Target. Target's shareholders receive the payment. A change that hurts B can make Target's payment more sensitive to B's value.

For this example, suppose B has prepared. Its value is either 1 or 10. The seller's minimum price is 0.5.

First, let A's bid be 0.8

If B's value is 1, B wins and pays 0.8. If B's value is 10, B also wins and pays 0.8. Knowing whether B is high-value or low-value tells us nothing about the payment in this particular auction.

Now let A's bid be 3

If B's value is 10, B wins and pays 3. If B's value is 1, A wins and pays B's bid of 1. Target's shareholders receive 3 in the high-value case and 1 in the low-value case.

$$\text{Difference in Target's payment}=3-1=2.$$

This difference is what the paper calls the target-payoff spread. It compares payments across the two possible values of B. It is not the market's bid-ask spread.

A stronger A can reduce B's profit while making Target's payment depend more on whether B is a good fit. An investor trading Target shares cares about the second effect, because Target shares pay their owners Target's proceeds.

We held preparation fixed to understand the auction. In the full model, we must also account for the possibility that B never prepares. We will do that explicitly when we calculate the stock price.

Keep the payoffs separate

A bids 3, B's value is 10, and the reserve is 0.5. B has prepared. What happens?
Yes. B wins, pays A's bid of 3, and keeps 10 − 3.
The winner pays the competing bid when it exceeds the reserve. Here that payment is 3, so B keeps 7.

5. How can the stock market help B?

A separate investor trades Target shares. This investor cannot buy the whole company and does not bid in the takeover auction. Neither A nor B trades shares in the model.

In the benchmark, the investor knows whether B's value is 1 or 10 before B prepares. This is a strong information assumption. The investor's knowledge might represent research about Target's business that helps assess its fit with B. Lesson 5 studies a version where both the investor and B have incomplete information, and B's own information can be more accurate.

If knowledge about B changes the expected payout of Target shares enough to cover trading costs, the investor has a reason to trade. It wants to buy after good news and sell after bad news.

Other people trade for unrelated reasons. Their combined buying and selling is called noise demand. The dealers setting the share price see total demand, but cannot separate the informed investor's order from those other trades.

A buying-heavy market can therefore be evidence of good news, without being proof of good news. Dealers use the evidence to set the share price. B sees that price before deciding whether to prepare.

The price is not an extra preparation fee. It is evidence B can use. B pays the eventual auction price only if it prepares and wins.

The central sequence is:

  1. A's strength changes how much Target's eventual payment depends on B's value.
  2. That changes whether the investor finds informed trading worthwhile.
  3. Trading changes what B can learn from Target's share price.
  4. B uses that information when deciding whether to pay for preparation.

6. Follow one sale from start to finish

  1. The seller announces the auction and its minimum acceptable price.
  2. The investor learns B's value and chooses its share order.
  3. Dealers observe total orders and quote Target's share price.
  4. B observes that price and its own preparation cost. B decides whether to pay.
  5. If B pays, it learns its exact value and can bid. A is already prepared. The auction takes place.
  6. The winner acquires Target, shareholders receive the sale payment, and share-trading payoffs are settled.

B sees the share price, not the individual orders or total order flow. It also does not see A's exact value. Lesson 3 explains why the price can nevertheless reveal the dealers' assessment of B's value.

Check the timing

Just before deciding whether to prepare, what does B know in the benchmark?
Yes. Preparation later reveals B's exact acquisition value.
B sees the price and its cost. It must prepare to learn its exact value and become able to bid.

7. What the paper claims

The paper compares three levels of A's strength while keeping the other model inputs fixed.

With a weak A, the investor's advantage is too small to cover trading costs. There is no informed trading, so the price gives B no new information. B prepares only when its cost is low.

With a stronger A, informed trading becomes worthwhile. Favorable prices sometimes justify preparation even when B's cost is high. Total preparation becomes more likely, despite the smaller acquisition profit at any fixed belief.

With a still stronger A, prices remain informative. But even the best possible news no longer justifies the high preparation cost. Preparation becomes less likely again.

In the benchmark, the preparation probability moves from 25% to about 52.28%, then back to 25%. These are probabilities across possible realizations of one sale. They are not numbers of buyers attending one auction.

Proposition 2 proves this comparison under stated conditions. It does not say every increase in rival strength raises entry, or that entry follows a smooth curve through all intermediate strengths.

8. Where the real-world setting fits

Takeover discussions can involve initial contact, access to confidential information, investigation, and proposals that become more committed as work progresses. The paper uses these stages to motivate a decision to incur preparation costs before bidding.

Its introduction discusses the 2016 Imprivata sale narrative and research by Boone and Mulherin and by Gentry and Stroup. Treat those references as motivation for the preparation stage. They do not establish that buyers actually learn from share prices in the way modeled here.

For this mechanism to operate, the sale opportunity must be publicly understood while B can still enter. The mechanism requires market participants to understand that a sale is possible while B still has time to prepare. The empirical proposal in Lesson 5 starts by checking this timing.

9. Explain it without looking

What does preparation buy B?

It reveals B's acquisition value and permits an executable bid. A buyer that declines stays outside the sale.

Why can a stronger A hurt B but increase the investor's reason to trade?

A stronger A lowers B's acquisition profit. It can also widen the difference between Target's payments with a high-value and a low-value B. Information about that difference can be valuable when trading Target shares.

Why is seeing the price before preparation essential?

B must receive the information before committing the cost. Information that arrives after preparation cannot change that earlier decision.

What would happen if price information were equally informative at both strengths?

The information advantage would not improve as A strengthens. With the same beliefs and costs, lower acquisition profits can only discourage preparation. Lesson 4 proves this comparison.

What is still missing from our argument?

We have not yet calculated auction averages, proved what prices reveal, or shown that the investor actually prefers the proposed trades. Those are the next three lessons.
