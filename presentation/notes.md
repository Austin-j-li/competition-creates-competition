## F0 | opening, 0.25 min
Thank you for having me. The paper asks whether a stronger bidder in a takeover auction keeps a competitor out or brings one in. I will give the short answer in the first three slides and spend the rest on why it is true and what it needs.

## Q1 | slide 1, 1.5 min
Start from the question on the slide. A competitor has to pay to check the books before it can bid. A first bidder can open high to keep it out. In Bulow and Klemperer's model, one more bidder beats optimal negotiation in expected seller revenue. Each answer has a clear logic. The question is which one holds.

## Q2 | slide 2, 1.25 min
The standard answer is deterrence. The incumbent is ready to bid. The challenger has to prepare first, and preparation costs money. A stronger incumbent raises the price a winning challenger pays, so preparation pays less, and fewer challengers enter. I keep this answer. It holds at every belief the challenger can have.

## Q3 | slide 3, 1.0 min
My claim is that the stock price changes what the challenger believes. A stronger incumbent pushes a value gap to target shareholders, so sale proceeds depend more on the challenger class. An informed investor can then profit from trading on the class, and the price becomes informative. Good news covers a high preparation cost. At the benchmark, entry rises from $\rho=1/4$ to 0.522757. Keep price information fixed and the rise disappears. That is the control on slide 14.

## F1 | slide 4, 1.0 min
The setting is a public sale where one bidder is ready and a second bidder is not. The target's stock trades while the second bidder decides. That interval is the object of the paper. The challenger can watch the price while it decides.

## F2 | slide 5, 2.0 min
Walk the five steps with the buttons. The seller commits to a cash second-price auction with a reserve. One order reaches the market. Market makers see the order and set the price. The challenger sees the price and its own cost, and decides whether to prepare. Then the bidders bid. The highest bid at or above the reserve wins and pays the larger of the reserve and the rival bid. If no bid reaches the reserve, there is no sale. The incumbent value $R$ is uniform on $[0,r]$, and the strength $r$ is public. The challenger class is $H$ or $L$, and the preparation cost is independent of class. Take a moment on the information sets: the challenger never sees the order, the class or $R$.

## F3 | slide 6, 2.0 min
One auction produces two claims that move in opposite directions. When the incumbent value $R$ is below $\ell$, both classes win and the target receives the same proceeds. When $R$ is above $\ell$, only the high class wins. The gap in target proceeds is $R-\ell$. So a stronger incumbent raises the spread $\Delta_T$ between the proceeds with a high-class and a low-class entrant, and it lowers what the challenger keeps. At the benchmark the spread moves from 0.016667 to 0.666667 between the weak and the strong incumbent, while profit at the prior falls from 4.804167 to 4.291667.

## F4 | slide 7, 1.75 min
This is the trading stage. There is one public order slot. With probability $\lambda$ an informed investor fills it, sees the class, and picks sell, nothing or buy. A nonzero order costs $k$. Otherwise a liquidity trader fills it, and sells, abstains or buys with probabilities $\beta$, $1-2\beta$ and $\beta$. No one sees who filled the slot. So every order can come from either class, and no price reveals the class for sure. The number $1+u$ is the largest likelihood ratio an order can carry.

## F5 | slide 8, 1.75 min
Because every order can come from either class, the belief the challenger forms from the price is bounded: between $m(\pi)$ and $M(\pi)$, whatever the investor does. Use the buttons. A sale takes the belief to the bottom of the band, no order leaves it at the prior, a buy takes it to the top. The price is strictly increasing in the belief, because entry is at least $\rho$ and the spread is positive. So the challenger can read the belief off the price.

## F6 | slide 9, 1.0 min
These are the declared inputs. The units are arbitrary and the numbers are not estimates. They illustrate the results. The comparisons change only the incumbent strength, across the weak, strong and stronger-still values. If time is short, skip this slide and give the inputs when they appear.

## F7 | slide 10, 2.5 min
Here is why the investor trades. A correct order, a buy after $H$ or a sale after $L$, earns at most $\Delta_T$ before cost under every profile. It earns at least $\rho\kappa\Delta_T$ under every profile, where $\kappa$ is the smaller distance between the belief band and the ends of the unit interval. Read the figure on a log scale in units of $k$. The ceiling crosses the cost line first, so below that strength no order pays and no trade is the only outcome. The floor crosses the cost line at $r_S$, so above it every correct order pays and full orders are the only outcome. Between the two, the bounds do not settle the order regime, and the label says so. The weak incumbent sits on the left and the strong one on the right. The same shift that cuts the challenger's profit turns informed trade on.

## F8 | slide 11, 2.25 min
Now the challenger's side. The cheap type always prepares, so entry is at least $\rho$. The expensive type prepares only at beliefs at or above the threshold $\tau(r)$. The shaded band is the set of beliefs a price can produce. At the strong incumbent the threshold sits inside the band, so a buy order clears it. Drag the slider to larger $r$. The threshold rises and meets the top of the band at the ceiling $r_C$, which lies just below $r_2$. Past it, no order clears the threshold and the expensive type stays out.

## F9 | slide 12, 2.0 min
Proposition 2 puts the pieces together. At the weak incumbent there is no trade, the price is flat and entry equals $\rho$. At the strong incumbent there are full orders, the price is informative and entry is above $\rho$. At the stronger-still incumbent the orders stay full and the price stays informative, but the best belief no longer covers the high cost, and entry returns to $\rho$. Three strict inequalities carry it, so small changes in the inputs keep each part. Ownership by the high class rises as well, under the condition at the bottom.

## F10 | slide 13, 1.5 min
The benchmark numbers. Entry goes from $\rho=1/4$ to 0.522757 and back to $\rho$. Profit at the prior falls the whole way, from 4.804167 to 4.134722, while the spread rises. The orders switch on at the strong incumbent and stay on. The price carries the rise: it becomes informative when trade switches on.

## F11 | slide 14, 1.75 min
The control that isolates the channel. At fixed information, a stronger incumbent can only lower entry. Fixed information means the class-signal experiment and the cost law are held fixed, and in each economy both values keep their independent laws given class and signal. In the full model the challenger sees the price and trade is re-solved at each strength. Freeze the orders at the full-order profile and entry no longer rises. Hide the price and entry stays flat at $\rho$. In both controls the information in the price does not change with strength, so deterrence works at each belief, as in the standard answer. The rise comes from the change in what the price reveals.

## F12 | slide 15, 1.5 min
At intermediate strengths, the no-trade outcome and an informative outcome both exist. Panel (b) zooms in on the thresholds. No trade survives up to $r_N$, and the informative branch starts at $r_A$. On branch R the high class mixes its purchase, which raises entry. From $r_F$ the high class buys for certain and entry is flat until the ceiling. The claim is that no trade and an informative outcome both exist on the interval from $r_A$ to $r_N$. Beyond $r_N$, the full and mixed-order outcomes also coexist on a shorter range. At the comparison points of Proposition 2, the outcome is unique. If time is short, skip this slide.

## F13 | slide 16, 1.5 min
Let the investor choose how much to research. Research sets the presence probability $\lambda$ at a quadratic cost, up to a cap. A success reveals the class and fills the slot. Zero research stays an equilibrium on part of the range. On the research branch, presence rises with strength, and entry rises with it, from 0.500846 to 0.522757. At the strong incumbent, research at the cap is the unique outcome. So competition in the auction creates research as well as entry. If time is short, give this slide as one sentence.

## F14 | slide 17, 1.0 min
The reversal does not depend on the benchmark's cost or value structure. It holds for cost laws with or without atoms, under the stated support gaps, with a strict mean gap between the value classes and an interior prior, with other value classes and priors, and with a challenger who has its own signal. In the payment-rule institution, with zero reserve and verifiable values, the sign of the spread response follows one minus twice the seller's share. The slot with unit orders is what carries the result across the other variants. If time is short, skip this slide.

## F15 | slide 18, 1.0 min
Welfare. Letting the challenger see the price raises expected target proceeds and total surplus at the strong incumbent. The extra preparation happens only after a purchase, at beliefs that cover the cost. A reserve at the exclusion level leaves entry flat. Strict deterrence returns at a higher reserve, which also excludes the weak incumbent. If time is short, skip this slide.

## F16 | slide 19, 1.0 min
Four predictions. Entry can rise with incumbent strength when the price comes first, and does not rise when the price is hidden. Purchase impact exceeds sale impact exactly when a purchase recruits. Above $r_S$ full orders are the unique outcome, so, given $\rho$ and the spread, sale impact and volume identify the informed share and the slot parameters. And orders predict the winner even above the ceiling, where they no longer predict entry. A valid test uses the price before preparation starts.

## F17 | slide 20, 0.75 min
A stronger incumbent can bring the challenger in. Strength shifts a value gap to shareholders, trade pays in the slot, and a buy order makes high-cost preparation pay. Competition in the auction can create competition for entry, through the stock price before preparation. Thank you. I am happy to take questions.

## A3 | backup, equilibrium object
The equilibrium object has five parts: order laws by class, a price for each order, beliefs, the entry rule and the bids. Every order is on path, so no off-path belief enters. A tied investor may mix, and a tied high-cost challenger prepares. Proposition 2 holds under each pair of tie rules.

## A4 | backup, posterior bounds
A buy in class $H$ comes from the investor or from a liquidity buy. In class $L$ a liquidity buy alone gives chance at least $b$. So the likelihood ratio stays between one over $1+u$ and $1+u$ for every investor strategy, and Bayes rule turns that into the band on the belief. Full orders attain both ends. The uniqueness proofs use these bounds on every profile, not only on the candidate.

## A5 | backup, admissible region
For each object the table gives the set of values that satisfy the strict conditions of the results, and the benchmark pick inside it. Small moves inside a set keep each result.

## A6 | backup, proof structure
Five steps. First, the bounds for every profile. Second, at the weak incumbent every nonzero order loses in expectation, so no trade is the only outcome. Third, at the strong incumbent every correct order pays under every profile and a wrong-signed order always loses, so the orders are full. Fourth, a buy gives belief $M$, which covers the high cost. Fifth, at the stronger-still incumbent trade still pays but $M$ no longer covers the high cost. The last line gives the ownership condition.

## A7 | backup, entry formulas
Under full orders class $H$ faces a buy with chance $\lambda+b$ and class $L$ with chance $b$. Entry for each class is $\rho$ plus the chance of a buy times one minus $\rho$. Averaging over the prior gives the entry in the table on slide 13.

## A8 | backup, thresholds
The six thresholds and their roles. Before $r_A$ no informative branch exists. On the interval from $r_A$ to $r_F$ the mix rate adjusts and entry rises. From $r_F$ to $r_N$ a full purchase coexists with no trade. Past $r_N$, no trade fails. Full orders are unique above $r_S$. The ceiling $r_C$ is where the top belief stops short of the high cost.

## A9 | backup, research in detail
Research picks $\lambda$ between zero and the cap before the slot. At the weak incumbent the unique outcome has no research. The research branch is an equilibrium on an interval, and at three strengths in it there are exactly two equilibria, no research and the branch. At the strong and stronger-still incumbents, research at the cap is unique. The cost form and the cap are set-up choices.

## A10 | backup, extensions
Six variants, each with its own statement. General values with a strict mean gap and interior priors, many classes with a separation condition, cost laws with or without atoms, private signals, price access, and the payment rule. For price access, shareholders gain relative to no informed investor if they bear the whole liquidity loss and the signal experiment stays fixed.

## A11 | backup, order sizes
The slot uses unit orders so that every price is on path. A menu of continuous sizes needs a convention for sizes no class uses. With Laplace noise and full unit orders, the top posterior equals the slot value when $1+u=e^{2/s}$. The two set-ups then share the posterior bounds and differ in how often each class reaches the top.

## A12 | backup, sale terms
Price access, the reserve and the payment rule in more detail. The payment rule has its own institution: zero reserve, verifiable values, an enforceable fallback, and challenger values $\ell<\bar r<h$ around the top of the incumbent support $[0,\bar r]$. With price access the proceeds gain and the surplus gain are both positive at the strong incumbent. At the exclusion reserve entry is flat. Strict deterrence needs a higher reserve that also excludes the weak incumbent.
