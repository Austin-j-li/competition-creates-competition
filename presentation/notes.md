## F0 | opening, 0.25 min
Thank you for having me. The paper asks whether a stronger bidder in a takeover auction keeps a competitor out or brings one in. I will give the short answer in the first three slides and spend the rest on why it is true and what it needs.

## Q1 | slide 1, 1.5 min
Start from the question on the slide. A competitor has to pay to check the books before it can bid. A first bidder can open high to keep it out. In Bulow and Klemperer's model, one more bidder beats optimal negotiation in expected seller revenue. The question is which one holds.

## Q2 | slide 2, 1.25 min
The standard answer is deterrence. The incumbent is ready to bid. The challenger has to prepare first, and preparation costs money. A stronger incumbent raises the price a winning challenger pays, so preparation pays less, and fewer challengers prepare. I keep this answer. It holds at every belief the challenger can have.

## Q3 | slide 3, 1.0 min
My claim is that the stock price changes what the challenger believes. A stronger incumbent pushes a value gap to target shareholders, so sale proceeds depend more on the challenger class. An informed investor can then profit from trading on the class, and the price becomes informative. Good news covers a high preparation cost, so a stronger incumbent can draw the challenger in. Keep price information fixed and that effect disappears. That is the control on slide 14.

## F1 | slide 4, 1.0 min
The setting is a public sale where one bidder is ready and a second bidder is not. The target's stock trades while the second bidder decides. That interval is the object of the paper. The challenger can watch the price while it decides.

## F2 | slide 5, 2.0 min
Walk the five steps with the buttons. The seller commits to a cash second-price auction with a reserve. One order reaches the market. Market makers see the order and set the price. The challenger sees the price and its own cost, and decides whether to prepare. Then the bidders bid. The highest bid at or above the reserve wins and pays the larger of the reserve and the rival bid. If no bid reaches the reserve, there is no sale. The incumbent value $R$ is uniform on $[0,r]$, and the strength $r$ is public. The challenger class is $H$ or $L$, and the preparation cost is independent of class. Take a moment on the information sets: the challenger never sees the order, the class or $R$.

## F3 | slide 6, 2.0 min
One auction produces two claims that move in opposite directions. When the incumbent value $R$ is below $\ell$, both classes win and the target receives the same proceeds. When $R$ is above $\ell$, only the high class wins. The gap in target proceeds is $R-\ell$. So a stronger incumbent raises the spread $\Delta_T$ between the proceeds with a high-class and a low-class challenger, and it lowers what the challenger keeps. In the example the spread moves from 0.016667 to 0.666667 between the weak and the strong incumbent, while profit at the prior falls from 4.804167 to 4.291667.

## F4 | slide 7, 1.75 min
There is one public order slot. With probability $\lambda$ an informed investor fills it, sees the class, and picks sell, nothing or buy. A nonzero order costs $k$. Otherwise a liquidity trader fills it, and sells, abstains or buys with probabilities $\beta$, $1-2\beta$ and $\beta$. No one sees who filled the slot. So every order can come from either class, and no price reveals the class for sure. The number $1+u$ is the largest likelihood ratio an order can carry.

## F5 | slide 8, 1.75 min
Because every order can come from either class, the belief the challenger forms from the price is bounded: between $m(\pi)$ and $M(\pi)$, whatever the investor does. Use the buttons. A sale takes the belief to the bottom of the band, no order leaves it at the prior, a buy takes it to the top. The price is strictly increasing in the belief, because entry is at least $\rho$ and the spread is positive. So the challenger can read the belief off the price.

## F6 | slide 9, 1.0 min
These are the declared inputs. The units are arbitrary and the numbers are not estimates. The comparisons change only the incumbent strength, across the weak, strong and stronger-still values. If time is short, skip this slide and give the inputs when they appear.

## F7 | slide 10, 2.5 min
A correct order, a buy after $H$ or a sale after $L$, earns at most $\Delta_T$ before cost under every profile. It earns at least $\rho\kappa\Delta_T$ under every profile, where $\kappa$ is the smaller distance between the belief band and the ends of the unit interval. Read the figure on a log scale in units of $k$. The ceiling crosses the cost line first, so below that strength no order pays and a quiet market is the only outcome. The floor crosses the cost line at $r_S$, so above it every correct order pays and full orders are the only outcome. Between the two, the bounds do not settle the order regime, and the label says so. The weak incumbent sits on the left and the strong one on the right. The same rise in $r$ that lowers challenger profit makes a correct order cover $k$.

## F8 | slide 11, 2.25 min
The cheap type always prepares, so entry is at least $\rho$. The expensive type prepares only at beliefs at or above the threshold $\tau(r)$. The shaded band is the set of beliefs a price can produce. At the strong incumbent the threshold sits inside the band, so a buy order clears it. Drag the slider to larger $r$. The threshold rises and meets the top of the band at the ceiling $r_C$, which lies just below $r_2$. Past it, no order clears the threshold and the expensive type stays out.

## F9 | slide 12, 2.0 min
Proposition 2 compares the three strengths. At the weak incumbent the market is quiet, the price is flat and entry equals $\rho$. At the strong incumbent there are full orders, the price is informative and entry is above $\rho$. At the stronger-still incumbent the orders stay full and the price stays informative, but the best belief no longer covers the high cost, and entry returns to $\rho$. Its conditions are the high-cost window, the trading-cost window and the exclusion condition. Each is a strict inequality, so the parts hold on an open set of inputs, under all four tie rules. Ownership by the high class rises as well, under the condition at the bottom; when the high class outbids the strong incumbent, it rises and its takeover share does not fall.

## F10 | slide 13, 1.5 min
Corollary 1 gives the numbers for the example. Entry goes from $\rho=1/4$ to 0.522757 and back to $\rho$. Profit at the prior falls the whole way, from 4.804167 to 4.134722, while the spread rises. The orders switch on at the strong incumbent and stay on. The price becomes informative at the strong incumbent, where the orders start. The three conditions hold together exactly when the mass ratio lies in the band at the bottom, that is, when the top posterior lies between the thresholds at the strong and the stronger-still incumbent.

## F11 | slide 14, 1.75 min
At fixed information, a stronger incumbent can only lower entry. Fixed information means that, given the class and the signal, the two values keep their class and incumbent laws, and cost is independent of class, values and signal. In the full model the challenger sees the price and trade is re-solved at each strength. Freeze the orders at the full-order profile and entry is constant up to the entry ceiling. Hide the price and entry stays flat at $\rho$. In both controls the information in the price does not change with strength, so deterrence works at each belief, as in the standard answer. The rise comes from the change in what the price reveals.

## F12 | slide 15, 0.75 min
Between the weak and the strong incumbent, a quiet market and an informative market can both be equilibria. Proposition 3 says exactly where, in terms of two numbers: the spread and the preparation threshold. Both exist when the threshold lies above the prior and at most at the top posterior, and the spread lies between two bounds. At the lower bound, a purchase that just reaches the threshold covers the order cost. At the upper bound, an order against a flat price earns at most that cost. In that region the informative market is unique: the low class abstains and only a purchase recruits. The figure shows the region and the path of the example through it. ## F12b | slide 16, 0.75 min
Corollary 2 follows the example along the path of strengths. The quiet market survives up to $r_N$, and the informative market starts at $r_A$, so the two coexist between them. On R the high class mixes its purchase and entry rises with strength. From $r_F$ the high class buys for sure and entry stays at its plateau up to the entry ceiling, then returns to $\rho$. Past $r_N$, mixed orders at the entry floor join the full purchase for a while. The thin dashed line is the threshold pair, which exists only when an indifferent high-cost challenger may prepare with any probability. If time is short, skip this slide.

## F13 | slide 17, 1.5 min
Let the investor choose how much to research. Research sets the presence probability $\lambda$ at a quadratic cost, up to a cap. A success reveals the class and fills the slot. When a purchase recruits, a wider spread raises the return to a purchase, so research rises, and entry rises with research. The figure draws Corollary 3. Zero research is an equilibrium up to $r_N$. Recruiting research starts at $r_R$, reaches the cap at $r_{\bar\lambda}$ and stays there up to the entry ceiling $r_C$. Floor-entry research, in which both classes trade after a success and no order recruits, coexists with it just above $r_N$ and is the only equilibrium above $r_C$. At the strong incumbent the unique outcome is recruiting research at the cap. So competition in the auction creates research as well as entry. If time is short, give this slide as one sentence.

## F14 | slide 18, 1.0 min
The reversal does not depend on the example's cost or value structure. It holds for cost laws with or without atoms, for every ranked pair of value classes and every prior at suitable costs, and with complementary signals. Many classes need a separation condition. Under a payment rule that gives the seller a share of the gap, the sign of the spread response follows one minus twice the seller's share. Each variant retains the slot with unit orders. If time is short, skip this slide.

## F15 | slide 19, 1.0 min
Letting the challenger see the price raises expected target proceeds and total surplus at the strong incumbent. The extra preparation happens only after a purchase, at beliefs that cover the cost. A reserve that excludes the low class leaves entry flat. Strict deterrence returns at a higher reserve, which also excludes the weak incumbent. If time is short, skip this slide.

## F16 | slide 20, 1.0 min
Entry can rise with incumbent strength when the price comes first; with the price hidden, a stronger incumbent can only lower it. Under full two-sided trade at a prior below the preparation threshold, the weighted purchase impact exceeds the weighted sale impact exactly when a purchase recruits. Full two-sided trade is the unique outcome between $r_S$ and $h$, and then the sale impact and the mean order identify presence and prior. When the high class outbids the low class, orders predict the winner even above the entry ceiling, where they no longer predict entry. A valid test uses the price before preparation starts.

## F17 | slide 21, 0.75 min
A stronger incumbent can bring the challenger in. Strength shifts a value gap to shareholders, trade pays in the slot, and a buy order makes high-cost preparation pay. Competition in the auction can create competition for entry, through the stock price before preparation. Thank you. I am happy to take questions.

## A3 | backup, equilibrium object
The equilibrium object has five parts: order laws by class, a price for each order, beliefs, the entry rule and the bids. Every order is on path, so no off-path belief enters. A tied investor may mix, and a tied high-cost challenger prepares. Proposition 2 holds under all four tie rules.

## A4 | backup, posterior bounds
A buy in class $H$ comes from the investor or from a liquidity buy. In class $L$ a liquidity buy alone gives chance at least $b$. So the likelihood ratio stays between one over $1+u$ and $1+u$ for every investor strategy, and Bayes rule turns that into the band on the belief. Full orders attain both ends. The uniqueness proofs apply these bounds to every profile.

## A5 | backup, recruitment and research regions
For each object the table gives the region cut out by the strict conditions of the results, and the value the example takes inside it. Because each condition is strict, the results hold on an open set of inputs.

## A6 | backup, proof structure
Five steps. First, the bounds for every profile. Second, at the weak incumbent every nonzero order loses in expectation, so a quiet market is the only outcome. Third, at the strong incumbent every correct order pays under every profile and a wrong-signed order always loses, so the orders are full. Fourth, a buy gives belief $M$, which covers the high cost. Fifth, at the stronger-still incumbent trade still pays but $M$ no longer covers the high cost. The last line gives the ownership condition.

## A7 | backup, entry formulas
Under full orders class $H$ faces a buy with chance $\lambda+b$ and class $L$ with chance $b$. Entry for each class is $\rho$ plus the chance of a buy times one minus $\rho$. Averaging over the prior gives the entry in the table on slide 13.

## A8 | backup, marked strengths
The six marked strengths and their roles. Before $r_A$ no informative market exists. On the interval from $r_A$ to $r_F$ the mix rate adjusts and entry rises. From $r_F$ to $r_N$ a full purchase coexists with a quiet market. Past $r_N$, the quiet market fails, and mixed orders at the entry floor join the full purchase until $r_J$. The low class sells for sure above $r_S$. The entry ceiling $r_C$ is where the top posterior stops short of the high cost.

## A9 | backup, research in detail
Research picks $\lambda$ between zero and the cap before the slot. Zero research is an equilibrium up to $r_N$. Recruiting research is an equilibrium from $r_R$ to the entry ceiling: below the cap up to $r_{\bar\lambda}$, at the cap after it, with entry rising and then constant. Floor-entry research, with entry at $\rho$, is an equilibrium just above $r_N$ up to $r_Q$ and above the entry ceiling. There is no other equilibrium. At the three intermediate strengths there are exactly two equilibria, zero research and recruiting research below the cap. The unique outcome has zero research at the weak incumbent, recruiting research at the cap at the strong one, and floor-entry research at the cap at the stronger-still one.

## A10 | backup, extensions
The six variants are general values and priors, many classes with a separation condition, cost laws with or without atoms, private signals, price access, and the payment rule. For price access, shareholders gain in total at every trade scale below the level on the slide.

## A11 | backup, order sizes
The slot uses unit orders so that every price is on path. A menu of continuous sizes needs a convention for sizes no class uses. With Laplace noise and full unit orders, the top posterior equals the slot value when $1+u=e^{2/\xi}$. The two set-ups then share the posterior bounds and differ in how often each class reaches the top.

## A12 | backup, sale terms
With price access the proceeds gain and the surplus gain are both positive at the strong incumbent. At a reserve that excludes the low class, entry is flat. The payment rule's spread response has the sign of one minus twice the seller's share. Strict deterrence needs a higher reserve that also excludes the weak incumbent.
