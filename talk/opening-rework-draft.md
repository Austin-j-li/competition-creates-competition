## New F2 — 2 minutes

TITLE: Does a stronger incumbent keep the challenger out?
SUBTITLE: The standard answer, and what it takes as given

BODY:
- The standard answer is yes.
- A stronger rival pushes up the price the winner must pay.
- So the challenger earns less if it gets ready. Fewer challengers bother. [cite: Fishman 1988; Hirshleifer and Png 1989]
- That answer takes one thing as given: what the challenger knows stays the same.
- Here it does not. The challenger watches the share price first, then decides.

TAKEAWAY:
My claim: the share price can flip the answer. A stronger incumbent can pull the challenger in.

## New F4 — 2 minutes

TITLE: This paper

LEAD:
One well-informed investor trades the target's shares before the challenger decides. The shares pay out from the final sale price, so the share price reflects what the market expects the sale to pay. I solve for trade and entry together, in equilibrium.

BODY:
- Competition creates competition. A stronger incumbent can turn a dull share price into a useful signal, and entry rises. In the benchmark case, entry rises from 0.250 to 0.523. At the same time, the challenger's profit before any news falls from 4.80 to 4.29.
- Why: a strong incumbent beats a weak challenger, but not a strong one. So the challenger keeps less, yet the sale price says more about which challenger showed up. The investor trades on that. The share price moves. Good news then makes costly preparation worth it.
- The channel is information. If the price tells the challenger no more than before, a stronger incumbent cannot raise entry. [status: sign analytical, Prop. A.3]

TAKEAWAY:
So a stronger rival does not always scare off competition. When shares trade, who joins the sale depends on what the price says first.
