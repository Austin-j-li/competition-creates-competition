# M&A terminology calibration

Date: 29 October 2026. Scope: discussion only. No paper or deck edits.

## Recommendation

Use **incumbent bidder** and **potential entrant** at the preparation stage. Define both terms at first use.

Suggested definitions: “The incumbent bidder has already completed preparation and knows its acquisition value. The potential entrant observes the target stock price, then decides whether to pay the preparation cost. Payment reveals its acquisition value and permits a bid.”

After entry, call the second party the **rival bidder**. If a fixed pair is essential throughout, use **incumbent bidder / challenger**, with the same explicit definitions. The evidence below supports incumbent and entrant vocabulary. It does not establish incumbent/challenger as a standard paired expression.

This recommendation reflects model fit. It is not a claim about the most frequent terms across the discipline.

## Model fit

I read CLAUDE.md, main.tex, slides.tex, and the model section under overleaf/slot. The model section, “The decision interval,” permits a disclosed approach, strategic review, or open contest. Its prepared party need not submit an initial offer. It explicitly states that public incumbent strength is a distribution, not a bid.

The section “Values, preparation, and the sale” gives the incumbent a sunk preparation cost. The other buyer must pay before it can learn its value and bid. The slides reserve first buyer / second buyer as locked words in their source comment. This note proposes discussion terms only.

“Initial bidder” can suggest the party that initiates the contest or submits its first offer. Neither action defines the prepared party here. “First bidder” has the same risk in the classic preemption models. These words do not logically require an offer in every possible model. Their actual use in the closest classic sources creates that expectation.

“Second bidder” can also suggest actual participation. A potential entrant may stay out. “Potential entrant” states that distinction directly. Entry here means entry into this sale process, not entry into a product market.

“Incumbent bidder” requires a definition. It means the buyer already prepared for this contest. It does not mean the target owner, target management, or an established product-market firm.

## Primary evidence

### Hirshleifer and Png (1989)

*Facilitation of Competing Bids and the Price of a Takeover Target*, Review of Financial Studies 2(4), 587–606. Location verified: abstract on the author's website. The publisher record confirms the corrected page range: https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article-abstract/2/4/587/1586915.

Exact terms: “The first bidder makes either a preemptive bid”; “the second bidder”.

The abstract links the second party's investigation decision to the first party's offer. This is close on costly value discovery. It differs on the signal that precedes entry. Here the target stock price supplies that signal.

Source: https://sites.uci.edu/dhirshle/abstracts/facilitation-of-competing-bids-and-the-price-of-a-takeover-target/

Access limit: the search tool returned the author-hosted abstract; a direct fetch later returned 502. I did not verify body-page terminology.

### Bulow and Klemperer (2009)

*Why Do Sellers (Usually) Prefer Auctions?*, American Economic Review 99, 1544–1575.

Publisher abstract: “potential buyers decide in turn whether to enter the bidding”. This supports potential-buyer vocabulary before costly participation.

Publisher source: https://doi.org/10.1257/AER.99.4.1544

The author-hosted June 2009 text also uses “the incumbent’s signal” and “entrant”. Location: discussion of common values, immediately before footnote 33. Its Appendix E, “Buyers’ Bargaining Power,” uses “the first bidder” for a buyer that makes an offer.

Author text: https://www.nuffield.ox.ac.uk/economics/papers/2009/w5/wdaforweb29jun09.pdf

Access limit: indexed author-text excerpts were accessible. A direct PDF open failed. These excerpts support usage, not a complete account of the paper's terminology.

### Gentry and Stroup (2019)

*Entry and Competition in Takeover Auctions*, Journal of Financial Economics 132, 298–324.

The authors' account uses “potential buyers” and “potential bidders”. Exact phrase: “more than half of potential bidders decline”. Location: paragraph on uncertainty before due diligence, after the discussion of go-shops.

This is strong support for potential-bidder language before costly participation. It does not establish initial bidder / rival bidder as their formal pair.

Primary author account: https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2019/05/07/entry-competition-in-takeover-auctions/

Article DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2018.10.007

Access limit: I verified the authors' account, not the article's model pages.

### Fishman (1988): important unresolved source

*A Theory of Preemptive Takeover Bidding*, RAND Journal of Economics 19, 88–101.

Publisher record: https://www.jstor.org/stable/2555399

The publisher page returned no readable article text. Bibliographic search results describe costly information after the auction starts and preemptive initial offers. I do not treat those intermediary results as verified primary-text quotations. Thus Fishman remains a priority for a body-text check. ## Comparison and limits

| Candidate | Assessment for this model |
| --- | --- |
| incumbent / challenger | Precise after definition; challenger as an established paired term remains unverified here. |
| initial bidder / rival bidder | Natural for an initial-offer contest; suggests an action the model does not require. |
| incumbent bidder / potential entrant | Best fit before preparation; entrant vocabulary has direct auction precedent. |
| first / second bidder | Direct classic precedent; can imply offer order and actual participation. |
| first / second buyer | Neutral role numbers; does not state the economic asymmetry. |

Three primary works supplied accessible terminology evidence. Fishman's primary body text remained inaccessible. The requested four-source threshold therefore remains unmet. No universal terminology standard follows from this small source set.

The main uncertainty concerns the fixed word “challenger,” not the costly-entry interpretation. A later source check should verify Fishman's body text and Gentry–Stroup's formal model before any claim about their exact paired labels.
