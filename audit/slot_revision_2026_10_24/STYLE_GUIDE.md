# Register and style guide for "Competition Creates Competition"

## 1. Decision

Use the Laplace draft as the base register for the manuscript. Keep the slot draft's discipline where it serves precision, and drop the controlled-language rules as sentence laws.

A theory argument often runs as one conditional chain: an actor faces a payoff, a price changes that payoff, and entry follows. The slot draft often breaks such chains at full stops, so a condition reads as a free-standing rule and its consequence arrives detached. The Laplace draft joins the clauses; the reader sees one condition with its reason attached.

A finance theory reader follows named actors and payoff comparisons. Four propositions mark the formal claims, and prose around them carries the economics. A reader of the abstract, the introduction, the proposition titles, and the intuition paragraphs should learn the mechanism and every headline result. Support statements belong in Appendix A.

Leave the Laplace draft's faults behind: typed decimals, one very long catch-all introduction paragraph, and defensive passages of its own. The slot draft's gains stay: numbers through `\reg{}`, one topic per tour paragraph, and a complete equilibrium set on the benchmark path.

Stay: one word and one symbol for one object; simple words; articles in place; an active verb with its actor for each decision; one argument per paragraph; "I"; every number through `\reg{}`.

Go as hard limits: the 20/25-word sentence caps and the six-sentence paragraph cap; one instruction per sentence; the ban on subordinate clauses, participles, semicolons, and the perfect tense; the noun-cluster count as a mechanical test. A long noun cluster still warns the writer to find a verb.

Judge each sentence by its logic. A sentence earns its length when each added clause carries a condition, a comparison, or a consequence that the main clause needs. It fails when a second actor, a second claim, or a scope condition hides inside it.

## 2. Style guide for the rewrite

### 2.1 Sentences and paragraphs

- Build a sentence around one move: a claim, a condition with its consequence, a comparison, or a definition of a new object through its economic role.
- Join a condition to its consequence in one sentence when a full stop would lose the link. Use "when", "if", "since", "so", "while", and "once" to name the link.
- Use a semicolon for two parallel claims under one subject, or for a claim and a close consequence with the same weight. Use a comma for a subordinate part. Use a colon to introduce a display or a precise restatement. Do not use an em dash as a connector.
- Expect most sentences to fall between 12 and 30 words. A sentence of 35 to 45 words is acceptable when it states a hypothesis in words or runs one timing sequence. Such a sentence needs one clear main verb and pronouns with close antecedents. Read it once, slowly; if the reader must restart, split it at the logical joint, and keep a connective word at the joint.
- Vary length on purpose. A short sentence carries a turn or a new consequence after a long sentence. It must add a fact. A short sentence that only repeats the paragraph goes out.
- Allow a participle at the end of a sentence when its subject is the sentence subject and it states a real consequence. The Laplace conclusion has a good case: a stronger incumbent increases sensitivity, making trade profitable and bringing a challenger in. Ban ornamental participles that add only emphasis, such as "highlighting" or "reflecting" at the end of a plain fact.
- Prefer the active voice for decisions: the investor buys, market makers set the price, the challenger prepares, the incumbent pays. Use the passive voice where the object matters and the actor is fixed by the model, as in "the cost is paid before the bid".
- Keep table and figure claims in the same register. State the economic pattern first, then the numbers that show it.
- Give each paragraph one argument, from claim to consequence. A mechanism paragraph in the introduction or after a proposition may run to eight or nine sentences when the chain needs them. A definitions paragraph stays short. A paragraph ends when the actor, the comparison, or the question changes.
- Open a paragraph with an actor, an object, or a claim. Do not open with a count. Openers of the form "Two functions organize...", "Two comparisons recur.", and "Two numbers summarize..." label the text instead of advancing it. State the first object in its own sentence, and let the second follow.
- End a paragraph on its last new fact or on the question the next paragraph answers. Do not add a one-line restatement for emphasis.
- Use the present tense for the model, for results, and for cited results that the argument uses. Use the perfect tense only where sequence in time matters, as with a cost already paid before a later decision.

### 2.2 The abstract and the introduction

- Keep the abstract in two parts: mechanism first, results second. State the control and the coexistence result in positive form: with price information fixed, a stronger incumbent can only lower entry; along the informative branch, entry rises and then stays constant. Keep any qualifier without which a sentence would be false, such as the recruitment region for the price-access gain. Keep symbols and decimals out of the abstract.
- Open the introduction with deterrence at fixed information and its citations, then the opposite force, then the result. The first paragraph of the slot draft already does this work and needs only light joins. The demonstration in Section 3 shows the treatment.
- Follow with the setting in one short paragraph, then the mechanism. The mechanism needs up to two paragraphs: the division of the class value gap between acquisition profit and target proceeds, then trade on the sensitivity of proceeds. The sentence on shareholder gains belongs after the text explains recruitment, because a holder through the sale receives proceeds at the sale.
- Introduce the order slot in words. Name the alternative-arrival structure and its source. Say that a liquidity order can match the investor's direction, so every order leaves the investor a residual advantage. Say it once. The introduction carries no order menu, no likelihood ratio, and no claim about what uniqueness covers.
- State the main result in one paragraph as a comparison of economies and outcomes: weak incumbent, strong incumbent, very strong incumbent. Words carry the entry and ownership conclusions. Rational pricing enters as one clause in that paragraph or the control paragraph, with market makers as its subject.
- Give the benchmark numbers paragraph the numbers of the tour. Name each object in words before its value, and let `\reg{}` print the value. A threshold symbol may enter here after its verbal name, since the paragraph reports a quantitative result. No formula in the introduction carries an argument that words have left unstated.
- Tour the remaining results in abstract order, one paragraph each: coexistence, research, robustness, welfare and sale terms, and empirical implications. Each paragraph states its result first and attaches one section reference to the claim. Do not repeat a scope condition in the tour when its home section states it.
- State coexistence and the research branch as results, with the same positive forms as the abstract. A branch "coexists with" another equilibrium; it does not sit "beside" one as a caveat.
- Close with one short roadmap paragraph. It names sections and the appendix once.
- Give the literature its own paragraphs after the tour, under the rules in Section 2.8 below.

### 2.3 The model section: setting first, notation inside sentences

- Start with the decision interval as an economic situation: a public sale opportunity, trading still open, a further buyer still able to enter. The timing condition belongs in the same sentence as the situation it defines. The empirical section pointer follows as a plain forward reference.
- Then say who is prepared, what preparation requires, what it costs, and what each side knows. Keep one sentence for the list of diligence tasks; a list inside a sentence reads as economics, where a vertical list reads as a procedure.
- Treat public incumbent strength as a distribution, with the disclosure examples in the same paragraph. A citation that supports a modeling choice stands in the sentence that makes the choice.
- Bring in disclosure evidence as part of the argument and point it forward to the empirical section. Two sentences suffice in the model section; the records own no paragraph of their own.
- Introduce each symbol inside a sentence that first gives its meaning in words. The pattern to keep: at a belief, the challenger's expected gross profit takes its formula, because its value depends on the price only through its class. No paragraph opens with "Write...". The word "write" serves only mid-sentence, after the object has a verbal name, or inside a proposition statement.
- Define a symbol once, at its first use, and keep the same symbol for the same object in every section and in the appendix. Say "the benchmark" in running text; the letter B already names gross profit. Keep the top posterior as a function of the prior, or of research intensity in the research section; do not redefine a bare letter for a special case mid-paper.
- If the main text uses an object fewer than three times, prefer words or one inline formula in the main text and keep the symbol for the appendix. Treat the count as a warning, not a law; a symbol that makes an "if and only if" condition readable stays, even at two uses.
- Keep displays for equations the reader reuses: the class ranking, the investor's profit, the posterior bounds, the price equation, the payoff and spread formulas, and the research optimality condition. State every other definition inline. A display that only names an object leaves the main text.
- State the class ranking in prose with one numbered display. Two sentences say that the high class dominates and that both supports lie above the reserve. Later text refers to "the class ranking" or to the display. The ranking gets one home, in the values subsection.
- Describe the trading stage and the timing as chronological prose. "The sequence is as follows" may open the timing sentence group, as in the Laplace draft. Numbered steps, and references to step numbers, leave the main text; the research section then says that research comes before the investor learns the class.
- Put the equilibrium concept in words before the payoff notation of its subsection. The reader meets the object first and its components second. Section 2.6 below gives the paragraph form.
- State normalizations and scope facts next to the objects they concern, in plain sentences. A normalization sentence says what changes and what stays fixed, once.

### 2.4 A numbered proposition

- Keep four numbered propositions, one for each headline claim of the abstract: competition and the two returns to information; competition creates competition; coexistence on the benchmark path; competition creates research. Keep them as proposition environments with a descriptive title and the status in the title. Proposition 4 names both parts: part (a) analytical and part (b) computer-assisted.
- Before each proposition, write a lead-in of two to four sentences in plain words. It states the comparison, the outcome in each case, and the economic point at stake. Notation that the statement needs enters at the end of the lead-in, in one sentence, directly before the statement.
- Open the statement with one "Let ... and suppose that" frame. State the hypotheses in words in that frame, then give the tagged displays. Tags stay where later text and the appendix refer to them, as with (H1) to (H3). A tag without later use goes.
- State each part as outcomes in words: who trades, what the price reveals, who prepares, and what entry and ownership do. Keep a formula where words cannot carry the content: an entry level, an "if and only if" ownership condition, an exact price. Where words say the same thing, words win; the statement needs no order-distribution notation for "does not trade" or "buys and sells".
- Define at most one piece of shorthand inside a statement, and only when the statement reuses it. Entry given the high class earns a symbol because the ownership condition needs it twice. A shorthand used once becomes its formula in place.
- Keep the short form of a computer-assisted proposition in the main text and the full form in the appendix, under a heading that names it as the full statement. The boundary strengths and the complete set belong to the full form. Thresholds that the main-text intuition never uses stay with the full form or enter where a later section uses them.
- After each proposition, put the intuition first. One paragraph reads the hypotheses as decisions, under Section 2.5. A second paragraph covers scope that carries economics: when the window is nonempty, in words; when ownership needs its own condition, in words; what happens at the next strength. Proof pointers and tie or status bookkeeping close the discussion in at most two plain sentences. No bold sentence stands in running text; bold belongs to proposition heads and table panel heads.
- Apply one reading check to each statement. A reader who skips the displays should still be able to say which economies the proposition compares and what happens in each.

### 2.5 Hypotheses read as economics

- Give each hypothesis an economic name beside its tag at first use: the high-cost window, the trading-cost window, and the ceiling. Use the name in running text and the tag where precision needs it, in tables, and in the appendix.
- For each hypothesis, one or two sentences answer four questions. Which actor's decision does it pin down? Which payoff does it compare? At which belief and against which incumbent? What changes when the inequality turns?
- Read the high-cost window through acquisition profit. Gross profit at the prior falls short of the high cost against the weak incumbent, so an uninformative price recruits no high-cost challenger; gross profit at the top posterior exceeds the high cost against the strong incumbent, so a purchase can recruit one.
- Read the trading-cost window through the return to a correct order under every candidate profile. In the weak economy that return cannot cover the trading cost; in the strong economy a fixed share of the wider spread covers it with room to spare. State the bounds once in formulas, after the words.
- Read the ceiling through the top posterior. Against the very strong incumbent, gross profit at the most favorable feasible belief falls short of the high cost, so trade stays informative and recruitment stops. The ceiling is a result about entry; state it as one.
- State a sufficient condition in words before its formula. The ownership condition holds when the stronger incumbent leaves the high class a good chance to win and the high class places a purchase more often than not; the formula then gives the exact comparison. A condition never appears as a bare formula with a reference to a part number.
- State when a window is nonempty as an economic comparison, then as a ratio. Competition must widen the target-payoff spread enough between the two incumbent distributions. A prior away from one half narrows the window because the price already leans toward the favored class.
- Keep words such as "weakly" inside formal statements, where a weak inequality is the mathematical content. In headlines and intuition paragraphs, state the exact path where the paper knows it: entry rises, then stays constant; a stronger incumbent can only lower entry at fixed information.

### 2.6 Prose in place of lemmas and the equilibrium definition

- State the equilibrium in one paragraph of prose, in the manner of the Laplace draft. An equilibrium specifies, for each class, a distribution over the three orders; the market makers' posterior and price after each order; the challenger's belief at each price; its preparation choice at each price and cost; and truthful bids. One sentence adds that the investor may mix and may place a wrong-signed order.
- Keep in the same paragraph group the facts a reader uses later: every order has positive probability in both classes because a liquidity trader can place it, so Bayes' rule gives a posterior after every order; prices are competitive; entry after an order takes its value from the price; and the investor's payoff stands in one display. The formal tuple and its six conditions open Appendix A, before the first proof subsection. The main text points to them once.
- Keep the distinction between the two comparisons in the model section, in two sentences. A unilateral deviation by the investor faces the candidate prices and entry rule. A comparison across incumbent strengths solves for the equilibrium again in each economy. The fixed-information control and the comparative statics both need the distinction. A sentence about which comparison a proof uses belongs with the proof.
- State the two default tie conventions once, in one sentence in the equilibrium subsection: an indifferent investor may mix over its best orders, and an indifferent high-cost challenger prepares. The appendix section on other tie rules names the alternatives and records every conclusion they change. In the main text, describe an alternative in words at the place where a figure still draws it; an acronym alone carries nothing.
- Turn the three lemmas and the corollary of the information section into two prose subsections with two displays, the posterior bounds and the two gross trading returns. Run each argument in causal order: each class reaches an order through a liquidity trader; the likelihood ratio stays inside fixed bounds; the posterior stays inside its interval under every strategy; full orders give the three posteriors; the challenger's belief at a price averages order posteriors; a decision that needs a belief above the top posterior follows no price.
- Run the price argument as prose too: the entry floor, the price formula, then the step from orders to prices as a short worked argument. "Suppose two orders have one price" starts that argument well; "To see why, take..." serves the same purpose in the payment-rule subsection. Such invitations mark a worked argument and nothing else.
- Refer to support results in words in the main text: the posterior bounds, the ceiling, the trading bound. Give one appendix pointer at the end of each prose subsection, where the formal statements and proofs sit as appendix lemmas. No cross-reference in the main text points to a part letter of a lemma.
- Treat the research optimality condition the same way. Two or three sentences in the research section say that the conjectured intensity equals the investor's best research choice at the equilibrium prices and entry rule, and that the best choice is unique. The extended tuple and its seventh condition open the research appendix.
- Let the forms and threshold tables of the coexistence section live with the full statement in the appendix. The main text names equilibrium patterns in words (no informed trade, a mixed purchase that recruits, a full purchase) and reserves single-letter form names for the appendix and for figure labels that the figure itself explains.

### 2.7 Status, scope, and open results without a defensive tone

- Put the status of a numbered proposition in its title, once. After a computer-assisted proposition, allow one sentence that states the method as a proof idea: which steps use exact computation, and that the rest are analytical. Drop any sentence of the form "The status is... because".
- For a prose result in the research, robustness, welfare, or empirical section, state its status once for each cluster of results: in the first sentence that reports the cluster, or in the notes to the table that reports its numbers. The opening sentence of such a section also says that the appendix states each result in full, with its status. The main-text reader learns the status without a tag on every sentence.
- Keep "numerical diagnostic" in the appendix unless such a value enters the main text. If one does, name its status at the value and give it no equilibrium claim.
- State an open item once, as a short fact about the frontier, in the section that owns it. Place it with the scope of the result it limits, near the start of a subsection or in a figure note that draws the uncharacterized region. Do not end a section or subsection with it, and do not repeat it in the conclusion unless the conclusion frames it as the next step. The seller's choice of terms with the whole continuation retained belongs in the conclusion in that positive form, and nowhere else.
- Give each scope condition one home, and cite the home rather than restating the condition. The class ranking lives in the values subsection. The bound on the liquidity probability lives in one footnote where the bound first appears, with its counterexample in the appendix. Tie conventions live in the equilibrium subsection and the appendix table. The recruitment region for the welfare gain lives in the price-access subsection. Selection between the two empirical clusters lives in the empirical section, where it limits a test a reader might run.
- Keep margins and distances to boundaries in the parameter appendix and in table margins. In the main text, state the positive fact a margin supports: a hypothesis holds on an interval of strengths, or a band has the width it has because the preparation threshold rises slowly with strength, with the payment formula as the reason. A paragraph whose subject is closeness to failure leaves the main text.
- Never state a result first as something the paper does not claim. State the claim, then its scope in the same sentence or the next, where scope changes how a reader uses the claim.
- Keep a modal verb only where it belongs to the result itself. "A stronger incumbent can attract a challenger" states an existence claim on an open set. Stacked forms such as "can possibly", "may in some cases", and "it could be argued" leave.
- Where the slot draft states a result that changed against the earlier draft, state the new result at full strength. The control can only lower entry. Entry along the informative branch rises and then stays constant. Coexistence is a headline result, with its own proposition.

### 2.8 Literature paragraphs

- Assign each literature paragraph one contrast. The slot draft's order serves well: feedback and real effects; learning from prices; auction entry; the outside trader. Keep secondary neighbors in footnotes, one topic per footnote, as the draft does.
- In each main-text sentence, pair what the cited paper shows with the difference in this paper. Where the difference is the point, join the pair in one sentence with a semicolon and "here". Edmans, Goldstein, and Jiang give the model pattern: corrective real decisions can discourage trade on bad news; here the real decision is the buyer's entry itself.
- Use the author as subject when the claim belongs to that paper, and a parenthetical citation when the citation supports a choice or a fact in the sentence. A model choice and its supporting evidence stand in the same sentence.
- Lead each paragraph with the positive object, then the neighbors. The investor paragraph opens with an outside trader who earns the trading residual; papers in which an informed trader holds a stake, conceals purchases, or affects tendering then mark the contrast. Four negations never open a paragraph.
- State a scope abstraction (toeholds, free riding) once, in a footnote at the paragraph it qualifies. The main paragraph stays on the mechanism.
- Do not end a literature paragraph by naming what it showed. End on the last contrast, and let the next paragraph start its own.

### 2.9 Transitions, section openings, and endings

- Open a section or subsection with the economic question or the object at stake. Reserve "This section..." for the introduction roadmap and for one appendix pointer per section.
- End a subsection with the dependency the next part takes up, where such a link exists. The payoffs section ends well at present: whether entry rises depends on how the payoff change affects the information in the price. Do not end with a summary of the subsection just read.
- Carry logic with connective words inside sentences first, and between sentences second. "Therefore" and "thus" appear where a consequence truly follows; they do not punctuate every paragraph.
- Before a table or figure, one sentence says what pattern to look for. After it, read the pattern in economic terms and give the few numbers the argument reuses. Do not repeat a claim that a table note already carries, as with a control row that the note already calls a control.
- Write the conclusion as one mechanism paragraph in Laplace form, one paragraph for coexistence and research in positive form, one for sale terms, and one for the next step and the tests. The first conclusion paragraph of the slot draft runs six short sentences where the Laplace draft runs one; rejoin the chain and end on recruitment.

### 2.10 Words and patterns to avoid

- Contrast formulas used for weight: "not X but Y", "not only X but Y", and the same contrast split over two sentences. Keep a contrast only where both sides name objects in the model and the distinction changes a payoff. A holder through the sale receives sale proceeds rather than the interim price; that contrast is the welfare argument and it stays, once in the introduction and once in full in the welfare section. "Public incumbent strength is a distribution, not a bid" corrects a belief a reader plausibly brings; it stays once, in the model.
- One-line closers and label sentences: "The band is exact.", "The admissible region is a result.", and any final sentence that names what the paragraph showed. A short opening claim is different; it earns its place when the sentences after it supply the evidence.
- Staged openers and aphorisms: "at its core", "the real question is", "what matters is", "in other words" as a preface to a repetition. Say the claim.
- Forced groups of three. A list has as many items as the model has. The diligence tasks in the model section form a real list; three parallel phrases added for rhythm do not.
- Inflated or vague words: crucial, pivotal, key as an adjective, powerful, valuable, showcase, underscore as a verb, highlight as a verb, delve, landscape for a field, robust as praise outside the section title it names. Prefer "is" and "has" to "serves as" and "functions as".
- Proof talk in the main text: "delivers uniqueness", "checks each condition", "uses each hypothesis once", "the only use of the bound", "certifies a corner", "cannot be weakened" as a main-text sentence, and any sentence whose subject is a proof step. Such sentences belong with the proof. The demonstration below keeps them only under the constraint of that exercise.
- Defensive forms: "is not needed" as a section opener, "does not rise" for the control, "rises weakly" as a headline, "leaves the slot open" as a paragraph opener, "no offer is announced" where the positive timing says the same, and a paragraph that answers an objection nobody in the text has raised.
- Synonym rotation for one object. Preparation names the act and its cost. Entry names the probability that the challenger prepares. Use each word for its own object in every section, in place of the earlier draft's rotation among entry, preparation, and participation.
- Commands outside statements and worked arguments. "Let", "fix", "suppose", and "take" frame a proposition or a worked example. In expository prose, a plain sentence names the object and its role.
- Acronyms as carriers of meaning in the main text: tie-rule names and one-letter equilibrium forms stay in the appendix, in tables, and in figure notes that explain them. Condition numbers for the equilibrium definition stay in the appendix.
- Em dashes as connectors, and dashes that pair around a clause. Choose the relation and mark it with a comma, a semicolon, a colon, or a full stop.

## 3. Demonstrations

Each demonstration keeps every fact, citation key, cross-reference, and `\reg{}` macro of its span. Neither span contains a `\reg{}` macro. Each adds no fact, number, mechanism, or citation.

### 3.1 The opening of the introduction

Original location: [01_introduction.tex](slot/manuscript/sections/01_introduction.tex:3), lines 3 to 15, the first two paragraphs.

```latex
At fixed information, a stronger incumbent reduces a prospective challenger's expected return from entry into a
takeover contest. The challenger must pay preparation costs before it can submit an executable offer, and a
stronger rival lowers what that preparation earns \citep{Fishman1988,HirshleiferPng1989}. I study a force in
the opposite direction. Competition can make the target's stock more informative about the challenger, and when a
potential buyer observes that price before it commits to acquisition preparation, the information effect can
outweigh the loss of acquisition profits. A stronger incumbent then attracts another buyer and raises the
probability that a challenger from the high-value class acquires the target.

The setting is a listed target with a public sale opportunity. One buyer is already prepared, and another has
not committed to the diligence that an executable proposal requires; Section~\ref{sec:interval} describes the
decision interval in which that buyer decides while the target's stock trades. Incumbent strength is the public
distribution of the prepared bidder's value; a stronger incumbent shifts it upward, and the challenger decides
before any offer is announced.
```

The first paragraph needs one join only. Two of its sentences state one channel with its timing condition, so one sentence holds both. The short turn sentence stays: it adds the author's move, and the sentence after it supplies the content. The second paragraph goes from five sentences to three. Semicolons attach the interval reference to the buyer it describes and attach the shift to the distribution it shifts. The last clause states the timing in positive form.

## 4. Points where I depart from the scans

I accept the main lines of the three prose scans: the Laplace register as the voice model, four numbered propositions, the removal of proof notes and repeated scope from the main text, and the list of scope conditions that stay, each in one place. I differ on the points below.

First, the text after the main proposition. The defensive scan deletes the tie-rule sentences at lines 42 to 45 because the appendix already states them, and the apparatus scan keeps one bold sentence in their place. I would keep one plain sentence at the end of the whole discussion of the proposition, after the paragraphs on the window and on ownership. Tie independence has economic content there: it separates the uniqueness of the main result from the tie sensitivity that the coexistence section later studies. A reader should meet that fact in the main text, once, without a paragraph of reassurance and without bold type.

Second, status in the later sections. The apparatus scan removes the inline status tags in Sections 5 to 8 and leaves status with the appendix statements. A main-text reader then meets computer-assisted examples in the robustness section with no sign of their status until the appendix. Section 2.7 above keeps status once per cluster of prose results, in the reporting sentence or a table note. One clause per cluster costs little and keeps the status in the reading path, as the brief asks.

Third, the coexistence thresholds. The apparatus scan defines five strengths in one long sentence before the short form of Proposition 3. That sentence compresses a table into prose and keeps the load it removes elsewhere. The main text needs the start of coexistence, the start of full purchases, the end of no trade, and the entry ceiling. The remaining two strengths belong with the full statement in the appendix; the sale threshold enters the empirical section in words, at the place that uses it.

Fourth, the two comparisons in the model. The defensive scan moves that paragraph to the appendix as material for proofs alone. I keep the distinction in the model section, in two sentences. A reader needs it to read the fixed-information control correctly: a deviation holds prices and the entry rule fixed, and a comparison across strengths solves the equilibrium again. The final sentence of the current paragraph, which says what proofs use, goes to the appendix with the proofs.

Fifth, the bargaining extension. The defensive scan deletes the sentence that calls the trading and preparation equilibrium under bargaining open, and puts positive scope at the start of the subsection instead. Positive scope alone may leave a reader to assume that the trading equilibrium follows by the same steps. I keep one open statement, placed with the scope sentence at the start of the subsection, and end the subsection on the payment-sensitivity argument. The frontier stays visible, and no subsection ends on it.

One smaller point concerns wording, not placement. The defensive scan proposes to open one introduction paragraph with "The change in information does the work." As an opener, the line still stages its claim. A full topic sentence serves better: the reversal comes from the change in information, and the rest of the sentence or the next one gives the rational-pricing fact and the fixed-orders control. The content of the scan's rewrite stands; the clipped first line goes.
