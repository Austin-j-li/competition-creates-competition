The order-slot model: an EGJ-like trading stage

One order of unit size arrives before the challenger decides. It comes from the informed investor or from a liquidity trader with an order of the same size. The market cannot tell which. Only the trading stage changes. The quality classes, their prior, the value distributions, the costs and the auction stay as in the paper.

Status: analytical complete proof   checked reproduced by an independent member   numerical diagnostic floating-point evidence   open

1The setup

  1. Nature draws the challenger's quality class \(j\in\{H,L\}\) with prior \(\pi=\Pr(j=H)\). Given the class, the challenger's value is \(\theta\sim G_j\). The high class dominates: \(G_H(u)\le G_L(u)\) for every \(u\), and both supports lie above the reserve \(p\). The incumbent's value \(R\) has distribution \(F\); in the leading case \(R\sim U[0,r]\).
  2. One order slot opens. With probability \(\lambda\), the informed investor fills it. The investor sees the class \(j\), not the value \(\theta\). It chooses \(q\in\{-1,0,1\}\) at cost \(k\lvert q\rvert\).
  3. With probability \(1-\lambda\), a liquidity trader fills it. It sells, waits or buys one unit with probabilities \(\beta\), \(1-2\beta\), \(\beta\).
  4. Competitive market makers see the order \(Q\) and set \(P=\mathbb{E}[V_T\mid Q]\). Nobody sees who placed the order.
  5. The challenger sees \(P\) and its own cost \(C\in\{0,c_H\}\), with \(\Pr(C=0)=\rho\). It then decides whether to prepare. Preparation reveals \(\theta\). The paper's second-price auction with reserve \(p\) follows.

The paper's benchmark is the point-mass case: \(G_H\) puts all its mass on \(h\), \(G_L\) on \(\ell\), and \(\pi=\tfrac12\). The numbers on this page use that benchmark unless a line says otherwise.

Under full orders (class H buys, class L sells), the order has these probabilities in each class. The prior does not enter them:

\[ \Pr(Q=q\mid j)\;=\; \begin{array}{c|ccc} & q=-1 & q=0 & q=+1\\ \hline j=H & b & 1-\lambda-2b & \lambda+b\\ j=L & \lambda+b & 1-\lambda-2b & b \end{array} \]

Here \(b=(1-\lambda)\,\beta\) is the probability of a liquidity buy, and also of a liquidity sale.

The same results hold with a buy-only menu \(q\in\{0,1\}\), in which the liquidity trader buys with probability \(\beta\).

2What an order tells the market

One number summarizes the slot. A buy is \(1+u\) times as likely in class H as in class L, where

\[ u\;=\;\frac{\lambda}{(1-\lambda)\,\beta},\qquad \frac{\Pr(Q=+1\mid H)}{\Pr(Q=+1\mid L)}=\frac{\lambda+b}{b}=1+u . \]

Bayes' rule with prior \(\pi\) gives the posterior that the class is H. Under full orders it is

\[ \mu_{+}=\frac{\pi(1+u)}{\pi(1+u)+1-\pi}\equiv M(\pi),\qquad \mu_{-}=\frac{\pi}{\pi+(1-\pi)(1+u)}\equiv m(\pi), \]

and \(\mu_{0}=\pi\) after no order.

Under every strategy, a single order moves the odds by a factor between \(1/(1+u)\) and \(1+u\). So every posterior satisfies \(m(\pi)\le\mu\le M(\pi)\), each order occurs in both classes, and no order is off the equilibrium path. No belief rule is necessary. analytical

The link to the paper's bounds. The paper's Laplace bounds are \(M(\pi)=\pi/\big(\pi+(1-\pi)e^{-2/b}\big)\) and \(m(\pi)=\pi/\big(\pi+(1-\pi)e^{2/b}\big)\). The slot gives the same two functions of \(\pi\) when \(1+u=e^{2/b}\), that is, \(u=e-1\) at \(b=2\). In the benchmark, \(M(\tfrac12)=\frac{1+u}{2+u}\) and \(m(\tfrac12)=1-M(\tfrac12)\).

Why EGJ's own block fails here. EGJ add noise \(Z\in\{-1,0,1\}\) to the order, so the flow is \(X=q+Z\). The flow \(X=2\) then needs an informed buy, so \(\Pr(H\mid X=2)=1\). At \(r_2=3.6\) a sure class H still covers \(c_H\), so entry cannot return to \(\rho\). This holds for every \(\lambda\), every noise weight and every \(k\). analytical

The liquidity buy removes the revealing flow: a buy can also come from class L.

3Prices, entry and the investor's return

The auction objects are the paper's. For a known value \(\theta\), let \(t(\theta)\) be expected target proceeds after the challenger prepares, and \(g(\theta)\) the challenger's expected gross profit; \(t_0\) is proceeds without entry. The class objects are expectations over the class distribution:

\[ t_j=\mathbb E_j\big[t(\theta)\big],\qquad g_j=\mathbb E_j\big[g(\theta)\big],\qquad j\in\{H,L\}, \] \[ \Delta_T=t_H-t_L,\qquad B(\mu)=g_L+\mu\,(g_H-g_L). \]

The challenger's value depends on the price only through its class, so its gross profit is linear in the posterior \(\mu\). In the point-mass benchmark with \(R\sim U[0,r]\):

\[ t_0=p\Big(1-\frac{p}{r}\Big),\qquad t_H=\frac r2+\frac{p^2}{2r},\qquad t_L=\ell-\frac{\ell^2-p^2}{2r}, \] \[ g_H=h-t_H,\qquad g_L=\ell-t_L,\qquad \Delta_T=\frac{(r-\ell)^2}{2r}. \]

The zero-cost type always prepares. The high-cost type prepares when its expected gain covers \(c_H\), that is, when the posterior reaches a threshold:

\[ e(\mu)=\rho+(1-\rho)\,\mathbf 1\{\mu\ge\tau(r)\}, \] \[ \tau(r)=\frac{c_H-g_L}{g_H-g_L}=\frac{48r-3}{4\,(20r-r^2-1)} . \]

The last expression uses the benchmark \(h=10,\ \ell=1,\ p=\tfrac12,\ c_H=6\).

The competitive price at posterior \(\mu\) is

\[ P(\mu)=t_0+e(\mu)\,\big(t_L-t_0+\Delta_T\,\mu\big). \]

Given that it fills the slot, the informed investor's net return from a full order is

\[ K_H=e(M)\,\Delta_T\,\big(1-M\big)-k\qquad\text{(class H buys)}, \] \[ K_L=\rho\,\Delta_T\,m-k\qquad\text{(class L sells)}, \]

with \(M=M(\pi)\) and \(m=m(\pi)\). A buy at any posterior \(\mu\) earns \(e(\mu)\Delta_T(1-\mu)\) before the cost, and a sale earns \(e(\mu)\Delta_T\mu\).

A buy that recruits the high-cost challenger raises \(e(M)\) from \(\rho\) to 1. This raises the return of the buy. This is the paper's feedback, now in a discrete form: each decision is one comparison of a return with \(k\).

5Coexistence: the Proposition 3 analog

Let \(\mathfrak r(D)=1+D+\sqrt{D^2+2D}\), the root of \(\Delta_T(r)=D\) above \(\ell\) in the benchmark family. Under no informed trade, every order gives the posterior \(\pi\). A buy by class H earns \(\rho\Delta_T(1-\pi)-k\), and a sale by class L earns \(\rho\Delta_T\pi-k\). So no trade is an equilibrium exactly when

\[ \rho\,\Delta_T(r)\,\max\{\pi,\,1-\pi\}\;\le\;k \iff r\le r_N(\pi)=\mathfrak r\Big(\frac{k}{\rho\max\{\pi,1-\pi\}}\Big), \]

8What it keeps, and what it costs

Keeps

  • The paper's class model: prior \(\pi\) and value distributions \(G_H,G_L\).
  • All of Proposition 2, with unique outcomes.
  • The paper's no-trade boundary \(r_N\).
  • Coexistence on \([1.394,\,1.748]\) in the benchmark.
  • The access-to-prices result.
  • No off-path beliefs, and short proofs.
  • A buy-only version with the same results.

Costs

  • Unit orders. With \(q\in[-1,1]\), a 0.99 buy reaches an unused order. Under a prior belief there it earns 0.0964 against 0.0446 at \(r_2\), and part (iii) fails. A continuous menu needs EGJ's belief convention.
  • An exogenous liquidity motive. A free uninformed trader would not trade.
  • No partial sale at 1.55–1.65. A mixed sale by class L appears only on \((1.748,\,2.107)\).
  • Flat entry at 1.55–1.65. The strict rise lies on \([1.394,\,1.461)\) and needs investor mixing.

The trade-off behind the last two costs. With unit orders at the benchmark, the return to \(\rho\) at \(r_2\) caps the top posterior at \(\tau(\tfrac{18}5)=0.731\). An EGJ-type branch with entry that rises at 1.55–1.65 needs a top posterior above 0.755. The setups that do both work only in boxes about 0.001 wide. analytical One option is to present the rise and the partial sale as features of continuous order sizes.
