The order-slot model: an EGJ-like trading stage

One order of unit size arrives before the challenger decides. It comes from the informed investor or from a liquidity trader with an order of the same size. The market cannot tell which. Only the trading stage changes; the classes, the costs, the auction and the challenger's information stay as in the paper.

1The setup

  1. Nature draws the challenger's class \(j\in\{H,L\}\), each with probability \(\tfrac12\), and the incumbent's value \(R\sim U[0,r]\).
  2. One order slot opens. With probability \(\lambda\), the informed investor fills it. The investor sees \(j\) and chooses \(q\in\{-1,0,1\}\) at cost \(k\lvert q\rvert\).
  3. With probability \(1-\lambda\), a liquidity trader fills it. It sells, waits or buys one unit with probabilities \(\beta\), \(1-2\beta\), \(\beta\).
  4. Competitive market makers see the order \(Q\) and set \(P=\mathbb{E}[V_T\mid Q]\). Nobody sees who placed the order.
  5. The challenger sees \(P\) and its own cost \(C\in\{0,c_H\}\). It then decides whether to prepare. The paper's second-price auction with reserve \(p\) follows.

Under full orders (class H buys, class L sells), the order has these probabilities in each class:

\[ \Pr(Q=q\mid j)\;=\; \begin{array}{c|ccc} & q=-1 & q=0 & q=+1\\ \hline j=H & b & 1-\lambda-2b & \lambda+b\\ j=L & \lambda+b & 1-\lambda-2b & b \end{array} \]

Here \(b=(1-\lambda)\,\beta\) is the probability of a liquidity buy, and also of a liquidity sale.

The same results hold with a buy-only menu \(q\in\{0,1\}\), in which the liquidity trader buys with probability \(\beta\).

2What an order tells the market

One number summarizes the slot: the odds that a buy in class H comes from the informed investor rather than from the liquidity trader,

\[ u\;=\;\frac{\lambda}{(1-\lambda)\,\beta}. \]

Under full orders, the posterior that the class is H after each order is

\[ \mu_{+}=\Pr(H\mid Q=+1)=\frac{\lambda+b}{\lambda+2b}=\frac{1+u}{2+u}\;\equiv\;M, \] \[ \mu_{0}=\Pr(H\mid Q=0)=\tfrac12,\qquad \mu_{-}=\Pr(H\mid Q=-1)=1-M . \]

Each order occurs in both classes under every strategy. So every posterior lies strictly between 0 and 1, and no order is off the equilibrium path. No belief rule is necessary. analytical checked

Why EGJ's own block fails here. EGJ add noise \(Z\in\{-1,0,1\}\) to the order, so the flow is \(X=q+Z\). The flow \(X=2\) then needs an informed buy, so \(\Pr(H\mid X=2)=1\). At \(r_2=3.6\) a sure class H still covers \(c_H\), so entry cannot return to \(\rho\). This holds for every \(\lambda\), every noise weight and every \(k\). analytical

The liquidity buy removes the revealing flow: a buy can also come from class L.

3Prices, entry and the investor's return

The auction objects are the paper's (incumbent value uniform on \([0,r]\), reserve \(p\)):

\[ t_0=p\Big(1-\frac{p}{r}\Big),\qquad t_H=\frac r2+\frac{p^2}{2r},\qquad t_L=\ell-\frac{\ell^2-p^2}{2r}, \] \[ \Delta_T=t_H-t_L=\frac{(r-\ell)^2}{2r}, \] \[ g_H=h-t_H,\qquad g_L=\ell-t_L,\qquad B_r(\mu)=g_L+\mu\,(g_H-g_L). \]

The zero-cost type always prepares. The high-cost type prepares when its expected gain covers \(c_H\), that is, when the posterior reaches a threshold:

\[ e(\mu)=\rho+(1-\rho)\,\mathbf 1\{\mu\ge\tau(r)\}, \] \[ \tau(r)=\frac{c_H-g_L}{g_H-g_L}=\frac{48r-3}{4\,(20r-r^2-1)} . \]

The last expression uses the benchmark \(h=10,\ \ell=1,\ p=\tfrac12,\ c_H=6\).

The competitive price at posterior \(\mu\) is

\[ P(\mu)=t_0+e(\mu)\,\big(t_L-t_0+\Delta_T\,\mu\big). \]

Given that it fills the slot, the informed investor's net return from a full order is

\[ K_H=e(M)\,\Delta_T\,(1-M)-k\qquad\text{(class H buys)}, \] \[ K_L=\rho\,\Delta_T\,(1-M)-k\qquad\text{(class L sells)}. \]

A buy that recruits the high-cost challenger raises \(e(M)\) from \(\rho\) to 1. This raises the return of the buy. This is the paper's feedback, now in a discrete form: each decision is one comparison of a return with \(k\).

5Coexistence: the Proposition 3 analog

Let \(\mathfrak r(D)=1+D+\sqrt{D^2+2D}\), the root of \(\Delta_T(r)=D\) above \(\ell\). Under no informed trade, every order gives the posterior \(\tfrac12\), and a deviation earns \(\rho\Delta_T/2-k\). So no trade is an equilibrium exactly when

\[ r\;\le\;r_N=\mathfrak r\Big(\frac{2k}{\rho}\Big)=1.747878 , \]

which is the paper's own boundary. An informative equilibrium exists from \(r_A\) on, where the investor and the challenger are both indifferent:

\[ \Delta_T(r_A)\,\big(1-\tau(r_A)\big)=k,\qquad r_A=1.393958 . \]

Between \(r_A\) and \(r_F=\mathfrak r\big(k/(1-M)\big)\), class H buys with an effective rate \(s\in(0,\lambda)\), and entry rises with strength:

\[ s=b\Big(\frac{\Delta_T(r)}{k}-2\Big),\qquad \mathsf{E}(r)=\rho+(1-\rho)\,\frac{b\,\Delta_T(r)}{2k}. \]

Above \(r_F\), class H buys fully and entry stays at \(\mathsf E(r_1)\) up to the ceiling. No trade and informative trade coexist on \([r_A,r_N]=[1.394,\,1.748]\), which contains the paper's points 1.55, 1.60 and 1.65. analytical checked

8What it keeps, and what it costs

Keeps

  • All of Proposition 2, with unique outcomes.
  • The paper's no-trade boundary \(r_N\).
  • Coexistence on \([1.394,\,1.748]\).
  • The access-to-prices result.
  • No off-path beliefs, and short proofs.
  • A buy-only version with the same results.

Costs

  • Unit orders. With \(q\in[-1,1]\), a 0.99 buy reaches an unused order. Under a prior belief there it earns 0.0964 against 0.0446 at \(r_2\), and part (iii) fails. A continuous menu needs EGJ's belief convention.
  • An exogenous liquidity motive. A free uninformed trader would not trade.
  • No partial sale at 1.55–1.65. A mixed sale by class L appears only on \((1.748,\,2.107)\).
  • Flat entry at 1.55–1.65. The strict rise lies on \([1.394,\,1.461)\) and needs investor mixing.

The trade-off behind the last two costs. With unit orders at the benchmark, the return to \(\rho\) at \(r_2\) caps the top posterior at \(\tau(\tfrac{18}5)=0.731\). An EGJ-type branch with entry that rises at 1.55–1.65 needs a top posterior above 0.755. The setups that do both work only in boxes about 0.001 wide. analytical One option is to present the rise and the partial sale as features of continuous order sizes.

9Precedent

  • Dow and Gorton (1997, Journal of Finance). An informed trader or a liquidity trader of the same size arrives. The arrival of information is "perfectly negatively correlated with the arrival of a liquidity trader" (p. 1095). verified
  • Edmans, Goldstein and Jiang (2015, American Economic Review). Unit orders, and three readings of \(\lambda\): presence, a liquidity shock, or information with probability \(\lambda\). verified
  • Admati and Pfleiderer (2009, Review of Financial Studies). A liquidity shock forces the same whole-stake sale as bad news. verified
