The overlapping sign, covariance, and trend proposals are treated together below. Write

$$
\Omega(F)=W_H(F)-W_L(F),\qquad D=G_L-G_H,\qquad \Gamma(r)=\int_0^rD(y)\,dy.
$$

Here \(\Omega\) always denotes a **difference**. Solver E Candidate 2 uses that same letter for a ratio; I call that ratio \(Q=W_H/W_L\). All comparative statics of the full-order gap below hold the prior and slot fixed unless explicitly stated otherwise.

## Solver C 1, Solver B 3, and Solver E 1(a)–(b): the general identity and its sign

**Verdict: proved.** Solver E's unrestricted strictness sentence requires the small correction stated below.

The standing ranking puts every challenger value at or above the reserve. Since the incumbent law is continuous and independent of challenger value, ties have probability zero, including when the classes have atoms. Thus

$$
\begin{aligned}
W_j(F)
&=\Pr(R<\theta_j)
=\int\Pr(\theta_j>y)\,dF(y)\\
&=\int[1-G_j(y)]\,dF(y),\\
\Omega(F)&=\int[G_L(y)-G_H(y)]\,dF(y)
=\mathbb E_F[D(R)]\ge0.
\end{aligned}
$$

Using \(G_j(y^-)\) instead makes no difference: the class laws have at most countably many atoms and \(F\) charges none. Because the integrand is nonnegative,

$$
\Omega(F)>0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\Pr_F\{D(R)>0\}>0.
$$

Conditional on an order, preparation depends on the resulting price and the independent cost draw. It does not select the class or value further:

$$
\Pr(H\mid X=x,A)=\mu(x),\qquad
\Pr(W\mid j,X=x,A)=W_j(F).
$$

Therefore, in any equilibrium,

$$
w_x=W_L+\mu(x)\Omega,\qquad
\Pr(W\mid X=x)=e(x)w_x.
$$

Subtracting at any two orders gives

$$
w_x-w_{x'}=[\mu(x)-\mu(x')]\Omega.
$$

Under full two-sided trade, \(\mu(1)=M>\pi>m=\mu(-1)\), so

$$
\boxed{w_1-w_{-1}=(M-m)(W_H-W_L)=(M-m)\mathbb E_F[D(R)]\ge0.}
$$

It is strictly positive exactly when \(\Pr_F(D(R)>0)>0\). This is the best general replacement for A.13(ii).

The related price ordering is also valid under full orders. Positive trading cost requires \(\Delta_T>0\) for full trade; entry is nondecreasing in the posterior, and \(P=t_0+e(\mu)[t_L-t_0+\Delta_T\mu]\), with \(e\ge\rho>0\) and \(t_L-t_0\ge0\). Thus distinct full-order posteriors have strictly ordered prices, and, when \(\Omega>0\), strictly ordered prepared-challenger win probabilities.

The quantile interpretation is also correct. Couple \(\theta_L=G_L^{-1}(U)\) and \(\theta_H=G_H^{-1}(U)\) using an auxiliary uniform variable independent of \(R\). Ranking gives \(\theta_L\le\theta_H\); subtracting the nested events \(\{R<\theta_L\}\subseteq\{R<\theta_H\}\) yields

$$
\Omega=\Pr(\theta_L\le R<\theta_H).
$$

This is an interpretation of two counterfactual class draws, not an additional assumption about two actual bidders.

**Strictness correction to Solver E 1(b).** Outside full orders, positive \(\Omega\) alone does not make two order-conditioned win probabilities different: the posteriors must differ too. At the actual benchmark \(r_0=6/5\), \(\Delta_T=1/60<k=1/50\), so no informed trade is the equilibrium, all order posteriors equal \(1/2\), and the order winner gap is zero although \(\Omega=1/6>0\). The ratio of the order gap to the class gap also requires \(\Omega>0\); at zero it is undefined.

**Level comparison.** If \(F_1\le F_0\) pointwise, then \(W_j(F_1)=\mathbb E_jF_1(\theta)\le\mathbb E_jF_0(\theta)=W_j(F_0)\). At a fixed posterior, \(w_x\) is a fixed convex combination of these two probabilities and therefore weakly falls. With interior posterior weights, a strict fall in either class probability makes the combination fall strictly. Solver C's result is about the level **conditional on preparation**; an unconditional winning probability also contains endogenous entry and need not fall.

## Solver C 1 and Solver E 2: odds and relative-risk signs

**Verdict: both algebraic reformulations are proved; neither yields an unconditional trend theorem.**

For the prepared-challenger odds ratio,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{OR}_W-1
&=\frac{w_1(1-w_{-1})-w_{-1}(1-w_1)}{w_{-1}(1-w_1)}\\
&=\frac{(M-m)\Omega}{w_{-1}(1-w_1)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

When \(\Omega>0\), interior posteriors imply \(0<w_{-1}<w_1<1\): \(W_H>0\), \(W_L<1\), and both posterior weights are positive. The denominator is therefore positive, so the odds ratio exceeds one. When \(\Omega=0\), the ratio is one if the common win probability is interior, and undefined at a common probability zero or one.

For \(W_L>0\), put \(Q=W_H/W_L\). The relative risk is

$$
\operatorname{RR}=\frac{w_1}{w_{-1}}
=\frac{1-M+MQ}{1-m+mQ},\qquad
\frac{\partial\operatorname{RR}}{\partial Q}
=\frac{M-m}{(1-m+mQ)^2}>0.
$$

Since \(Q\ge1\), \(1\le\operatorname{RR}<M/m\). Equality at the lower bound is equivalent to \(\Omega=0\). The upper bound is approached as \(Q\to\infty\); if \(W_L=0<W_H\), the risk ratio itself is defined and equals \(M/m\). At prior one-half, \(M/m=1+u\). The auxiliary conditional probability \(\Omega/W_H=1-1/Q\) has Solver E's proposed quantile-coupling interpretation.

The known point-class counterexample illustrates the distinction. With values \(1,2\) and the benchmark slot, for \(r\ge2\), \(Q=2\) and \(\operatorname{RR}=1.364175327\) is constant. Nevertheless the absolute gap is \(\vartheta/r\), and the odds ratio falls:

| \(r\) | Absolute gap | Winner odds ratio |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 2 | 0.231058579 | 3.708213003 |
| 3 | 0.154039052 | 1.860974323 |
| 10 | 0.046211716 | 1.440413481 |
| 100 | 0.004621172 | 1.370590465 |

These reproduce Solver E's numbers. A nonnegative odds or risk association is a valid reformulation; replacing a difference by a ratio does not by itself repair a strength comparative static.

## All three solvers: the covariance constants agree

**Verdict: both constants in the question are right, at the symmetric prior under full orders. There is no factor discrepancy.**

Write \(\bar f=b+\lambda/2\). At prior one-half,

$$
\vartheta=M-m=\frac{\lambda}{2b+\lambda},\qquad
\bar f\vartheta=\left(b+\frac\lambda2\right)\frac\lambda{2b+\lambda}
=\frac\lambda2.
$$

Full orders give \(\Pr(X=1)=\Pr(X=-1)=\bar f\) and \(\mathbb E X=0\). If entry is \(\rho\) after every order, then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Cov}(X,\mathbf1_W)
&=\mathbb E[X\mathbf1_W]
=\bar f\rho(w_1-w_{-1})\\
&=\boxed{\bar f\rho\vartheta\Omega
=\frac{\rho\lambda}{2}\Omega}.
\end{aligned}
$$

It is strictly positive iff \(\Omega>0\), given the standing positive \(\rho,\lambda\). Meanwhile \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,\mathbf1_A)=0\). Liquidity mass disappears from this expression **holding informed presence \(\lambda\) fixed**.

Solver C's arbitrary-prior extension is also exactly right. Under full orders,

$$
\mathbb E[X\mid H]=\lambda,\qquad
\mathbb E[X\mid L]=-\lambda,
$$

so \(\mathbb E X=\lambda(2\pi-1)\). Bayes' rule and iterated expectations give

$$
\mathbb E\mu(X)=\pi,\qquad
\mathbb E[X\mu(X)]=\mathbb E[X\mathbf1_H]=\pi\lambda.
$$

Consequently

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Cov}(X,\mathbf1_W)
=\rho\Omega\operatorname{Cov}(X,\mu(X))
=2\pi(1-\pi)\rho\lambda\Omega.}
$$

Solver B's comment that the general-prior calculation must be centered is correct but incomplete; this supplies the coefficient. One cannot retain \(\bar f\rho\vartheta\) unchanged away from \(\pi=1/2\).

## Solver E 1(c)–(d): two channels and the winner second difference

**Verdict: proved as a within-model detection result under symmetric full orders and purchase-only recruitment. Its magnitude is not auction-free.**

Suppose \(\pi=1/2\), full orders hold, and \(e(-1)=e(0)=\rho\). Under C1, let \(\chi=\mathbf1\{B_F(M)\ge c_H\}\), so \(e(1)=\rho+(1-\rho)\chi\). These entry restrictions follow from \(\tau>1/2\); they must accompany a generalized statement.

Define \(y_x=\Pr(W\mid X=x)=e(x)w_x\). Subtracting purchase and sale gives

$$
y_1-y_{-1}=\rho(w_1-w_{-1})+(1-\rho)\chi w_1.
$$

Multiplying by \(\bar f\) proves the full covariance decomposition:

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Cov}(X,\mathbf1_W)
=\frac{\rho\lambda}{2}\Omega
+\bar f(1-\rho)\chi w_1.}
$$

For the proposed difference-in-differences, symmetry gives \(M+m=1\), so

$$
w_1-2w_0+w_{-1}=(M-1+m)\Omega=0.
$$

Hence

$$
\boxed{(y_1-y_0)-(y_0-y_{-1})=(1-\rho)\chi w_1.}
$$

If \(\chi=0\), the statistic is zero. If \(\chi=1\), then \(B_F(M)\ge c_H>0\). Positive expected acquisition profit requires positive probability of winning, so \(w_1>0\); since \(1-\rho>0\), the statistic is strictly positive. Thus its **sign identifies whether the purchase recruits** without estimating the auction distribution. It does not identify the recruitment increment's magnitude from this statistic alone, because

$$
w_1=W_L+M\Omega
$$

still depends on the auction. Solver E's sentence that the statistic “does not involve piv(F)” is false if read as a claim about its magnitude. What cancels is the additive class-composition second difference. Under CM, replace \(\chi\) by the high-cost entry probability after a purchase; positivity then detects positive extra entry.

The restrictions are substantive. At a general prior, with the same purchase-only entry pattern,

$$
y_1-2y_0+y_{-1}
=\rho(M+m-2\pi)\Omega+[e(1)-\rho]w_1.
$$

An explicit false positive without recruitment uses \(p=1/2\), class values \(1,2\), \(R\sim U[0,3]\), \(\pi=1/4\), \(\rho=1/2\), \(\lambda=\beta=1/4\), \(c_H=1\), and \(k=.01\). Here \(M=7/16\), \(m=1/8\), \(\Omega=1/3\), and entry is \(\rho\) at every order, yet the second difference is

$$
\frac12\left(\frac7{16}+\frac18-\frac12\right)\frac13
=\frac1{96}=0.010416667>0.
$$

The gross correct-order returns are \(0.140625\) and \(0.03125\), both exceeding \(k\), and their universal bounds force full orders. Conversely, if all three orders recruit, a symmetric second difference is zero even though entry is high after purchases. This is an order-category second difference, not an unrestricted causal difference-in-differences design for observational data.

## Solver C 2, Solver E 1(f), and Solver B 1–2: exact and primitive trend conditions

### Exact uniform-family condition

**Verdict: proved, with one-sided derivatives at atoms.**

For \(F_r=U[0,r]\),

$$
\Omega(r)=\frac1r\int_0^rD(y)\,dy=\frac{\Gamma(r)}r.
$$

Since \(D=0\) below \(p\), the candidates' integrals beginning at zero and at \(p\) agree when \(r\ge p\). At a continuity point of \(D\), the fundamental theorem of calculus and the quotient rule give

$$
\Omega'(r)=\frac{rD(r)-\Gamma(r)}{r^2}
=\frac{D(r)-\Omega(r)}r.
$$

Thus the gap's derivative is nonnegative exactly when

$$
\boxed{D(r)\ge\frac1r\int_0^rD(y)\,dy.}
$$

Strict inequality gives a positive derivative. For a whole interval of strengths, the weak condition holding almost everywhere is necessary and sufficient for nondecrease, because \(\Gamma(r)/r\) is absolutely continuous on compact intervals bounded away from zero. For two particular strengths, the exact condition is simply \(\Gamma(r_1)/r_1\ge\Gamma(r_0)/r_0\); pointwise positivity everywhere is unnecessary.

Solver C correctly handles jumps: the right derivative uses \(D(r)\), the left derivative uses \(D(r^-)\). Solver E's truncated-value version therefore requires

$$
\Omega'_+(r)=\frac{\mathbb E_L[\theta\mathbf1\{\theta\le r\}]
-\mathbb E_H[\theta\mathbf1\{\theta\le r\}]}{r^2},
$$

whereas replacing \(\le r\) by \(<r\) gives the **left** derivative. To see this, integration by parts gives

$$
\mathbb E_j[\theta\mathbf1\{\theta\le r\}]
=rG_j(r)-\int_0^rG_j(y)\,dy;
$$

subtracting the two class expressions yields \(rD(r)-\Gamma(r)\). Solver E mixes \(D(r^-)\) with \(D(r)\) in this step; that matters exactly at the point-mass benchmark's turning point.

### Monotone class separation: sufficiency and the correct necessity claim

**Verdict: sufficient as stated in substance; necessity needs the universal-family quantifier and the endpoint correction.**

For any FOSD strengthening, couple incumbent draws with \(R_1\ge R_0\) almost surely. If \(D\) is nondecreasing across the relevant incumbent values, then

$$
D(R_1)\ge D(R_0)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\Omega(F_1)\ge\Omega(F_0).
$$

Strict increase holds exactly when \(\Pr(D(R_1)>D(R_0))>0\) under this monotone coupling. On an interval where \(D\) is nondecreasing, integration by parts equivalently gives

$$
\Omega(F_1)-\Omega(F_0)
=\int(F_0-F_1)\,dD,
$$

so strictness requires positive mass for the measure \(dD\) where \(F_0-F_1>0\).

For absolutely continuous classes, this condition is \(\psi_L\ge\psi_H\) almost everywhere on the interval, where \(\psi_j\) denotes a class density, not the paper's profit \(g_j\). For arbitrary classes it is the measure inequality \(dG_L-dG_H\ge0\), including jumps. The posterior interpretation “a value here is never good news about H” is correct at values where the mixture has a well-defined positive density or mass. A density comparison only on disconnected incumbent supports is not enough if it ignores a decrease between the supports; nondecrease of the CDF difference across the ordered support values is the safe statement.

Necessity is true for monotonicity of \(\Omega\) under **every continuous FOSD pair in a common interval**, on \([0,\bar r)\). If \(a<b<\bar r\) and \(D(a)>D(b)\), choose \(F_0=U[a,a+\varepsilon]\) and \(F_1=U[b,b+\varepsilon]\), with disjoint small intervals. The second dominates the first. Right continuity gives

$$
\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow0}\{\Omega(F_1)-\Omega(F_0)\}
=D(b)-D(a)<0.
$$

If full support starting at zero is required, mixing the same small uniform distribution on \([0,\bar r]\) into both laws preserves FOSD and multiplies the negative difference by a positive constant. This proves the interior necessity claim. It is not a necessity theorem for one specified family such as \(U[0,r]\), nor automatically for a restricted subset where fixed-cost full-trade incentives happen to hold.

**Counterexample to Solver B's literal closed-endpoint necessity.** Set \(G_L=\delta_1\), \(G_H=\delta_2\), and \(\bar r=2\). For every continuous incumbent law supported on \([0,2]\), \(W_H=1\) and \(\Omega=1-F(1)\), so every strengthening weakly raises \(\Omega\). Nevertheless \(D(1)=1\) and \(D(2)=0\): \(D\) is not nondecreasing on the closed interval. An atom of the high class at the maximum incumbent value causes an irrelevant endpoint drop because the incumbent puts no mass there. Solver E's use of \([p,\bar r)\) avoids this error.

**Not necessary for the uniform family.** Solver C's example is valid:

$$
G_L=\tfrac12\delta_1+\tfrac12\delta_2,\qquad
G_H=\tfrac25\delta_{3/2}+\tfrac35\delta_{10}.
$$

Here \(D\) takes the consecutive values \(1/2,1/10,3/5,0\), so it decreases inside the incumbent range. Yet for \(2<r<10\),

$$
\Gamma(r)=\tfrac35r-\tfrac9{10},\qquad
\Omega(r)=\tfrac35-\frac9{10r},\qquad
\Omega'(r)=\frac9{10r^2}>0.
$$

Direct calculation gives \(\Omega(3)=.30\), \(\Omega(3.6)=.35\), and \(\Omega(5)=.42\). The manuscript's wide-class example is another valid failure of necessity, quantified below.

### High-class outbidding: the useful shared economic condition

**Verdict: proved as a sufficient weak trend condition. It is the same auction restriction used to repair the research-branch ownership result.**

Assume \(W_H(F_s)=1\) throughout the path. Then

$$
\Omega(F_s)=1-W_L(F_s),\qquad
w_1-w_{-1}=(M-m)[1-W_L(F_s)].
$$

A strengthening weakly lowers \(W_L\), proving the weak trend. Strict increase requires a strict decrease in \(W_L\): the incumbent must actually take wins away from low-class challengers. For example, with class values \(1,10\), shifting the incumbent from \(U[2,3]\) to \(U[4,5]\) leaves \(W_L=0\), \(W_H=1\), and the gap constant despite a strict strengthening.

The candidates' primitive \(G_H(\bar r)=0\) is sufficient but stronger than necessary at the endpoint. With a continuous incumbent, \(G_H(\bar r^-)=0\), meaning every H value is at least \(\bar r\), already ensures H wins almost surely. Equivalently, state the economic condition \(W_H\equiv1\) directly.

The nesting is strict. For example, \(G_L=\delta_1\), \(G_H=\tfrac12\delta_1+\tfrac12\delta_{10}\), and incumbent upper bound 3.6 give \(D=1/2\) on \([1,3.6]\), which is nondecreasing on the incumbent range, but at \(r=3.6\), \(W_H=23/36<1\). Together with Solver C's preceding example, this proves both strict inclusions from outbidding to monotone separation to the uniform trend condition.

The link to ownership is exact but should keep the equilibrium branches distinct. On the research branch at prior one-half,

$$
O_H(F)=\tfrac12W_H(F)
\left[\rho+(1-\rho)\{\beta+(1-\beta)\lambda_R(F)\}\right].
$$

If \(W_H=1\), its direct decline disappears, and rising research intensity raises ownership on that branch. For the winner-gap theorem, the same auction restriction removes the decline in \(W_H\), leaving the decline in \(W_L\) to widen the gap under a **fixed full-trade slot**. This is one economically interpretable restriction supporting two different results, not a claim that ownership increases across every regime or ceiling.

For the benchmark path, \(h-\bar r=10-3.6=6.4\) and \(h/\bar r=25/9\). The condition holds with a large support margin. It is sufficient, not the weakest possible restriction.

## Solver B 2 and Solver E 1(e): path derivatives and hump shape

**Verdict: the benchmark hump and the uniform single-peaked-D result are proved; an unrestricted hump and a universal smooth-root characterization are false.**

For a sufficiently differentiable FOSD family and absolutely continuous class laws, integration by parts gives

$$
\Omega(F_s)=\int(1-F_s)\,dD,
$$

because the boundary terms vanish. Differentiating under an integrable domination condition yields

$$
\frac{d}{ds}\Omega(F_s)
=\int[-\partial_sF_s(y)]\,[\psi_L(y)-\psi_H(y)]\,dy.
$$

Thus Solver B's exact path derivative is correct under these regularity conditions. With atoms, replace the density expression by the corresponding signed Stieltjes integral when differentiation is justified. Likewise, the scale-family expression \(\mathbb E[Z D'(rZ)]\) needs sufficient smoothness; using an ordinary almost-everywhere derivative of an atomic CDF difference would incorrectly give zero.

For the uniform family let \(Z(r)=rD(r)-\Gamma(r)\). As a measure identity,

$$
dZ(r)=r\,dD(r).
$$

If \(D\) first increases and then decreases, \(Z\ge0\) on the increasing portion, and \(Z\) is nonincreasing on the decreasing portion. Hence \(\Omega'=Z/r^2\) changes sign at most once from positive to negative, allowing flat segments. Since finite class means imply \(\int_0^\infty D<\infty\), a nonzero unimodal \(D\) eventually produces a negative \(Z\): its decreasing tail has \(rD(r)\to0\), while \(\Gamma(r)\) approaches a positive mean gap. This establishes weak single-peakedness.

Likelihood-ratio-ordered densities give the required single-crossing density difference. But weak MLRP can include equal classes or flat portions, so it does not on its own justify every strict inequality, a unique peak, or \(D'<0\) at every root. With distinct continuous classes and a strictly decreasing positive part of \(D\) after its density crossing, the unique peak occurs above that crossing and solves \(D(r)=\Gamma(r)/r\). For non-smooth classes the peak can instead occur at a downward jump of \(Z\), without any root of that equality.

For point values \(0<p<\ell<h\), direct evaluation gives

$$
\Omega(r)=
\begin{cases}
0,&r\le\ell,\\
1-\ell/r,&\ell<r\le h,\\
(h-\ell)/r,&r\ge h.
\end{cases}
$$

Thus the benchmark's unique positive peak is at \(r=h=10\), where \(\Omega=.9\) and the full-order gap is \(0.415905442\). At \(r=12\), \(\Omega=.75\); at \(r=20\), \(\Omega=.45\) and the gap is \(0.207952721\). Its rise is strict on \((\ell,h)\), not literally on every \(r<h\), since it is zero below \(\ell\).

At the peak,

$$
\Omega'_-(10)=.01,\qquad \Omega'_+(10)=-.09,
\qquad D(10)=0\ne\Gamma(10)/10=.9.
$$

So Solver B cannot use the displayed smooth equality as the characterization of the atomic benchmark peak. Solver E's truncated-moment formula with \(\theta<10\) produces the left numerator \(1\), while the right numerator is \(1-10=-9\). Solver C's right-derivative statement is the correct version at the kink.

**Counterexample to a general hump under ranking alone.** Take

$$
G_L=\tfrac12\delta_1+\tfrac12\delta_5,\qquad
G_H=\tfrac12\delta_2+\tfrac12\delta_{10},\qquad F_r=U[0,r].
$$

The classes are ranked. Here \(D=1/2\) on \([1,2)\) and \([5,10)\), and zero elsewhere. Its running average has a local peak \(\Omega(2)=.25\), then a trough \(\Omega(5)=.10\), then another peak \(\Omega(10)=.30\), then falls. With \(\pi=\rho=1/2\), \(\lambda=\beta=1/4\), \(c_H=100\), and \(k=.001\), entry is always \(\rho\) and full orders are uniquely optimal for all \(r\ge2\): at \(r=2\), \(\Delta=.125\), so the universal correct-order bound is \(.5\times.3\times.125=.01875>k\), and \(\Delta\) subsequently rises. The order gap therefore has the same two peaks, with values \(.10,.04,.12\). Solver E's introductory inference “zero at both extremes, so a hump” is invalid without its later single-peaked-D condition.

The general endpoint statements themselves are correct: \(F(p)=1\) implies \(\Omega=0\); if \(F_s(y)\to0\) at every finite \(y\), dominated convergence gives \(W_j\to0\); and for uniform incumbents \(\Omega(r)\le(\mathbb E_H\theta-\mathbb E_L\theta)/r\to0\). These imply eventual small gaps, not a single turning point.

## Solver E 2: the relative-risk trend conditions

**Verdict: proved with the stated conditions. Economically useful, but a more demanding alternative to the general sign theorem.**

For uniform incumbents, define the capped class mean

$$
C_j(r)=\mathbb E_j[\min(\theta,r)]=\int_0^rS_j(y)\,dy.
$$

Then \(W_j=C_j/r\), \(Q=C_H/C_L\), and, at continuity points,

$$
\frac{d\log Q}{dr}
=\frac{S_H(r)}{C_H(r)}-\frac{S_L(r)}{C_L(r)}
=\frac{\varepsilon_H(r)-\varepsilon_L(r)}r,
\quad \varepsilon_j(r)=\frac{rS_j(r)}{C_j(r)}.
$$

If \(S_H/S_L\) is nondecreasing, then

$$
S_H(r)C_L(r)-S_L(r)C_H(r)
=\int_0^r[S_H(r)S_L(y)-S_L(r)S_H(y)]\,dy\ge0.
$$

This cross-product proof also covers zero survival probabilities without dividing by zero. It proves \(Q\), and hence RR, nondecreasing. Since every value is at least \(p\), \(Q=1\) for \(r\le p\); finite means give the limit \(Q\to\mathbb E_H\theta/\mathbb E_L\theta\). If H has a finite upper support bound, ranking bounds L's support as well and the ratio is constant once \(r\) exceeds that bound. These facts justify Solver E's limits and saturation statement.

For a general incumbent family, the supplied likelihood-ratio plus reverse-hazard-order sufficient condition is correct. Relative to a common dominating measure for the class laws,

$$
\begin{aligned}
&W_H(u)W_L(t)-W_H(t)W_L(u)\\
&=\frac12\iint[\psi_H(a)\psi_L(b)-\psi_H(b)\psi_L(a)]
[F_u(a)F_t(b)-F_t(a)F_u(b)]\,da\,db.
\end{aligned}
$$

For \(a<b\), both brackets are nonpositive under the stated orders; for \(a>b\), both are nonnegative. Their product integrates to a nonnegative number. Division by positive \(W_L(u)W_L(t)\) proves the ratio comparison. The cross-product formulation handles atoms and zero CDF values. For translated CDFs, log-concavity gives

$$
\partial_y\log\frac{F_0(y-u)}{F_0(y-t)}
=(\log F_0)'(y-u)-(\log F_0)'(y-t)\ge0
\quad(u>t),
$$

with the corresponding monotone-ratio interpretation at nonsmooth points. The uniform scale family also satisfies this CDF-ratio order.

**Why the extra restrictions cannot be omitted.** Set

$$
G_L=\tfrac12\delta_1+\tfrac12\delta_{10},\qquad
G_H=\tfrac12\delta_2+\tfrac12\delta_{10}.
$$

These classes satisfy FOSD. For \(2\le r\le10\),

$$
Q(r)=\frac{r+2}{r+1},
$$

which falls strictly. At the slot \(M=.7,m=.3\), RR falls from \(1.121212121\) at \(r=2\) to \(1.075471698\) at \(r=4\). The same small-cost, high-entry-cost construction used above makes both points full-order equilibria. Thus class ranking alone does not rescue the ratio even for uniform incumbents.

Likewise, class likelihood-ratio order alone is insufficient for an arbitrary FOSD incumbent path. With point values 1 and 2, let \(F_0\) put masses .1 and .9 on uniform windows \([0,.5]\) and \([1.25,1.75]\); let \(F_1\) retain .1 on the first window, reduce the second to .1, and put .8 on \([3,4]\). The second incumbent is stronger. The pairs \((W_H,W_L)\) are \((1,.1)\) and \((.2,.1)\), so RR at \(M=.7,m=.3\) falls from \(1.972972973\) to \(1.307692308\). The incumbent-family restriction in Solver E 2(c) is doing real work.

Numerical checks reproduce the benchmark RR values \(1.600978364\) at \(r=3\), \(1.707080323\) at \(r=3.6\), and the saturation value \(2.215929688<\mathrm e\) for \(r\ge10\). I also reran Solver E's saved print-only exploratory program as supplied. These are simulation counts, not necessity evidence; the proof above supplies sufficiency.

## Solver E 3: marginal proceeds and the ratio to the price spread

**Verdict: proved; retain as an interpretation or remark rather than the principal repair.**

Under class ranking the fixed-incumbent proceeds kernel is

$$
\xi(R)=\int_0^R D(y)\,dy,\qquad \Delta_T(F)=\mathbb E_F[\xi(R)].
$$

The bounded function \(D\) makes \(\xi\) Lipschitz. Its derivative equals \(D\) at every continuity point of \(D\); discontinuities are at most countable and a continuous incumbent law charges none. Therefore

$$
\Omega(F)=\mathbb E_F[\xi'(R)].
$$

For an admissible location shift \(R_t=R_0+t\), the difference quotients of \(\xi\) are bounded by one and converge almost surely. Dominated convergence gives

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\Delta_T(F_t)=\mathbb E[D(R_0+t)]=\Omega(F_t).
$$

Consequently the fixed-slot winner gap increases along such a path exactly when the spread's derivative increases, equivalently when the spread is convex on the interval. For uniform incumbents,

$$
\Delta_T(r)=\frac1r\int_0^r\Gamma(y)\,dy,
\quad [r\Delta_T(r)]'=\Gamma(r)=r\Omega(r),
$$

which proves \(\Omega=\Delta_T'+\Delta_T/r\). In the benchmark, \(\Delta_T=(r-1)^2/(2r)\); at \(r=3\), the two terms are \(4/9\) and \(2/9\), totaling \(2/3\). The monotone-function versus monotone-derivative distinction explains why the spread is generally increasing but the winner gap is not. Universal characterizations inherit the interval/endpoint qualification given above for \(D\).

Above the ceiling, with full orders and \(\Delta_T>0\), subtraction of the price and winning-probability formulas gives

$$
\frac{\Pr(W\mid1)-\Pr(W\mid-1)}{P(1)-P(-1)}
=\frac{\rho(M-m)\Omega}{\rho(M-m)\Delta_T}
=\boxed{\frac{\Omega}{\Delta_T}}.
$$

This ratio identity actually holds at any prior with fixed full orders and common entry. The covariance version \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,\mathbf1_W)=vI_-\Omega/\Delta_T\) additionally uses the symmetric-prior relation \(I_-=\rho\Delta_T\vartheta/2\). At \(r_2\), the winning-frequency difference \(.083437820061\) divided by price spread \(.108469166079\) equals \(.769230769231\). In the benchmark post-ceiling region the ratio is \(2/(r-1)\): .5 at \(r=5\), and \(2/7=.285714286\) at \(r=8\). The claimed decline is correct.

Solver E's zero-gap economy is valid. With

$$
p=\tfrac12,\quad R\sim U[2,3],\quad
G_L=\tfrac12\delta_1+\tfrac12\delta_5,\quad
G_H=\tfrac12\delta_{3/2}+\tfrac12\delta_6,
$$

both class win probabilities equal \(1/2\). The separation below all incumbent values contributes \(\Delta_T=(1/2)(1.5-1)=1/4\); separation above them contributes \(\gamma=(1/2)(6-5)=1/2\). Direct profits are \(g_L=1.25\) and \(g_H=1.75\). Thus positive proceeds and profit gaps do not imply strict winner prediction.

For a stronger demonstration that the reduced coordinates do not determine the winner gap, match those same \(\Delta_T,g_L,g_H\) with point values

$$
\ell'=\frac{15+\sqrt{331}}8=4.149175675,\quad
h'=\ell'+\tfrac34=4.899175675,\quad
r'=\tfrac34(2\ell'+\tfrac34)=6.786263512.
$$

With \(R\sim U[0,r']\) and the same reserve, \(g_j=(\theta_j^2-p^2)/(2r')\) gives exactly \(g_L=1.25\), \(g_H=1.75\), and \(\Delta_T=.75-.5=.25\), but \(\Omega=.75/r'=0.110517371\). Keeping the same preparation cost gives identical \((\Delta_T,\tau)\) and different winner gaps. With \(c_H=6\), \(\rho=1/4\), the benchmark slot and \(k=.01\), both economies have common entry \(\rho\) and unique full orders.

## Ranking and recommendation

1. **Use the general sign theorem as the proposition's main statement:** the purchase-minus-sale winner gap equals \((M-m)\mathbb E_F[D(R)]\), and is positive exactly when the incumbent sometimes falls where class membership changes the chance of winning. Pair it with the general post-ceiling covariance \(2\pi(1-\pi)\rho\lambda\mathbb E_F[D(R)]\). This is Solver C 1 / Solver B 3 / the core of Solver E 1. It is exact, economically interpretable, and needs no new distributional restriction.
2. **Retain a strength trend under the shared outbidding condition**, with strictness stated correctly: if H always beats the incumbent, strengthening widens the full-order gap exactly to the extent it takes wins from L. This aligns naturally with the repair to research-branch ownership. For the uniform-family discussion, state the sharper necessary-and-sufficient derivative condition \(D(r)\ge\Gamma(r)/r\), which also covers the wide-class example.
3. **Add the winner second difference as a separate empirical implication**, retaining prior one-half, full orders, and purchase-only recruitment. Its positive sign detects recruitment within this model. Do not describe its magnitude as independent of the auction.
4. **Keep the relative-risk trend as an optional extension.** It is correct under hazard-rate order for uniform incumbents, or the stronger pair of class and incumbent orders for general families. It needs more assumptions and can be constant while the economically relevant absolute frequency difference collapses.
5. **Use the marginal-spread identity as interpretation**, not as the main repair. It explains the distinction between increasing proceeds differences and an unsigned winner-gap trend, while adding no general monotonicity of its own.

Suggested replacement theorem, in substance:

> Under the class ranking and full two-sided trade, purchases predict a higher prepared-challenger winning probability than sales exactly when the incumbent has positive probability of a value at which the two classes differ in their chance of beating it. This prediction remains after entry has returned to its floor. With the prior and slot fixed, the winner gap weakly widens as the incumbent strengthens if the high class always outbids it; the widening is strict when the strengthening reduces the low class's winning probability.

Then put the exact uniform condition and benchmark hump in the following remark, not in an unrestricted strength theorem.

## What remains weak after the best workaround

The robust sign is the central empirical association, but it is a sorting prediction shared with models in which trading never recruits anyone. The covariance decomposition and conditional winner second difference carry the additional recruitment content. Neither establishes causal identification in heterogeneous observational samples without the model's information, timing, independence, and order-pattern assumptions.

A rising absolute gap is genuinely non-general: it can fall during active recruitment, not only when the incumbent is so strong that recruitment has already ceased. A single hump is likewise not general. High-class outbidding is a useful economic restriction rather than a necessary one, and it deliberately excludes some strong-incumbent environments.

Finally, the factor \(M-m\) is constant only when the slot and prior are fixed. With endogenous research intensity and full orders at each compared point, its movement must be included:

$$
\frac{d}{ds}(w_1-w_{-1})
=(M-m)'\Omega+(M-m)\Omega'.
$$

Outside full orders, replace \(M-m\) by the actual posterior difference \(\mu(1)-\mu(-1)\); the same product decomposition applies, but the posterior bounds need not be attained.

Solver E's sufficient full-order condition \(\rho\kappa(\pi)\Delta_T>k\) does form an up-set on a FOSD path with the slot, costs, and prior fixed: \(\Delta_T\) weakly increases, and the trading lemma bounds both correct-order returns below by this expression. That is a useful sufficient domain for the fixed-posterior comparison, not a claim that equilibrium research intensity stays constant or that this bound is necessary for full trade.

Nothing here changes the two-point preparation cost. The benchmark registry values are correct; the substantive repair concerns theorem scope, strictness, and the interpretation of the additional empirical statistic, plus correction of the earlier Solver B numerical illustrations.
