# Workarounds for the five weaknesses: what the paper can claim

## Summary for the author

Every printed proposition holds for the benchmark it is stated for. Most reach further; each sharp claim needs its own condition.

In general, no trade and an informative equilibrium coexist exactly inside a fixed region drawn in two numbers: the extra proceeds a strong bidder brings, and the belief a high-cost challenger needs to prepare. A stronger incumbent raises both, so the economy can pass in and out of the region; entry never falls as the first number grows. At an even prior, when an indifferent investor may randomize, research, entry and strong challengers' preparation rise with the first number, and the weak challenger's selling does not stop them. When the informed investor trades both ways, purchases predict a winning challenger more often than sales do, even after orders stop predicting entry, provided the two kinds of challenger sometimes differ in their chance of beating the incumbent. Weighting price impacts by order frequency gives a recruitment test at any prior. Order counts identify the informed investor at a known prior other than one half. An announced preparation earns a positive average return exactly when preparation was in doubt.

The conditions are these. When the strong challenger outbids every incumbent, high-class ownership rises with incumbent strength and the winner gap between purchases and sales widens. Coexistence holds on one interval of strengths when a uniform incumbent's top value stays between the weak and strong values, and the strong challenger's average value exceeds the highest such top value by at least the preparation cost. Cheap research keeps recruiting up to its cap. The entry part of the price gap rises when the reserve is low. The interval condition caps the preparation cost and a strong form of outbidding sets a floor; the benchmark's cost lies between them.

The paper concedes that high-class takeovers can become rarer as the incumbent strengthens and beats strong challengers more often. Along an arbitrary incumbent path, coexistence can break into pieces and research can stop recruiting early. If an indifferent investor abstains instead, the research result fails on part of the benchmark path. Off an even prior the research results are unproved. The order-based results need two-sided trading and one public state, not pooled deals.

In the benchmark, recruiting at the research cap now lasts until the entry ceiling, so the research section's narrative and figure change.

## W1. Coexistence on an interval of strengths

The paper says no trade and an informative equilibrium coexist on an interval of strengths that starts where J = Δ(1 − τ), the most a recruiting purchase can earn, reaches the trading cost k. J is a race: a stronger incumbent widens the spread Δ but also raises the threshold τ. So J can fall, and the coexistence set can split into pieces.

The repair states coexistence where it lives, in the plane of the spread and the threshold. Hold the prior and the slot fixed. No trade and an informative equilibrium coexist exactly when two things hold. First, the threshold lies above the prior and within reach of a purchase. Second, the spread lies in a band: wide enough that a recruiting purchase covers its trading cost, and narrow enough that an uninformed order does not pay (appendix W1.1). The region is convex and does not depend on the incumbent. A stronger incumbent widens the spread and, wherever the threshold is positive (as it is throughout the region), raises the threshold, so the economy can enter the region, leave it and return. Inside the region the informative equilibrium is unique when an indifferent challenger prepares. When the challenger may prepare with any probability, a threshold equilibrium can join it, as the manuscript already records (`appendix/A4_coexistence.tex:357`). Entry on the informative equilibrium depends on the spread alone. It never falls with the spread, and it rises strictly until the investor buys for sure. So any two coexisting strengths can be ranked by entry, even when coexistence fails between them. This ranks informative equilibria; it says nothing about which equilibrium is played.

Two facts tie the region back to strength. First, the stretch of coexistence where the investor buys for sure is always one interval of strengths, along any path. There a recruiting purchase pays even at the top posterior, and the remaining conditions move monotonically with strength. So any gap lies at spreads too narrow for a sure purchase to pay. Second, one economic condition restores the whole interval. Take a uniform incumbent whose upper bound, along the path, lies above every low-class value and below every high-class value. Suppose class H's mean value exceeds the strongest incumbent's highest value by at least the preparation cost. Then J rises strictly, and coexistence is an interval at every prior. For point masses the condition reads r_2 + c_H ≤ h, and the benchmark meets it with 10 − 3.6 − 6 = 0.4 to spare. A weaker sufficient bound lifts the benchmark's cost ceiling from 6.4 to 6.865. The proof takes a few lines, covers dispersed classes, and replaces the polynomial root count behind the rise of J. For general incumbent families a balance condition does the same job. J cannot fall while class H's net margin over the preparation cost stays positive and the spread times that margin does not fall: the spread must grow proportionally at least as fast as the margin shrinks. A squared version of the balance condition replaces the second root count. It shows that Δ(1 − τ)², which sets entry on the threshold branch when an indifferent challenger may prepare with any probability, rises on the whole benchmark path, not only up to r = 2. The threshold order and the boundary claims keep their existing arguments.

The main text would say:

> No trade and an informative equilibrium coexist exactly when the preparation threshold lies above the prior but within reach of a purchase, and the spread is wide enough for a recruiting purchase to cover its trading cost yet too narrow for an uninformed order to pay. A stronger incumbent widens the spread and, once the threshold is positive, raises it too, so the economy can enter this region, leave it and return. The strengths at which coexistence holds with a sure purchase always form one interval, any gap lies at spreads too narrow for a sure purchase to pay, and entry on the informative equilibrium rises with the spread until purchases are certain. With a uniform incumbent whose upper bound lies above every low-class value and below every high-class value, coexistence holds on one interval of strengths whenever class H's mean value exceeds the strongest incumbent's highest value by at least the preparation cost.

What remains weak is the interval along an arbitrary path. Solver A built an exact two-piece case that keeps every benchmark primitive and changes only the incumbent family. The incumbent is a two-part mixture. It first strengthens only where it loses to both classes. That cuts both classes' auction profits without widening the spread, so τ rises and the recruiting purchase stops paying. It then strengthens between the class values; the spread widens and coexistence returns. Along that path coexistence holds on [0, 0.483] ∪ [1.150, 2]. Class H wins for sure and covers c_H against the strongest incumbent throughout, with 2.19 to spare, so those two conditions alone do not protect arbitrary families; the uniform family does real work. The path is built to isolate the two forces, so it shows a possibility, not a typical case. The original counterexample (c_H = 2, narrow shifted uniforms) only moves the endpoint. J is single-peaked in that family, so the strengths at which it clears any given trading cost form one interval. Solver B puts the trading cost that coexistence needs there at about thirty times the benchmark's or more; the referee's own coexistence sets in that family use trading costs of 1.5 and 2, that is 75 and 100 times the benchmark's. The endpoints r_A and r_N and the four prior cases of Corollary A.2 still need their own crossing and attainment conditions; an interval proof does not supply them. The prior ceiling π < 1/(1 + ρ), which is 0.8 at ρ = 1/4, is necessary for coexistence. It is not the benchmark's actual maximum prior, 0.650. For the economics this matters little. It matters for the words "coexist on an interval of strengths".

## W2. Ownership by the high class

High-class ownership is a product, O_H = π·e_H·W_H(F): the prior, the chance that a class-H challenger prepares, and its chance of beating the incumbent once prepared. Research raises e_H, but a stronger incumbent lowers W_H. Proposition 4(a) uses W_H = 1. Without it, ownership can fall while research and entry rise. In the referee's example the uniform incumbent's upper bound lies above the high value (r from 11 to 12, h = 10); research rises 6.7 percent and total entry 1.0 percent, while ownership falls 6.4 percent.

The repair separates the two channels. Information moves only e_H. The incumbent's own bidding moves only W_H. At prior 1/2, where the study proves the research results, two statements then hold with no condition on the auction. On the research branch, a wider spread raises research, total entry and class-H entry, and lowers class-L entry, so the pool of prepared challengers improves. And, holding the incumbent's realized value fixed, the chance that class H acquires the target rises with the spread wherever class H can win at that value. The second statement concerns a value nobody observes. It holds at almost every value; where a class has an atom, the auction's tie rule decides it, and outside the incumbent's support it is a counterfactual. One remark completes the picture. At every point of the research branch, a zero-research equilibrium coexists against the same incumbent, and ownership on the research branch divided by ownership there equals e_H/ρ. W_H cancels, so this ratio says no more than that class-H entry rises above its floor. It is not a level prediction, and the difference between the two ownership levels can still fall.

The level claim returns under one plain condition: class H outbids every incumbent on the branch. Then W_H = 1 and ownership is π·e_H. A condition on values and costs alone covers every economy in the benchmark family, whatever the strength. Call it decisive advantage: wherever preparing at cost c_H pays for a challenger known to be class H, that challenger beats every incumbent value. In words, preparation is costly enough that class H prepares only against incumbents it is sure to beat. Any order that recruits needs class H's gross profit above c_H, so under decisive advantage every economy in which some order recruits has W_H = 1. For point masses and the whole uniform family the condition reads c_H ≥ (h² − p²)/(2h), which is 4.9875 at the benchmark. With c_H = 6 the margin is 1.0125: no benchmark-family economy recruits at r ≥ 7.97, below h, whatever the slot, trading cost, ρ, prior, cap or research cost. In general, ownership rises from one incumbent law to a stronger one exactly when the proportional loss in class H's win probability is smaller than the proportional gain in class-H entry (appendix W2.5).

The main text would say:

> Research brings class H to the auction more often. At prior one half, on the research branch, a wider spread raises research, entry and class-H entry and lowers class-L entry, so the challengers who prepare are stronger on average. Holding the incumbent's realized value fixed, it also raises the chance that class H acquires the target, wherever class H can win at that value. High-class ownership itself rises with incumbent strength when class H outbids every incumbent on the branch, as it does in the benchmark.

What remains weak is the number of high-class takeovers across incumbents. No ratio predicts that level, and the statement at a fixed incumbent value conditions on a value nobody observes, so it is not an observable trend. Decisive advantage is sufficient, not necessary. It is a property of the uniform family, not of the benchmark values: a two-part incumbent with h, ℓ, p and c_H at benchmark values lowers ownership by 0.39 percent. The same-incumbent comparison says nothing about which equilibrium is played. When the incumbent can win, it wins only when it values the target more, so the fall in the level is not a misallocation; nothing here ranks the change in welfare. On size, the risk is small near the benchmark and large away from it. With point-mass classes, the branch's own condition that a low-class sale does not pay caps class H's chance of losing at 1.78 percent at benchmark costs, against a 13 percent rise in class-H entry across the benchmark branch. Above h the balance reverses. There class H's win probability falls in inverse proportion to the incumbent's upper bound (W_H = h/r), while class-H entry responds weakly: its proportional response to the spread is below 2(1 − √ρ)/(1 + √ρ), which is 2/3 at ρ = 1/4, for any quadratic research cost. With uniform incumbents, point classes, ρ = 1/4 and h ≥ 5ℓ, ownership falls at every strength above h while research is below its cap. the referee's counterexample sets the preparation cost at a quarter of the high value and the trading cost at two percent of it; no calibration says whether such costs are typical. Within the uniform family its essential features are r > h and c_H below 4.9875.

The other ownership results follow the same factorization. Proposition 2 already states the exact condition, and decisive advantage makes it automatic. The zero-research comparison does not carry over to Proposition 2, because its hypotheses exclude no trade at the strong incumbent. A.4 has the same trade-off. A.5 constructs its ownership inequality directly and should not be narrowed to decisive advantage. A.7 generalizes with ownership ½·e_H·W_H(F_1).

Solver A, Solver F and both Solver B files found the same-incumbent ratio; Solver B the statement at a fixed incumbent value, decisive advantage and the ceiling on how strongly class-H entry responds to the spread. the referee proved these, extended that ceiling to every ρ, and refuted Solver F's hazard-order conjecture, Solver F's "if and only if" for the takeover share, and Solver G's strict rise from a weak bound. the referee ranked the ratio first among the unconditional statements; this revision keeps it as a remark (see the last section).

## W3. Where the research branch ends

The paper's research branch has the low class abstaining, so it must stop where a low-class sale starts to pay, at ρΔ = 2k. The paper says the benchmark branch [r_R, r̄_R) ends where research reaches its cap. That needs two things. The cap must come before selling, an inequality in the slot, the costs and ρ alone (appendix W3.7), and the recruitment margin must cross zero once, upward. the referee's economy (ρ = 1/2, cheap research) fails the first: the abstaining branch ends at r = 5.83, before the cap at 7.28.

The repair lets the low class sell, and the branch carries on. Fix the prior at 1/2, and let an indifferent investor mix, as the paper's default rule does. The low class at first sells only as often as keeps its sale break-even. Its selling pushes the posterior after a sale down to the point where the sale earns nothing. An indifferent seller adds nothing to the value of research, and a sale never recruits. So research, the purchase posterior and entry follow the same functions of the spread as before. Once even a sure sale pays, its profit becomes a second return to research, and the research schedule kinks upward at that point. One equation covers both pieces, capped at the research cap (appendix W3.2). Entry depends on research alone, whatever the low class does. The equilibrium exists exactly when the spread exceeds twice the trading cost and the top posterior that its research produces still reaches a threshold above 1/2. Reaching the cap does not end it: the capped part survives while the threshold stays within reach of the top posterior at the cap. In the referee's economy the recruiting equilibrium runs from r = 4.03 through the first sale at 5.83 and the cap at 7.28 to r = 8.91, where τ reaches the top posterior at the cap, 0.8125. Entry rises from 0.608 to 0.66.

Neither economy shows the second return, because in both research reaches its cap before a sale pays outright. In the benchmark the cap comes at r = 1.678 and a sure sale pays only from r = 2.124; in the referee's economy the low class still mixes at the ceiling. Solver D's example shows it: with c_H = 5, ρ = 1/2, k = 0.02, a = 0.1 and the benchmark slot, the low class sells for sure from r = 1.541, before the cap at 2.042, and the slope of research in the spread jumps there from 1.56 to 2.06.

The same work completes the map of equilibria with research, when an indifferent investor may mix and an indifferent challenger prepares. There are three outcomes. Zero research exists while an informed order at the prior would not pay its trading cost (ρΔ ≤ 2k). Full recruitment is the branch above. The third is new: research at floor entry. Both classes trade after a successful draw, no order recruits, and entry stays at ρ. It exists once an informed order at the prior would pay and the threshold lies above the top posterior its research produces. At the benchmark this fills the gray band of the research figure. Zero research and the research branch coexist from r_R to r_N. Capped recruiting and floor-entry research coexist on (r_N, 2.167). Capped recruiting is alone from 2.167 to the ceiling r_C = 3.593, and floor-entry research is alone above r_C. Uniqueness starts at 2.167, below r_U = 2.434, where the paper's sufficient condition U begins.

The main text would say:

> At an even prior, and when an indifferent investor may mix, the equilibrium in which a researched purchase recruits has research and entry rising with the spread until research reaches its cap, and the low class's selling does not interrupt them. At first the low class sells only as often as keeps its sale break-even, which leaves the value of research unchanged; once a sale pays outright, its profit adds a second return to research, although in the benchmark research reaches its cap first. The equilibrium exists exactly where the top posterior that research produces still reaches the preparation threshold, so along a path of incumbent strengths research reaches its cap only while that condition keeps holding. If an indifferent investor abstains instead, the equilibrium disappears wherever the low class would mix, which on the benchmark path runs from the first sale to the strength at which the low class sells for sure.

What remains weak is the path. The equilibrium exists strength by strength, and a stronger incumbent can carry the economy out of the recruiting set and back. Solver A's exact example shows this with J rising, class H always winning, no selling and no binding cap: the recruitment margin crosses zero twice. So "research rises from r_R to the cap" along a path needs the recruitment margin to stay positive. A sufficient condition is research cheap enough that the recruitment margin keeps rising, with the margin negative at the start of the path and positive at the cap. The benchmark's research cost is 0.025 against the paper's bound of 0.162, and that bound is itself conservative by a factor of about three. The continuation through mixed selling needs an investor who may mix. If an indifferent investor abstains, full recruitment drops out where the low class would mix, at the benchmark on (1.748, 2.124]. There only floor-entry research remains when an indifferent challenger prepares. When the challenger may prepare with any probability, a threshold equilibrium with partial preparation also survives (at r = 2: research 0.224, entry after a purchase 0.36, overall entry 0.287). The research results are proved at prior 1/2 only. Ownership on the branch still carries W_H (see W2). The old cap-before-selling inequality is exact for the abstaining branch, but the benchmark meets it with a margin of only 0.0016. Raising ρ from 0.25 to 0.30 breaks it, and so does a above 0.0343, which covers 16 percent of Proposition A.2's admissible cost window. That is why it should not define the general endpoint. For the economics this matters little: what broke was a description of the low class's order, not the joint rise of research and entry.

Solver A, Solver B and Solver D built the same continuation, and Solver B and Solver D mapped the three outcomes. the referee proved both, ranked Solver A's statement first, and refuted Solver B's claims that the first sale lies inside the branch, that the coexistence band is never empty and that nothing recruits at the critical cost a_max = 0.408, and Solver D's "no condition beyond the start".

## W4. Orders predict the winner

Proposition A.13 says the gap between the prepared challenger's win probability after a purchase and after a sale rises with strength. In general the gap is (M − m)(W_H − W_L), and W_H − W_L = E_F[D(R)], the average class difference in the chance of beating the incumbent. That average can fall. With class values 1 and 2 and R ~ U[0, r], r > 2, it is 1/r.

The repair states the sign, which is general. Under full two-sided trade the gap equals (M − m)·E_F[D(R)]. It is positive exactly when the incumbent's value sometimes falls where the two classes differ in their chance of beating it. The factor E_F[D(R)] has a plain reading. Pair a low-class and a high-class challenger at the same rank within their classes; the factor is the chance that the incumbent's value falls between their two values. The pair is a counterfactual device, not two actual bidders. Above the entry ceiling, the covariance between the order and a challenger win is 2π(1 − π)ρλ·E_F[D(R)] at every prior, while the covariance between the order and entry is zero. Orders keep predicting the winner after they stop predicting entry, and that needs no restriction on the incumbent family.

The trend returns under the condition that also restores ownership in W2. Hold the prior and the slot fixed. If class H outbids every incumbent, the gap is (M − m)(1 − W_L), which widens as the incumbent takes wins from the low class. For uniform incumbents the exact condition is that class separation at the incumbent's top value r is at least its average over [0, r] (appendix W4.3). It holds at every strength the paper reports for its example economies, including the wide classes, whose gap peaks at √37 = 6.08, above their top strength 5.5. Winners also give a recruitment test, at prior 1/2 with full orders and entry at its floor after a sale and after no order. Take how much a purchase raises the challenger's win rate relative to no order, minus how much a sale lowers it. That difference is positive exactly when a purchase recruits, and zero otherwise. Its size depends on the auction (appendix W4.2).

The main text would say:

> When the investor trades in both directions, purchases predict a higher chance that the prepared challenger wins than sales do, exactly when the incumbent's value sometimes falls where the two classes differ in their chance of beating it. The prediction survives above the entry ceiling, where orders no longer predict entry. With the prior and slot fixed, when class H outbids every incumbent, the gap widens as a stronger incumbent takes wins from the low class.

What remains weak is the trend, which is not general. The gap can fall while purchases still recruit: with benchmark values but c_H = 3, the recruiting window (8.06, 12.19] straddles the gap's peak at r = h = 10. With the benchmark's own c_H = 6, recruitment ends at r_C = 3.59, so above h the gap falls only after recruitment has stopped. A single peak is not general either; two-point classes can produce two. The benchmark's peak at r = h is a kink, because the class difference jumps there: E_F[D(R)] has slope 0.01 from the left and −0.09 from the right, and the smooth peak equation does not hold. The sign is shared with models in which trading recruits nobody. Only the part of the covariance that runs through entry, and the comparison of the purchase effect with the sale effect, speak to recruitment. The factor M − m is fixed only with the slot and prior fixed; with endogenous research it moves too. Strictness needs distinct posteriors: at r_0 nobody trades, and the order gap is zero although E_F[D(R)] = 1/6. For the economics this matters little. An empiricist tests the sign and the covariance above the ceiling.

Solver C, Solver E and the earlier Solver B candidate all found the sign theorem and the outbidding trend; Solver C added the covariance at every prior, Solver E the two-channel covariance and the winner second difference. the referee proved these and refuted the Solver B candidate's lognormal numbers (its ceiling of about 19 is really 25.07) and Solver E's single-hump inference.

## W5. Tests that rely on the symmetric prior

Three empirical results use prior 1/2. A.11's test, "the purchase impact exceeds the sale impact exactly when a purchase recruits", relies on the two posterior moves being equal, M − ½ = ½ − m. At any prior below 1/2, every full-trade equilibrium without recruitment gives it a false positive. A.12's finding that order counts cannot identify the informed share holds only at 1/2. A.14's announcement signs need a positivity condition. A separate issue: A.11's rising entry component needs the entry benefit at the top posterior, A_F(M), to rise, which stochastic dominance does not give.

The repair for the impact test weights each impact by how often its order occurs. Under full two-sided trade, Bayes' rule makes the frequency-weighted posterior moves equal, so weighted class news cancels at every prior. What is left is the price effect of changes in preparation. The weighted difference equals the average price after an opportunity minus the price after no order. When the prior alone does not recruit, scale the sale impact by the ratio of sale to purchase frequencies and subtract it from the purchase impact. The result is the entry gain after a purchase times the entry benefit at the top posterior, positive exactly when a purchase recruits (appendix W5.1). The test needs prices and order frequencies, both already in the design, and no auction objects. It is the same moment as A.14(iv), the price drop after no order, so the paper can state it once. A regime the paper never meets appears at priors that clear the preparation threshold on their own, which on the benchmark path means priors of at least 0.633: a sale deters preparation, and the statistic turns negative.

For identification, what matters is what the econometrician knows about the prior. Under full two-sided trade with a known prior other than 1/2, counts identify the informed presence directly, and with it the slot; prices then identify ρΔ. Near 1/2 the count estimate is noisy: its variance carries the factor 1/(2π − 1)², which is 100 at π = 0.55. With an unknown prior, a more lopsided prior with a rarer informed investor produces the same counts. Before the investor's trading incentives are imposed, the parameters that match the counts form a curve. Where those incentive constraints are slack, a piece of the curve survives them and presence stays unidentified locally. Where they bind, they can cut the curve to a single point, as in the referee's example at π = 0.6, λ = 0.4, β = 1/3. The sale-price moment resolves the confound when entry after a sale and after no order sits at its floor and ρΔ is known; it then gives λ and π together. Nonidentification at a known prior is the knife-edge case at 1/2.

For announcements, the mean return to a preparation report is positive exactly when preparation is still uncertain after some order. This holds at every prior and in every equilibrium. A sufficient condition on primitives is that the prior alone does not recruit a high-cost challenger; the benchmark clears it by at least 1.196.

For the entry-component trend, entry helps the seller in two ways. It rescues a sale when the incumbent misses the reserve, worth the reserve times the chance of a miss, and it raises the price through competitive bidding. A stronger incumbent shrinks the first and enlarges the second. The competitive part always rises under stochastic dominance, by at least M times the rise in the spread. The total rises when the chance that the incumbent misses the reserve stays the same. With a uniform incumbent it also rises when the reserve is below 1/√3 of the lowest challenger value and of the smallest incumbent upper bound; the benchmark has ℓ² − 3p² = 1/4.

The main text would say:

> When the investor trades in both directions, the purchase impact exceeds the sale impact, once each is weighted by how often its order occurs, exactly when a purchase recruits a challenger, at any prior below the preparation threshold; the weighted difference equals the price discount when an opportunity passes without an order. Order counts then identify the informed investor's presence whenever the prior is known and differs from one half, although the estimate is noisy near one half. At one half, or when the prior is unknown, the sale price identifies it, provided entry after a sale and after no order sits at its floor and the payoff scale is known. Preparation announcements earn a positive mean return exactly when preparation was still uncertain. As the incumbent strengthens, entry adds more to the price through competition and less by rescuing a sale the incumbent alone would not complete, so the entry part of the price gap rises when the incumbent's chance of missing the reserve stays fixed, or, with a uniform incumbent, when the reserve is low.

What remains weak is the domain of the weighted test. It needs full two-sided informed trade. With purchases only it can give a false positive: at π = 0.3 and r = 2 there is an equilibrium with no recruitment and a positive statistic. It needs one target at one strength, not a pool of deals, because pooling across states breaks it. When the prior equals the threshold and an indifferent challenger may prepare with any probability, recruitment and deterrence can cancel. The price inversion needs that entry after a sale and after no order sits at its floor and that ρΔ is known, and a strictly mixed purchases-only branch leaves presence unidentified even with prices. The entry-component trend fails under plain stochastic dominance even at prior 1/2 with every benchmark cost. On an incumbent path whose low part crosses the reserve, the entry component, the purchase impact and the total spread all fall. A positive announcement mean shows that preparation was uncertain, not that prices recruited. On size, the unweighted test's bias is 45 percent of the sale impact at π = 0.3. At π = 0.468, the bottom of the benchmark's own prior band (98.7 percent of which lies below 1/2), it is 6.1 percent. Against a recruitment signal it is at most about 3.3 percent on the benchmark's full-trade domain. A large sample would still reject falsely, and the weighting removes the bias at no cost. The unweighted test stays valid as a corollary when π ≥ 1/2 and ρ ≤ 1/2 and an indifferent challenger prepares; exact symmetry is not necessary.

## Conditions, one per result

The sharp claims do not share one condition. Five claims keep a named condition, and they use four different ones. The rewrite should promise one condition per result and say that they differ.
