# W4-winner-gap: Solver E

Notation. The paper's, plus a few abbreviations local to this note. θ_j ~ G_j is a class-j value, D = G_L − G_H ≥ 0, S_j = 1 − G_j. W_j(F) = E_j[F(θ)] = Pr(θ_j > R); under the class ranking every value is at least p, so the indicator in the manuscript's definition (02_model.tex:228) is redundant. **piv(F) := W_H(F) − W_L(F)** is the class pivotality of the incumbent. w_x = Pr(W | X = x, A). ϑ = M − m (prior 1/2: u/(2+u)), f̄ = b + λ/2, χ_r = 1{B_F(M) ≥ c_H}, e_+ = ρ + (1−ρ)χ_r, Γ(r) = ∫_p^r D, Γ̄ = E_Hθ − E_Lθ, μ_j = E_jθ. "Full orders" means full two-sided trade. A path is F_t with t increasing in strength; the uniform path is F_r = U[0,r].

## Candidate 1: The winner gap is the informed share times the chance that the incumbent splits the classes

Angle: both. The reformulation (a)–(e) holds with no extra condition beyond A.13's own standing hypotheses (prior 1/2 and full orders for (c) and (d), and purchase-only recruitment, τ > 1/2, which A8 line 9 already assumes). Part (f) gives the exact and the sufficient primitive conditions for the original trend claim.

Intuition: Among prepared challengers an order changes only the class posterior, so the winner gap is how far the order moves the posterior times how much the class matters against this incumbent. The second factor is the probability that the incumbent's value falls between two same-quantile challengers of the two classes. It is zero against an incumbent that every challenger beats, zero against one that none beats, and positive in between, so it is a hump in strength. The benchmark ordering r < h (eq:order, 02_model.tex:82) puts the paper on the rising side. The bold sentence for the text: orders predict the winner exactly as far as the incumbent can tell the two classes apart.

Formal statement. Take the class ranking, a continuous F, any prior, slot and equilibrium (with or without research).

- (a) *Pivotality.* piv(F) = E_F[D(R)] ∈ [0,1]. If θ_L ≤ θ_H are drawn at the same quantile of their classes (a device for reading the formula, not a model assumption), piv(F) = Pr(θ_L < R < θ_H). So piv(F) > 0 iff Pr_F(D(R) > 0) > 0: the incumbent's value lands, with positive probability, where the classes differ in their chance of exceeding it.
- (b) *Slope.* For every order x: w_x = W_L(F) + μ(x)·piv(F), and Pr(W | X = x) = e(x)·w_x. Hence w_x − w_x' = [μ(x) − μ(x')]·piv(F), which is ≥ 0 when μ(x) ≥ μ(x') and > 0 iff piv(F) > 0. Under full orders at prior π, w_1 − w_{−1} = [M(π) − m(π)]·piv(F); at prior 1/2 it is ϑ·piv(F). The ratio of the order-conditioned win gap to the class-conditioned win gap is therefore ϑ, whatever F, the classes, ρ and c_H.
- (c) *Two channels (prior 1/2, full orders).* Cov(X,1_W) = f̄·[ρϑ·piv(F) + (1−ρ)χ_r·w_1] = (ρλ/2)·piv(F) + f̄(1−ρ)χ_r·w_1, with w_1 = W_L + M·piv(F). Above the ceiling (χ_r = 0), Cov(X,1_W) = (ρλ/2)·piv(F), which is > 0 iff piv(F) > 0. The post-ceiling covariance claim of A.13(iii) is therefore sharp: it holds exactly when the incumbent can split the classes. The liquidity mass b drops out.
- (d) *Winner impacts (prior 1/2, full orders).* [Pr(W|1) − Pr(W|0)] − [Pr(W|0) − Pr(W|−1)] = (1−ρ)χ_r·w_1. This is > 0 iff a purchase recruits (χ_r = 1 forces w_1 ≥ M·W_H > 0), and it does not involve piv(F). It is the winner analogue of A.11(ii) and needs no knowledge of the auction.
- (e) *Path.* The sufficient condition ρκ(π)Δ_T(F) > k for unique full orders is an up-set along every FOSD path, so on it ϑ is constant and all dependence of the gap on F runs through piv(F). Moreover piv(F) = 0 if F(p) = 1, piv(F_t) → 0 if F_t → 0 pointwise, and piv(F_r) = Γ(r)/r ≤ Γ̄/r on the uniform path. So no monotone statement can hold on a path from a weak to a dominant incumbent. If D is single-peaked, piv(F_r) is single-peaked: it rises while D(r) ≥ Γ(r)/r and falls after D crosses its running average Γ(r)/r from above (at r = h in the benchmark, where D jumps down); the peak is at or after the peak of D.
- (f) *Trend.* Let r̄ be the highest value any incumbent on the path can take.
  - (C1) *Value mass, exact on the uniform path.* piv'(r) = [E_L(θ; θ<r) − E_H(θ; θ<r)]/r². The gap rises exactly where the low class holds at least as much value below the incumbent's top as the high class. Equivalently D(r) ≥ Γ(r)/r.
  - (C2) *Density, for every FOSD pair.* D is nondecreasing on [p, r̄), that is dG_H ≤ dG_L there; in words Pr(H | θ = y) ≤ π for every value y the incumbent can reach, so a value the incumbent can reach is never good news about the class. Then piv(F_1) ≥ piv(F_0) for all continuous F_0 ≤_st F_1 supported in [0, r̄]. This is also necessary for "every FOSD pair".
  - (C3) *Outbidding.* The high class outbids every incumbent on the path, G_H(r̄) = 0. Then W_H ≡ 1 and piv(F) = 1 − W_L(F) = Pr(R > θ_L), which rises weakly under FOSD and strictly when mass moves across values that G_L charges.
  - (C3) ⟹ (C2) ⟹ (C1) on uniform paths, and both inclusions are strict. (C3) is the same condition as SCAN Decision 3 (ownership rises when W_H ≡ 1): one condition, two results.

Derivation.
- (a) θ_j is independent of R. G_j has countably many atoms and F charges none, so ties have probability zero, and W_j = Pr(R < θ_j) = E_F[1 − G_j(R)]. Subtract: piv = E_F[G_L(R) − G_H(R)] = E_F[D(R)]. G_H ≤ G_L gives the quantile coupling θ_L = G_L⁻¹(U) ≤ G_H⁻¹(U) = θ_H. Then {R < θ_L} ⊂ {R < θ_H} and piv = Pr(θ_L ≤ R < θ_H). D ≥ 0 gives piv ≥ 0, with equality iff D(R) = 0 a.s.
- (b) Entry depends on x only through the price and the cost is independent of (j, θ, R), so Pr(A | j, x) = e(x) in both classes, and Pr(H | x, A) = μ(x). Given j and A the value is still θ_j, independent of x, so Pr(W | j, x, A) = W_j. Total probability: w_x = μ(x)W_H + [1−μ(x)]W_L = W_L + μ(x)·piv. Full orders give μ(1) = M(π), μ(−1) = m(π). This holds in every equilibrium, including the purchases-only forms F and R.
- (c) At prior 1/2 with full orders, Pr(X=±1) = f̄ and E[X] = 0, so Cov(X,Y) = E[XY] = f̄(y_1 − y_{−1}) with y_x = E[Y | X=x]. For Y = 1_W, y_x = e(x)w_x, e(−1) = ρ, e(1) = e_+. Then y_1 − y_{−1} = e_+w_1 − ρw_{−1} = ρ(w_1 − w_{−1}) + (e_+ − ρ)w_1 = ρϑ·piv + (1−ρ)χ_r w_1. Since f̄ϑ = (b + λ/2)·λ/(2b+λ) = λ/2, the first term is (ρλ/2)·piv.
- (d) With posteriors (m, 1/2, M) and entry (ρ, ρ, e_+): w_1 − w_0 = (M − 1/2)·piv = (1/2 − m)·piv = w_0 − w_{−1}. The purchase impact is e_+w_1 − ρw_0 = ρ(w_1 − w_0) + (e_+ − ρ)w_1, and the sale impact is ρ(w_0 − w_{−1}). Subtract.
- (e) Lemma A.3(c): in every candidate K_H ≥ ρ(1−M)Δ_T and K_L ≥ ρmΔ_T. If ρκΔ_T > k both exceed k and Lemma A.3(b) gives unique full orders. Proposition 1(b) says Δ_T weakly rises under every strengthening, so the inequality persists. If F(p) = 1 then D(R) = 0 a.s. If F_t → 0 pointwise, W_j(F_t) = E_j[F_t(θ)] → 0 by dominated convergence. For U[0,r] and r > p: piv = (1/r)∫_p^r D = Γ(r)/r, and Γ ≤ Γ̄. Single peak: piv'(r) = Z(r)/r², where Z(r) := rD(r) − Γ(r) has dZ = r·dD. On the part where D is nondecreasing, D(r) ≥ D(y) for all y ≤ r, so D(r) ≥ Γ(r)/r and Z ≥ 0. On the part where D is nonincreasing, Z is nonincreasing, so it crosses zero at most once, from above. Hence piv' has the sign pattern +, then −.
- (f)(C1) E_j(θ; θ<r) = rG_j(r⁻) − ∫_p^r G_j (integration by parts, G_j = 0 below p). Subtract: E_L(θ; θ<r) − E_H(θ; θ<r) = rD(r⁻) − Γ(r) = Z(r), which has the sign of piv'.
- (f)(C2) If D is nondecreasing and R_0 ≤ R_1 a.s. (monotone coupling), D(R_0) ≤ D(R_1) a.s., so piv(F_1) ≥ piv(F_0). Necessity: if D(y_0) > D(y_1) for some p ≤ y_0 < y_1 < r̄, take F_i = U[y_i, y_i + ε]. They are FOSD ordered, and by right-continuity piv(F_1) − piv(F_0) → D(y_1) − D(y_0) < 0. If the support must start at zero, mix the same small uniform component on [0, r̄] into both laws, as in the proof of Proposition 1(c); this scales the difference by a positive factor and keeps its sign. Strictness: piv(F_1) > piv(F_0) iff Pr(D(R_1) > D(R_0)) > 0 under the coupling.
- (f)(C3) G_H(r̄) = 0 gives D = G_L on [0, r̄], which is nondecreasing, and W_H = 1. Inclusions: if D is nondecreasing on [0,r] then D(r) ≥ its average Γ(r)/r, which is (C1). Strictness of (C3) ⟹ (C2): G_L = δ_1, G_H = ½δ_1 + ½δ_10 has D = ½ on [1,10), nondecreasing, but G_H(r̄) = ½ for any r̄ ≥ 1. Strictness of (C2) ⟹ (C1): the wide classes in the benchmark check below.

Conditions: (a), (b) none beyond the standing model. (c), (d) prior 1/2, full orders and purchase-only recruitment (τ > 1/2, so that e(−1) = e(0) = ρ), the standing assumptions of A.11–A.13 (E[X] = 0 and symmetric posteriors); this is a restatement of A8 line 9, not a new condition. The gap formula in (b) is prior-free. (e) FOSD path; exact formulas for the uniform family. (f) as stated, in economic terms: (C1) the low class carries at least as much value below the incumbent's top; (C2) a value the incumbent can reach is never good news about the class; (C3) the high class outbids every incumbent on the path.

Benchmark check.
- Values: ϑ = 0.462117, λ = 0.336122, f̄ = 0.363676, ρλ/2 = 0.042015. piv(F_r) = 1 − ℓ/r on (ℓ,h): piv(3) = 2/3 and piv(3.6) = 0.722222, so ϑ·piv = 0.308078 and 0.333751, the registry values wgap_r1_B and wgap_r2_B.
- (c): at r_1 the class channel is 0.028010 and the recruitment channel 0.223853, total 0.251863 = cov_win_r1_B. Recruitment is 89% of the covariance there. At r_2 only the class channel survives, 0.030344 = cov_win_r2_B.
- (d): at r_1 the purchase impact on winning is 0.65404, the sale impact 0.03851, and the difference 0.61553 = (1−ρ)w_1. At r_2 both impacts equal 0.04172 and the difference is zero.
- (e): the benchmark hump is piv = 1 − ℓ/r on [ℓ,h] and (h−ℓ)/r above, with its peak piv = 0.9 at r = h = 10 (winner gap 0.4159) and piv = 0.45 at r = 20 (gap 0.2080). The full-orders interval (r_S, h) = (2.124, 10) lies entirely on the rising side.
- (f): (C3) margin h − r̄ = 10 − 3.6 = 6.4 (h/r̄ = 2.78). (C1): Z(r) = ℓ = 1 on (ℓ,h), so piv' = ℓ/r², which is the derivative in the manuscript's proof of A.13(ii). Table A6: moderate gap (δ_1, δ_2) has r_1 = 1.5 against a peak at 2; narrow bands peak at r* = 9.96; same-means points peak at 10; wide classes U[0.6,1.4] / U[1,19] peak at r* = 6.083 (piv = 0.7176), against r_1 = 4.5 and r_2 = 5.5. In the wide-classes economy W_H(4.5) = 0.924 so (C3) fails, D falls on [1.4,19] so (C2) fails, and (C1) holds with a thin margin: D(5.5) = 0.75 against Γ/r = 0.716, a margin of 0.034 at r_2. Only (C1) covers all of Table A6.

What is lost: The unconditional claim "the gap rises with r". It is false beyond the peak, so A.13(ii) needs (C1), (C2) or (C3); with (C3) it is the benchmark's own ordering r < h. Nothing else is lost: sign, slope, post-ceiling covariance and winner impacts hold generally, and (c) adds the recruitment channel that A.13(iii) omits.

## Candidate 2: The purchase-to-sale win ratio rises with strength even where the absolute gap falls

Angle: both. The ratio is a reformulation; its monotonicity uses a qualitative ordering of the classes.

Intuition: Against a dominant incumbent nobody wins, so any difference in win probabilities must vanish; that is the mechanical reason the absolute gap falls. The relative advantage of a purchase over a sale survives. It depends on the incumbent only through W_H/W_L, and that ratio keeps rising as the incumbent strengthens, because a stronger incumbent hurts low values proportionally more.

Formal statement. Full orders at any prior; W_L > 0. Let Ω(F) = W_H(F)/W_L(F) ≥ 1 and RR = w_1/w_{−1}.

- (a) *General, no extra condition.* RR = [MΩ + 1 − M]/[mΩ + 1 − m], strictly increasing in Ω alone. RR ≥ 1, with equality iff piv(F) = 0. RR < M/m (= 1+u at prior 1/2, the largest likelihood ratio one order can carry), approached as Ω → ∞ (W_L → 0). Write sel(F) = 1 − 1/Ω = Pr(θ_L < R | R < θ_H): given that the high-class twin wins, the chance that the low-class twin loses. RR is increasing in sel.
- (b) *Uniform path, exact.* Ω(r) = E_H[min(θ,r)]/E_L[min(θ,r)] and d ln Ω/dr = [ε_H(r) − ε_L(r)]/r, where ε_j(r) = r·S_j(r)/E_j[min(θ,r)] is the elasticity of the class's capped mean with respect to the cap. Ω, hence RR, rises exactly where the high class's capped value is at least as elastic in the cap as the low class's. Sufficient: the hazard-rate order of the classes, S_H/S_L nondecreasing (a higher bar is cleared proportionally more often by the high class); the likelihood-ratio order implies it. Then Ω rises from 1 (at r ≤ p) to μ_H/μ_L and is flat above the top of H's support, and RR rises from 1 to [Mμ_H + (1−M)μ_L]/[mμ_H + (1−m)μ_L] < 1+u.
- (c) *General incumbent families.* If the classes are likelihood-ratio ordered and the incumbent family is reverse-hazard-rate ordered (F_u(y)/F_t(y) nondecreasing in y for u > t), then Ω is nondecreasing in strength. The uniform path and every translated window with a log-concave CDF F_0 satisfy the second condition.

Derivation.
- (a) w_1/w_{−1} = [M·W_H + (1−M)·W_L]/[m·W_H + (1−m)·W_L]; divide by W_L. d/dΩ = [M(mΩ+1−m) − m(MΩ+1−M)]/(mΩ+1−m)² = (M−m)/(mΩ+1−m)² > 0. Ω = 1 gives RR = 1; Ω → ∞ gives M/m; at prior 1/2, M/m = [(1+u)/(2+u)]/[1/(2+u)] = 1+u. sel = (W_H − W_L)/W_H = Pr(θ_L ≤ R < θ_H)/Pr(R < θ_H).
- (b) For R ~ U[0,r], W_j = ∫_0^1 Pr(θ_j > rz)dz = (1/r)∫_0^r S_j = E_j[min(θ,r)]/r. So Ω is the ratio of capped means, and d/dr E_j[min(θ,r)] = S_j(r). Hence d ln Ω/dr = S_H(r)/E_H[min(θ,r)] − S_L(r)/E_L[min(θ,r)]. This is ≥ 0 iff S_H(r)/S_L(r) ≥ ∫_0^r S_H / ∫_0^r S_L, a weighted average of S_H/S_L over [0,r] with weights S_L. If S_H/S_L is nondecreasing the average is at most its value at r. The limits follow from E_j[min(θ,r)] → μ_j as r → ∞.
- (c) For u > t write W_j(t) = ∫F_t dG_j with densities or masses g_j. Then W_H(u)W_L(t) − W_H(t)W_L(u) = ½∬[g_H(θ)g_L(θ') − g_H(θ')g_L(θ)]·[F_u(θ)F_t(θ') − F_t(θ)F_u(θ')]dθdθ'. For θ < θ' the first bracket is ≤ 0 under the likelihood-ratio order. The second is ≤ 0 because F_u/F_t is nondecreasing. Both flip sign for θ > θ'. So the integrand is ≥ 0 and Ω(u) ≥ Ω(t). For F_t(y) = F_0(y − t) the ratio F_0(y−u)/F_0(y−t) has y-derivative (ln F_0)'(y−u) − (ln F_0)'(y−t) ≥ 0 when ln F_0 is concave.

Conditions: none for (a). (b) uniform incumbents and the hazard-rate order of the classes (or the exact elasticity inequality). (c) likelihood-ratio classes plus a reverse-hazard-ordered family. In plain terms: a higher bar is cleared relatively more often by the high class, and a stronger incumbent beats low values proportionally more than high values.

Benchmark check.
- Point masses satisfy the hazard-rate order: S_H/S_L = 1 on [0,ℓ) and ∞ on [ℓ,h). So Ω(r) = r/ℓ on [ℓ,h] and h/ℓ = 10 above. RR(3) = 1.60098 and RR(3.6) = 1.70708. RR saturates at 2.21593 for r ≥ h, against the bound e = 2.71828.
- In the known counterexample (δ_1, δ_2) the ratio does not fall: Ω ≡ 2 and RR ≡ 1.3642 for r ≥ 2. At the benchmark slot the absolute gap falls from 0.2311 (r = 2) to 0.0462 (r = 10).
- The winner odds ratio is not a repair: in the counterexample it falls from 3.708 (r = 2) to 1.861 (r = 3), 1.440 (r = 10) and 1.371 (r = 100), because it keeps the level of the win probabilities.
- Wide classes: Ω rises from 1.11 (r = 1) through 4.16 (r = 4.5) and 4.94 (r = 5.5) to 10 at r = 19, and S_H/S_L is nondecreasing, although piv peaks at 6.08 and falls to 0.474 at r = 19. In a random experiment (1500 FOSD-ordered discrete pairs, uniform incumbent), none of the 280 pairs with hazard-rate order had a falling Ω, but 1112 of the 1220 without it did.

What is lost: The level. RR does not say how large the absolute gap is, and the absolute gap is what vanishes against a dominant incumbent. The monotone claim needs the hazard-rate order of the classes (uniform incumbents) or the likelihood-ratio plus reverse-hazard conditions (general families); only (a) holds without conditions. So not every claim here is "no extra condition", but the condition is a qualitative ordering that the benchmark and all of Table A6 satisfy.

## Candidate 3: The winner gap is the marginal spread, and relative to the price gap it is a slot-free ratio

Angle: reformulate.

Intuition: The spread Δ_T is the average class gap in proceeds, E_F[ξ(R)] with ξ(R) = ∫_p^R D. The winner gap is the marginal version, E_F[ξ'(R)] = E_F[D(R)]. Raising the incumbent's value by dR raises the proceeds by the challenger's chance to exceed R, and the class difference in that chance is D(R). This explains why the spread is monotone in strength under the class ranking (ξ is nondecreasing) while the winner gap is not (the derivative of a monotone function need not be monotone), and why winners lie outside the (Δ_T, τ) reduction.

Formal statement.
- (a) piv(F) = E_F[ξ'(R)], where ξ = Γ and Δ_T(F) = E_F[ξ(R)] (A1, proof of Proposition 1(c)).
- (b) Location paths F_t(y) = F_0(y − t): piv(F_t) = dΔ_T/dt, so the gap rises iff the spread is convex in strength.
- (c) Uniform path: piv(F_r) = Δ_T'(r) + Δ_T(r)/r = [rΔ_T(r)]'/r.
- (d) Proposition 1(c) has an exact parallel: the spread rises under every strengthening iff ξ is nondecreasing iff D ≥ 0; the winner gap rises under every strengthening in a range iff ξ is convex there iff D is nondecreasing.
- (e) Above the ceiling, [Pr(W | X=1) − Pr(W | X=−1)] / [P(1) − P(−1)] = piv(F)/Δ_T(F): winning frequency per unit of price spread. It contains no slot parameter and not ρ. Equivalently Cov(X,1_W) = v·I_−·piv(F)/Δ_T(F), where v = 2b+λ is the order volume and I_− the sale impact.
- (f) The reduced outcome does not fix piv. Example: p = 1/2, G_L = ½δ_1 + ½δ_5, G_H = ½δ_{1.5} + ½δ_6, R ~ U[2,3]. Then Δ_T = 1/4 and γ = 1/2, so the trading, entry and price machinery of the paper applies, but W_H = W_L = 1/2 and orders never predict the winner.

Derivation.
- (a) ξ is Lipschitz (|D| ≤ 1) with ξ' = D almost everywhere. F is continuous, so it charges none of D's countably many jumps, and E_F[ξ'(R)] = E_F[D(R)] = piv.
- (b) Δ_T(F_t) = E[ξ(R_0 + t)]. Differentiate under the expectation (ξ Lipschitz): E[ξ'(R_0+t)] = E_{F_t}[D(R)] = piv(F_t). For a general smooth family, dΔ_T/dt = ∫Dφ_t dy with φ_t = −∂_tF_t ≥ 0, whereas piv(F_t) = ∫D f_t dy; the two coincide when φ_t = f_t, that is, for location shifts.
- (c) A6, proof of Proposition A.5, Step 1: Δ_T(r) = (1/r)∫_p^r Γ. So rΔ_T = ∫_p^r Γ and (rΔ_T)' = Γ(r) = r·piv(F_r).
- (d) Proposition 1(c): Δ_T(F) = E_F[ξ(R)] with ξ nondecreasing iff D ≥ 0. For the gap, E_F[ξ'(R)] is monotone under all FOSD pairs in a range iff ξ' is nondecreasing there (necessity by the narrow-window construction in Candidate 1).
- (e) Above the ceiling e ≡ ρ, so Pr(W|1) − Pr(W|−1) = ρ(w_1 − w_{−1}) = ρϑ·piv, and S = P(1) − P(−1) = ρΔ_Tϑ (A.11(i) with χ_r = 0). The ratio is piv/Δ_T. With I_− = ρΔ_Tϑ/2 and f̄ = v/2: Cov(X,1_W) = f̄ρϑ·piv = vI_−·piv/Δ_T.
- (f) Directly: in both classes W_j = ½·0 + ½·1 = 1/2, since the low atoms (1, 1.5) lose to every R ≥ 2 and the high atoms (5, 6) win against every R ≤ 3. Δ_T = ∫(1−F)D = ½·½·1 = 1/4 from [1,1.5), where F = 0; γ = ∫FD = ½·1 = 1/2 from [5,6), where F = 1.

Conditions: (a) and (e) none beyond the standing model ((e) needs prior 1/2, full orders, above the ceiling). (b) location shifts. (c) the uniform scale family. (d) the stated range.

Benchmark check.
- Δ_T = (r−ℓ)²/(2r), Δ_T' = (r²−ℓ²)/(2r²), and Δ_T' + Δ_T/r = 1 − ℓ/r. At r = 3: 0.4444 + 0.2222 = 0.6667 = piv.
- (e) at r_2 = 3.6: winning-frequency gap 0.083438, price spread 0.108469 (the registry value ba_r2_B), ratio 0.769231 = 2/(r − ℓ). Unlike piv, this ratio falls in r: 0.500 at r = 5 and 0.286 at r = 8.
- ξ(R) = (min(R,h) − ℓ)₊ is convex up to h and concave above, which is the kink at h seen in Candidate 1.

What is lost: It is an identity and an interpretation, not a new monotonicity theorem. (b) is exact only for location shifts and (c) only for the uniform scale family. For other FOSD paths the winner gap and the spread's slope weight D differently. Condition (C1) of Candidate 1 is the same statement in different words.

## Importance of the weakness

- **The counterexample is the benchmark with r > h.** It uses G_L = δ_1, G_H = δ_2, p = 1/2, R ~ U[0,r], r > 2; the only departure from the benchmark ordering 0 < p < ℓ < r < h is that the incumbent's range overshoots the high class's value. That is not exotic: a strong strategic incumbent beating even a high-quality challenger is natural, and the paper itself takes entry to return to its floor against a strong enough incumbent. The benchmark rules it out by assumption (eq:order).
- **Solver A's economy never recruits.** With π = ρ = 1/2, λ = β = 1/3, k = 1/100, c_H = 2 (ϑ = 3/7), B_F(M) < 1.22 < c_H for every r, and for r > 2 the trading bounds give K_H, K_L ≥ Δ_T/7 > 1/28 > k. So the falling gap is a pure class-channel effect in an economy where the paper's mechanism is dormant.
- **Where the weakness bites inside the mechanism.** Recruitment survives past r = h iff c_H < B_h(M) = 3.656 = 0.366h at the benchmark slot, against the benchmark c_H = 6 (1.64 times larger). With the benchmark primitives and c_H = 4 the recruiting window {B(1/2) < c_H ≤ B(M)} is (4.12, 9.06], entirely on the rising side. With c_H = 3 it is (8.06, 12.19], straddling the peak: the gap falls from 0.4159 at r = 10 to 0.3413 at the ceiling. With c_H = 2 it is (12.56, 18.28], entirely on the falling side. So the benchmark's cost is 64% above the level at which the trend reverses inside the window.
- **The paper's own economies are on the rising side.** Narrow bands, low prior, high prior and same-means points have r_2 ≤ 5.5 against peaks at 9.96 to 10; the moderate-gap economy (δ_1, δ_2) has r_1 = 1.5 against a peak at 2. Wide classes are the close call: the peak is at 6.083 against r_2 = 5.5.
- **Economically, the trend is not central; the sign is.** The main text (08_empirical.tex:44–50) states a sign (purchase beats sale at both strengths), two values, the contrast with the entry covariance, and the posterior-rises-with-price claim. None of these is a trend claim, and the slide (F16) states only the sign. The trend appears only in A.13(ii). An empiricist tests Pr(W | buy, prepared) > Pr(W | sell, prepared), the winner difference-in-differences (Candidate 1(d)), and the covariance above and below the ceiling. A trend in measured strength is a secondary test: strength is measured with error, the peak depends on unobserved class values, and in a pooled sample the slope is unsigned unless all deals lie below the peak. For point-mass classes the sign holds at every strength r > ℓ, even above h; only the monotone rise reverses.
- **Verdict.** Minor and local. One named condition ((C3) outbidding, shared with ownership; or (C1) for the exact uniform statement) restores the trend, and the relative-risk statement of Candidate 2 restores a monotone claim without any condition on where the incumbent sits, beyond the hazard-rate ordering of the classes.

## Other findings (errors in the manuscript, anything surprising)

1. **The post-ceiling covariance does not depend on the liquidity mass.** (b + λ/2)ρϑ(W_H − W_L) in A.13(iii) equals (ρλ/2)(W_H − W_L), because (b + λ/2)ϑ = λ/2. The winner covariance above the ceiling therefore identifies the presence probability λ (given ρ and piv), a second route to λ besides A.12's λ = vϑ. The two routes coincide identically, so equating them is not a model test. It is the cross-equation restriction Cov(X,1_W) = v·I_−·piv/Δ_T of Candidate 3(e), which ties the winner covariance to prices and volume through the pure auction ratio piv/Δ_T, and it is testable only if that ratio is known from auction data.
2. **cov_win_r1_B uses a formula the proposition does not state.** The registry value 0.251863 is the full two-channel covariance (0.028010 + 0.223853). A.13(iii) states the winner covariance only for r > r_C. The proof's general step Cov = f̄(y_1 − y_{−1}) supports the two-channel form, but the statement should give it (Candidate 1(c)). At r_1, 89% of the covariance is recruitment.
3. **Winners lie outside (Δ_T, τ) even in sign.** The example in Candidate 3(f) has Δ_T > 0 and γ > 0 yet W_H = W_L. So "Δ_T > 0 and g_H > g_L" does not imply that orders predict the winner; the missing hypothesis is exactly that the incumbent can fall where D > 0.
4. **The difference-in-differences for winners (Candidate 1(d)) is a clean test that the weakness does not touch.** It is the winner analogue of A.11(ii), holds under the same conditions (prior 1/2, full orders), and (1−ρ)χ_r w_1 is positive on the whole recruiting range and zero above the ceiling, whatever piv(F) is.
5. **Winners are a hump in strength; Δ_T is monotone.** Proposition 1 gives Δ_T nondecreasing because ξ is nondecreasing. piv = E_F[ξ'(R)] has no such property. This is the sharpest statement of why A.13 cannot inherit the monotonicity that the rest of the appendix gets from Proposition 1.
