\section{Equilibrium and auction objects}\label{sec:objects}

\subsection{The complete equilibrium object}
The class is $J\in\{H,L\}$, with $\Pr(J=H)=\pi$.
The investor sees the class, while preparation reveals the challenger's value $\theta$.
The pair $(J,\theta)$, the incumbent value $R$, the preparation cost, and the slot draws are mutually independent.
The cost equals zero with probability $\rho$, and equals $c_H$ otherwise.

Write $\eta(q)$ for the liquidity order law, with $\eta(-1)=\eta(1)=\beta$ and $\eta(0)=1-2\beta$.
Use $\eta_q=\eta(q)$ as an abbreviation.
An equilibrium is the tuple
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tuple}
\mathcal E=(\sigma_H,\sigma_L,P,\mu,\zeta,\text{bids}).
\end{equation}
Here $\sigma_j\in\Delta(\{-1,0,1\})$ is the order law conditional on an informed slot and class $j$.
Write $P_q=P(q)$ and $\mu_q=\mu(q)$.
The map $\bar\mu(P)$ gives the challenger's posterior at each realized price.
The probability $\zeta(P)$ gives high-cost preparation; zero-cost preparation equals one at every price.
Thus $A(P,0)=1$, $A(P,c_H)=\zeta(P)$, and $e(q)=\rho+(1-\rho)\zeta(P_q)$.

The bids are $b_R=R$ and $b_\theta=\theta$ after preparation, subject to the reserve.
This is the equilibrium definition in \texttt{P1\_core.tex}; the price beliefs and $A$ are its derived objects.
Write $\Delta=\Delta_T$ for the target-payoff spread, as in that package.

The equilibrium conditions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Bayes' rule gives the raw posterior at every order.
\item Bayes' rule gives the challenger's posterior at every realized price.
\item High-cost preparation maximizes expected acquisition profit net of its cost at the observed price.
\item Each price equals expected terminal target proceeds conditional on the order.
\item Each prescribed order maximizes the informed investor's conditional payoff over the entire unit menu.
\item Each prepared bidder bids its value, and a bid at the reserve is admissible.
\end{enumerate}

The investor rule IM permits any mixture among optimal orders.
The alternative rule I0 requires zero when zero ties with a nonzero optimal order.
The high-cost challenger rule C1 requires preparation when gross profit equals cost.
The alternative high-cost rule CM permits mixtures at that equality.

The zero-cost realization prepares at every price under both rules, as in the core definition.
All strict examples below satisfy both investor rules and both challenger rules.
At $p_H$, the high-cost entry probability depends on C1 or CM.

For any candidate order laws, set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bayes}
 a_j(q)=(1-\lambda)\eta_q+\lambda\sigma_j(q),\quad
 d_q=\pi a_H(q)+(1-\pi)a_L(q),\quad
 \mu_q=\frac{\pi a_H(q)}{d_q}.
\end{equation}
Every order has positive liquidity mass.
Thus every permitted deviation reaches a price already on the equilibrium path.
Beliefs at other prices have no effect on these deviations.

\subsection{Auction payoffs for general value classes}
The class laws have finite means, and both supports lie above $p>0$.
Assume $G_H$ first-order stochastically dominates $G_L$.
The incumbent CDF $F$ is continuous on $[0,\bar r]$.
For a prepared value $v\ge p$, truthful bids give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kernels}
 T(R,v)=\max\{p,\min(R,v)\},\qquad g(R,v)=(v-\max\{p,R\})_+.
\end{equation}

Without preparation, target proceeds equal $p\ind\{R\ge p\}$.
The challenger wins if $R<v$.
Truthful bids are optimal because each bidder wins exactly when its value exceeds the required payment.
A higher bid can cause a purchase above value; a lower bid can remove a profitable purchase.

\begin{lemma}[Auction objects; analytical]\label{lem:auction}
The class objects are
\begin{align}\label{eq:class}
 t_0&=p[1-F(p)],&
 t_j&=p+\int_p^\infty[1-G_j(v)][1-F(v)]\dd v,\\
 g_j&=\int_p^\infty[1-G_j(v)]F(v)\dd v,&
 W_j&=\E_j[F(\theta)].\nonumber
\end{align}
Let $D(v)=G_L(v)-G_H(v)$ and $\Gamma=\E_H[\theta]-\E_L[\theta]$.
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:split}
 \Delta=t_H-t_L=\int_p^\infty D(v)[1-F(v)]\dd v,\quad
 g_H-g_L=\int_p^\infty D(v)F(v)\dd v=\Gamma-\Delta.
\end{equation}
A stochastic increase in incumbent strength weakly lowers each $g_j$ and weakly raises $\Delta$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For $v\ge p$, write
\[
 T(R,v)=p+\int_p^v\ind\{R>x\}\dd x,\qquad
 g(R,v)=\int_p^v\ind\{R<x\}\dd x.
\]
Take expectations over $R$ and then over $v$.
Tonelli's theorem applies to each nonnegative integral.
This gives \eqref{eq:class}.
Subtract the two class expressions to obtain \eqref{eq:split}.

For each $v$, the two expected payoffs sum to $v$.
This also gives $\Delta+g_H-g_L=\Gamma$.
A stronger incumbent has a smaller CDF, which gives the stated signs directly in the integrals.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Beliefs, prices, and deviations}
Put $b=(1-\lambda)\beta$ and $u=\lambda/b$.
For $0<\beta\le1/3$, the common liquidity mass at zero is at least $b$.
Consequently, under every candidate profile,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bounds}
 m=\frac{\pi}{\pi+(1-\pi)(1+u)}\le\mu_q\le
 M=\frac{\pi(1+u)}{1+\pi u},\qquad q\in\{-1,0,1\}.
\end{equation}
Indeed $a_H(q)/a_L(q)$ lies between $(1+u)^{-1}$ and $1+u$ at each order.
These bounds allow arbitrary mixtures in each class.

Define
\[
 B_F(\mu)=g_L+\mu(g_H-g_L),\qquad
 e_F(\mu)=\rho+(1-\rho)\ind\{B_F(\mu)\ge c_H\}.
\]
The core definition fixes zero-cost preparation at every price under both challenger rules.
Hence entry at every price is at least $\rho$ in every candidate equilibrium.
Competitive prices satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:price}
 P_q=t_0+e(q)[t_L-t_0+\Delta\mu_q].
\end{equation}
Entry $e(q)$ depends on $q$ only through $P_q$, since it equals $\bar e(P_q)=\rho+(1-\rho)\zeta(P_q)$.
If $\Delta>0$, the price recovers the raw posterior through
\begin{equation}\label{eq:invert}
 \mu_q=\frac{P_q-t_0-e(q)(t_L-t_0)}{e(q)\Delta}.
\end{equation}
Thus equal prices imply equal raw posteriors.
Bayes' rule at the price then gives $\bar\mu(P_q)=\mu_q$.
Under C1, preparation optimality gives $e(q)=e_F(\mu_q)$.
Under CM, a high-cost tie permits any $e(q)\in[\rho,1]$.

A deviation keeps the candidate prices and preparation rule fixed.
Its payoff, conditional on the informed slot, is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:deviation}
 U_j(q)=q\{t_0+e(q)(t_j-t_0)-P_q\}-k|q|.
\end{equation}
The four nonzero payoffs therefore equal
\begin{align}\label{eq:payoffs}
 U_H(1)&=e(1)\Delta(1-\mu_1)-k,&
 U_H(-1)&=-e(-1)\Delta(1-\mu_{-1})-k,\\
 U_L(-1)&=e(-1)\Delta\mu_{-1}-k,&
 U_L(1)&=-e(1)\Delta\mu_1-k.\nonumber
\end{align}
The zero order pays zero.
The factor $\lambda$ enters unconditional profit, but does not enter the conditional choice problem.

\section{Value classes and priors}\label{sec:binary}

\subsection{The two-class comparison}

\begin{proposition}[General classes and priors; analytical]\label{prop:binary}
Let $F_1\le F_0$ and $F_2\le F_1$ be continuous incumbent CDFs.
Let $0<\pi,\rho,\lambda<1$ and $0<\beta\le1/3$.
Write $\Delta_i=\Delta(F_i)$ and $\kappa=\min\{m,1-M\}$.
Suppose these strict inequalities hold:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:region}
 B_0(\pi)<c_H<B_1(M),\qquad
 \Delta_0<k<\rho\kappa\Delta_1,\qquad
 B_2(M)<c_H.
\end{equation}
Under either challenger rule, the equilibrium orders and all realized prices are unique at each strength.
At $F_0$ the orders are $(0,0)$ and entry is $\rho$.
At $F_1$ the orders are $(1,-1)$, and only the positive order recruits the high-cost realization.
At $F_2$ the orders remain $(1,-1)$, and entry is $\rho$.

The complete objects are as follows.
For $F_0$, let
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weak-tuple}
 \sigma_H=\sigma_L=\delta_0,\quad \mu_q=\pi,\quad
 P_q=t_{0,0}+\rho[t_{L,0}-t_{0,0}+\Delta_0\pi],\quad
 \bar\mu(P_q)=\pi,\quad A(P_q,C)=\ind\{C=0\}.
\end{equation}
For $F_i$, $i\in\{1,2\}$, let
\begin{equation}\label{eq:full-tuple}
 \sigma_H=\delta_1,\quad\sigma_L=\delta_{-1},\quad
 (\mu_{-1},\mu_0,\mu_1)=(m,\pi,M),\quad
 P_q=t_{0,i}+e_i(\mu_q)[t_{L,i}-t_{0,i}+\Delta_i\mu_q],
\end{equation}
with $\bar\mu(P_q)=\mu_q$ and $A(P_q,C)=\ind\{C\le B_i(\mu_q)\}$.
Append the truthful bids from \eqref{eq:tuple} to both objects.

At $F_1$,
\begin{align}\label{eq:entry-own}
 \mathsf E_1&=\rho+(1-\rho)(b+\pi\lambda),&
 e_{H,1}&=\rho+(1-\rho)(b+\lambda),\\
 \mathsf O_{H,0}&=\pi\rho W_H(F_0),&
 \mathsf O_{H,1}&=\pi e_{H,1}W_H(F_1).\nonumber
\end{align}
High-class ownership rises precisely when
\begin{equation}\label{eq:own-condition}
 [\rho+(1-\rho)(b+\lambda)]W_H(F_1)>\rho W_H(F_0).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
First consider any equilibrium at $F_0$.
If $\Delta_0>0$, equations \eqref{eq:bounds}--\eqref{eq:payoffs} bound every correctly signed gross return by $\Delta_0<k$.
The two wrong-sign returns are negative.
Thus each class must choose zero.
If $\Delta_0=0$, both classes have identical target proceeds and every nonzero order pays $-k$.
Zero orders again follow.

Bayes' rule then gives $\mu_q=\pi$ at every order.
The inequality $B_0(\pi)<c_H$ fixes entry at $\rho$ and gives \eqref{eq:weak-tuple}.
Its constant price is competitive.
The cost comparison and the payoff calculation verify all conditions against this fixed object.

Next consider any equilibrium at $F_1$ or $F_2$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:auction} gives $\Delta_2\ge\Delta_1>0$.
Under every candidate profile, a high-class purchase earns at least $\rho(1-M)\Delta_i-k>0$.
A low-class sale earns at least $\rho m\Delta_i-k>0$.
The wrong-sign orders lose, and zero pays zero.
Thus every equilibrium has $\sigma_H=\delta_1$ and $\sigma_L=\delta_{-1}$.

This argument excludes mixed alternatives as well as pure alternatives.

For these forced orders, the conditional probabilities are
\[
 (a_H(-1),a_H(0),a_H(1))=(b,b_0,b+\lambda),\qquad
 (a_L(-1),a_L(0),a_L(1))=(b+\lambda,b_0,b),
\]
where $b_0=(1-\lambda)(1-2\beta)$.
Substitution into \eqref{eq:bayes} gives $(m,\pi,M)$.
Since $B_1(\pi)\le B_0(\pi)<c_H<B_1(M)$, only the positive order recruits at $F_1$.
The positive cost gap also implies $g_{H,1}>g_{L,1}$.
At $F_2$, $g_{H,2}\ge g_{L,2}$ and $B_2(M)<c_H$ exclude the high-cost realization at every order.

The proposed prices in \eqref{eq:full-tuple} are therefore competitive.
Equation~\eqref{eq:invert} verifies the price beliefs, and the cost comparisons verify preparation.
Equations \eqref{eq:payoffs} verify every deviation with these prices fixed.
Truthful bids verify the auction condition.
The forced orders, price inversion, and strict cost comparisons also establish uniqueness.

The positive order has unconditional probability $b+\pi\lambda$.
Its class-$H$ probability is $b+\lambda$, and its class-$L$ probability is $b$.
These probabilities give \eqref{eq:entry-own}.
Conditional on class and preparation, the value law and incumbent law remain unchanged.
Multiplication by the class win probability therefore gives ownership and \eqref{eq:own-condition}.
\end{proof}

The strict conditions define an open set of costs, priors, strengths, and interior slot parameters.
For general CDFs, continuity of the displayed payoff objects is the required topology.
The bound $\beta\le1/3$ is a structural domain restriction.
At its boundary, the set is relatively open in that domain.

The strict cost comparisons give the same outcome under CM.
In every finite uniform example below, $B_i(m)>0$ also makes zero-cost preparation strict under every profile.
The two investor rules give the same result because every selected order is strict.

The sufficient ownership condition $(1+\rho)W_H(F_1)\ge2\rho W_H(F_0)>0$ applies when $b+\lambda>1/2$.
Indeed $e_{H,1}>(1+\rho)/2$ then implies \eqref{eq:own-condition}.
For general slots, \eqref{eq:own-condition} is the exact condition.

\subsection{A nonempty region for every ranked pair}

\begin{proposition}[Every prior and every positive class gap; analytical]\label{prop:nonempty}
Fix the class laws, $p>0$, $\Gamma>0$, and $0<\pi,\rho,\lambda<1$ with $0<\beta\le1/3$.
For uniform incumbents, a nonempty open set of $(r_0,r_1,r_2,c_H,k)$ satisfies \eqref{eq:region} and \eqref{eq:own-condition}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Set $\Gamma(x)=\int_p^xD(v)\dd v$ and $v_* =\inf\{x\ge p:\Gamma(x)>0\}$.
Finite class means and $\Gamma>0$ give $p\le v_*<\infty$.
For $R\sim U[0,r]$, Tonelli's theorem gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nonempty}
 \Delta(r)=\frac1r\int_p^r\Gamma(x)\dd x,\qquad
 \Delta'(r)=\frac{r\Gamma(r)-\int_p^r\Gamma(x)\dd x}{r^2}
 \ge\frac{p\Gamma(r)}{r^2}>0\quad(r>v_*).
\end{equation}
The derivative formula holds because $\Gamma(x)$ is continuous.
Also $\Delta(r)\to0$ as $r\downarrow v_*$ and
\[
 g_H(r)-g_L(r)=\Gamma-\Delta(r)\ge p\Gamma/r>0.
\]
At $r=v_*$,
\[
 B_{v_*}(M)-B_{v_*}(\pi)=(M-\pi)\Gamma>0.
\]
Dominated convergence gives continuity of $g_j(r)$ and $W_H(r)$.
It also gives $g_j(r)\to0$ as $r\to\infty$.
Since $\theta\ge p$, $W_H(v_*)\ge p/v_*>0$.

Let $e_H=\rho+(1-\rho)(b+\lambda)>\rho$.
Choose $r_1>v_*$ near enough that
\[
 B_{r_1}(M)>B_{v_*}(\pi),\qquad e_HW_H(r_1)>\rho W_H(v_*).
\]
Choose $r_0\in(v_*,r_1)$ near enough that $\Delta(r_0)<\rho\kappa\Delta(r_1)$.
Because $B_r(\pi)$ and $W_H(r)$ decrease with $r$, these choices give
\[
 B_{r_0}(\pi)<B_{r_1}(M),\qquad e_HW_H(r_1)>\rho W_H(r_0).
\]

Select $c_H$ and $k$ strictly inside the two intervals in \eqref{eq:region}.
Select $r_2>r_1$ large enough that $B_{r_2}(M)<c_H$.
The limit $g_j(r)\to0$ permits this last choice.
Every strict inequality persists under small perturbations by continuity.
Proposition~\ref{prop:binary} supplies and verifies the complete equilibrium tuples throughout this set.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Value-class examples; computer-assisted]\label{prop:examples}
The benchmark, moderate, narrow, A, B, and C examples satisfy their stated conditions in Proposition~\ref{prop:binary}.
The moderate example states only the first two strengths.
Every stated equilibrium has the tuple \eqref{eq:weak-tuple} or \eqref{eq:full-tuple}.
Example D has unique zero orders at its weak strength and unique full orders at both higher strengths.
Its entry stays at $\rho$ at all three strengths.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For a point value, integrate \eqref{eq:kernels} over the intervals split by $p$ and $v$.
For $p\le v\le r$, the result is
\[
 t_v=v-\frac{v^2-p^2}{2r},\qquad g_v=\frac{v^2-p^2}{2r},\qquad W_v=v/r.
\]
For $v\ge r$, the result is $t_v=r/2+p^2/(2r)$, $g_v=v-t_v$, and $W_v=1$.
A uniform class averages these polynomials over its support, with a split at $r$.
The script performs these integrations with exact rational arithmetic.

For the narrow classes, direct integration gives
\begin{align}\label{eq:bands}
 t_L&=\ell-\frac{\ell^2+\varepsilon^2/3-p^2}{2r},&
 g_L&=\frac{\ell^2+\varepsilon^2/3-p^2}{2r},\\
 t_H&=r/2+p^2/(2r),&
 g_H&=h-t_H,\qquad
 \Delta=\frac{(r-\ell)^2+\varepsilon^2/3}{2r},\nonumber
\end{align}
where $\varepsilon=1/20$.
In C, the low and high class supports both cross relevant incumbent boundaries.
The same integral formulas, with the required splits, give
\[
 W_H(r_0)=1,\qquad W_H(r_1)=599/648,\qquad W_H(r_2)=79/88.
\]
The low CDF exceeds the high CDF on their common interior support.
Outside that support, the CDF order follows from their endpoints.
Thus C satisfies the class order even though its supports overlap.

The interval margins in Section~\ref{sec:margins} verify every strict cost and trade condition for the successful examples.
They also verify \eqref{eq:own-condition}.
Proposition~\ref{prop:binary} then proves uniqueness through bounds on every profile.
The script additionally prints every price, posterior, cost comparison, and deviation payoff against the fixed tuple.
The arithmetic uses outward intervals at sixty decimal digits for expressions that depend on $e$.
No rounded decimal determines an entry decision.

For D, the upper cost margin at $r_1$ is $\reg{margin_p2_ex_d_strong_top_B}<0$.
The trade bounds for every profile still force full orders.
The inequalities $B_1(M)<c_H$ and $B_2(M)<c_H$ then exclude every high-cost realization.
The weak trade bound forces zero orders at $r_0$, and its prior profit is below cost.
These calculations verify D directly, and its constant entry floor.
\end{proof}

\section{More than two classes}\label{sec:many}

\subsection{A separation condition}
Let $j\in\{1,\ldots,J\}$ have prior $\pi_j>0$.
Each class has finite means and values above $p$.
The equilibrium tuple includes every $\sigma_j$, every raw posterior vector, and the posterior vector at each price.
The other components are those of \eqref{eq:tuple}.
Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:polytope}
 \mathcal M_\pi=\left\{\nu\in\Delta_J:
 \frac{\nu_j}{\pi_j}\le(1+u)\frac{\nu_l}{\pi_l}\ \text{for every }j,l\right\}.
\end{equation}
Every raw posterior belongs to this convex set.
Every price posterior also belongs to it because it is a mixture of raw posteriors.
The price identity recovers only the posterior mean of class proceeds:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:many-invert}
 \bar t_q=\sum_j\mu_j(q)t_j=t_0+\frac{P_q-t_0}{e(q)}.
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Two separated groups; analytical]\label{prop:groups}
Partition the classes into nonempty groups $L$ and $H$, with high-group prior mass $\bar\pi$.
The incumbent law strengthens from $F_0$ to $F_1$.
Suppose
\begin{align}\label{eq:groups}
 \max_jt_{j,0}-\min_jt_{j,0}&<k,\qquad \sum_j\pi_jg_{j,0}<c_H,\\
 \min_{j\in H}t_{j,1}-\max_{\nu\in\mathcal M_\pi}\nu\cdot t_1&>k/\rho,\nonumber\\
 \min_{\nu\in\mathcal M_\pi}\nu\cdot t_1-\max_{j\in L}t_{j,1}&>k/\rho.\nonumber
\end{align}
Let $\bar t_H,\bar t_L,\bar g_H,\bar g_L$ be prior-weighted means within each group at $F_1$.
Assume $\bar g_H>\bar g_L$ and
\[
 \bar g_L+M(\bar\pi)(\bar g_H-\bar g_L)>c_H.
\]
Under either challenger rule, weak orders are uniquely zero and entry is $\rho$.
Strong orders are uniquely $+1$ in group $H$ and $-1$ in group $L$.
Only the positive order recruits, so
\[
 \mathsf E_1=\rho+(1-\rho)(b+\bar\pi\lambda)>\rho.
\]

The strong price reveals the whole posterior vector in this equilibrium.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For any weak candidate, competitive prices subtract the raw mean proceeds from each class's proceeds.
Every residual has absolute value at most $\max_jt_{j,0}-\min_jt_{j,0}<k$.
Thus zero is the unique order in each class.
The complete weak tuple has all orders zero, posterior $\pi$, entry $\rho$, and price $t_{0,0}+\rho(\pi\cdot t_0-t_{0,0})$.
The cost inequality verifies entry, and direct substitution verifies competitive prices and every deviation.
The auction bids remain truthful.

For any strong candidate, \eqref{eq:many-invert} gives a class-$j$ payoff
\[
 U_j(q)=q e(q)[t_{j,1}-\mu(q)\cdot t_1]-k|q|.
\]
The separation inequalities make a high-group purchase strictly profitable and a high-group sale strictly unprofitable.
They make a low-group sale strictly profitable and a low-group purchase strictly unprofitable.
Zero pays zero.
These bounds hold under every candidate profile, so they force the stated group orders.

Under those orders, Bayes' rule gives the group posterior $(m(\bar\pi),\bar\pi,M(\bar\pi))$.
Within each group, class proportions remain at their prior proportions.

The posterior vector at group posterior $z$ is therefore
\[
 \mu_j(z)=
 \begin{cases}
 z\pi_j/\bar\pi,&j\in H,\\
 (1-z)\pi_j/(1-\bar\pi),&j\in L.
 \end{cases}
\]

The strict profit gap makes group gross profit increase with $z$.
Its prior value is below $c_H$ because incumbent strength lowers each class's profit.
Its value at $M(\bar\pi)$ exceeds $c_H$ by hypothesis.
Thus only the positive order recruits.
Set $P_q=t_{0,1}+e(z_q)[\bar t_L-t_{0,1}+(\bar t_H-\bar t_L)z_q]$.
The separation condition implies $\bar t_H>\bar t_L$.
The entry floor then makes the price strictly increase with $z$.

This verifies the price posterior, the preparation rule, and competitive prices for the fixed tuple.
The displayed payoff formula verifies every permitted deviation.
The bounds for every profile establish uniqueness.
\end{proof}

High-group ownership rises exactly when
\[
 [\rho+(1-\rho)(b+\lambda)]\sum_{j\in H}\pi_jW_j(F_1)
 >\rho\sum_{j\in H}\pi_jW_j(F_0).
\]
The group proof needs neither arbitrary posterior recovery nor a special relation between $g_j$ and $t_j$.

A strict four-class example uses equal priors and point values $(1,1001/1000,9999/1000,10)$.
It retains the benchmark costs, reserve, and first two strengths.
The certificate finds a separation distance $\gamma=\reg{p2_groups_gamma}$.
Its strict trade margin is $\rho\gamma-k=\reg{p2_groups_trade}$.
The weak range, weak cost, strong profit gap, and upper profit margins are also positive.

For this finite certificate, write $\nu_j=\pi_jw_j/\sum_l\pi_lw_l$ with $1\le w_j\le1+u$.
A linear fractional mean attains each extremum at endpoints of each weight interval.
Its derivative in one weight has constant sign, or is zero throughout that interval.
The script therefore evaluates all sixteen endpoint combinations.
This certifies the separation condition over the entire polytope.

\section{The payment rule}\label{sec:payment}

\begin{proposition}[Verifiable values and an enforceable fallback; analytical]\label{prop:payment}
Set the reserve to zero.
After preparation, values are verifiable and a sale to the next buyer at its value is enforceable.
Let the seller's share be $0\le\eta<1$.
With the winner's value $V$ and fallback $z\le V$, the transfer is $(1-\eta)z+\eta V$.
For point values $0<\ell<\bar r<h$ and incumbent support $[0,\bar r]$,
\begin{align}\label{eq:payment}
 T_\eta(R,\theta)&=(1-\eta)\min(R,\theta)+\eta\max(R,\theta),\\
 g_{\theta,\eta}&=(1-\eta)\E[(\theta-R)_+],\nonumber\\
 \Delta_\eta&=\eta(h-\ell)+(1-2\eta)\E[(R-\ell)_+].\nonumber
\end{align}
A stronger incumbent weakly lowers challenger profit.
Its effect on the spread has the sign of $1-2\eta$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The acquisition continuation specifies allocation to a buyer with maximal value and fallback allocation to the next buyer.
Without a challenger, it specifies fallback zero and transfer $\eta R$.

For $V>z$ and $0<\eta<1$, maximize
\[
 (P-z)^\eta(V-P)^{1-\eta}\quad\text{over }z\le P\le V.
\]
The logarithm has derivative $\eta/(P-z)-(1-\eta)/(V-P)$.
Its second derivative is strictly negative.
Its unique optimum solves $\eta(V-P)=(1-\eta)(P-z)$, so $P=(1-\eta)z+\eta V$.

At $V=z$, feasibility fixes the transfer.
At $\eta=0$, the declared continuous limit gives $P=z$.
The next buyer accepts the enforceable fallback at zero surplus.
These choices verify the allocation and transfer continuation after every value realization.

A challenger wins when $\theta>R$ and retains $(1-\eta)(\theta-R)$.
Its expected profit is the second line of \eqref{eq:payment}.

For $R\le\ell$, the difference between the high and low target payments is $\eta(h-\ell)$.
For $R>\ell$, that difference is $\eta(h-\ell)+(1-2\eta)(R-\ell)$.
Average these two cases to obtain the third line.

The function $(\theta-R)_+$ decreases with $R$.
The function $(R-\ell)_+$ increases with $R$.
Stochastic dominance gives the signs in the proposition.
The change is strict when the relevant expected positive part changes strictly and its coefficient is nonzero.
\end{proof}

At the benchmark strengths, exact integration gives
\[
\begin{array}{c|cc}
 &r_0=6/5&r_1=3\\\hline

\end{array}
\]
In particular,
\[
 \Delta_\eta(r_1)-\Delta_\eta(r_0)=\frac{13}{20}(1-2\eta).
\]
The sign boundary is exactly $\eta=1/2$.
This result specifies the acquisition continuation for every earlier history.

\subsection{General reserve comparisons}

\begin{proposition}[Strict reserve continuations; analytical]\label{prop:general-reserve}

\item If $B_0(\pi)<c_H<B_0(M)$ and $B_1(M)<c_H$, entry falls strictly:

\begin{proof}
