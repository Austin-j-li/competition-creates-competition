The short answer. The four points lead to additions, not to a new model. Every generalization contains the current model as a special case, and none changes the mechanism. The work: 15 research memos, separate derivations of each new result, numerical checks of every closed form, a brute-force check of 495,450 discrete cases, a reading of the literature from full texts, and a final adversarial review of 26 key claims. The review refuted no claim and corrected 6. The brute-force check corrected three claims about the discrete models; this report uses the corrected versions. 1Challenger value distributions

Alex: a two-point value with probability one half each is unconventional. Instead, let the challenger draw its value from one of a few distributions, and learn which one only when it receives information.

Two readings of "choosing from a few distributions"

ReadingWhat happensVerdict
N · nature drawsNature draws a class j ∈ {H, L} with prior π. Given the class, the value comes from the distribution Gj. The investor sees the class. Preparation reveals the value.Fits the words "only when they receive information" and the paper's story. The main line.
C · the challenger choosesThe challenger picks a plan (a risk profile) from a menu, and learns the realized value at diligence.If the challenger knows its own plan, the price teaches it nothing, and the mechanism stops. Only a hybrid survives.

We recommend Reading N. Alex should confirm which one he meant (see Questions for Alex).

Where Reading N sits in the literature

Auction-entry models have three standard cases. The bidder can know its value before it pays to enter (Samuelson 1985). It can know only the common distribution (Levin and Smith 1994). Or it can hold a signal that selects the distribution of its value: the selective-entry or affiliated-signal model (Marmer, Shneyerov and Xu 2013; Roberts and Sweeting 2013; Gentry and Li 2014). Alex's idea is the third case.

The paper fits this framework with one new feature: the pre-entry signal is the stock price. It is public, an outside investor produces it, and it is an equilibrium object. The current two-point model is the special case where each class has a single value. That is why it looks unusual to an auction reader. A binary state with prior one half is normal in feedback models (Edmans, Goldstein and Jiang 2015; Goldstein and Guembel 2008; Dow, Goldstein and Guembel 2017). It is not normal in auction-entry models.

Alex's remark about the second-price auction is correct PROVED. Each bidder bids its value whatever anyone knows, so asymmetric information changes only the entry decisions. A first-price auction would make bids, and so all payoffs, depend on public beliefs.

The neat result: the value gap is a fixed pie

In the second-price auction, the target's proceeds plus the challenger's profit equal the challenger's value: tθ + gθ = θ, at every incumbent strength. The reason: the total surplus is E[max{R, θ}] = θ + E[(R − θ)+], and the incumbent keeps exactly the second term. So the target and the challenger share exactly θ. Therefore

ΔT(r) + [gH(r) − gL(r)] = h − ℓ   for every r.

The gap between a good and a bad challenger is fixed. A stronger incumbent does not change its size. It moves the gap from the challenger's profit (what preparation earns) to the target's payoff (what informed investors trade on). This is the paper's "two claims on the same surplus" in one line. PROVED

With value distributions, the right-hand side becomes EH[max{θ, p}] − EL[max{θ, p}]. It still does not depend on the incumbent if both classes are equally likely to fall below the reserve, or if the incumbent's value is never below the reserve. PROVED

The main result extends

  • Any ranked pair works. Suppose GH first-order dominates GL above the reserve, both classes put the same mass below the reserve, and they differ somewhere above it. Then "competition creates competition" holds on a nonempty open set of primitives, at every prior, noise scale and low-cost probability. PROVED
  • Any prior works, at a cost. With prior π, the belief bounds become m(π) and M(π), and the threshold flow shifts by the prior log-odds. A prior away from one half narrows the trading-cost window in (A3). PROVED
  • The price still reveals the market's belief (Proposition A.2 is unchanged). PROVED
  • Numbers. Narrow classes around ℓ and h reproduce the benchmark: entry rises from 0.25 to 0.5228. A scan finds valid parameter pairs in 15 of 30 family and prior combinations. Three classes give 0.25 → 0.5228 → 0.25. COMPUTED

Two new effects

The reserve can reverse the sign. At a known value θ, the target's proceeds jump up at θ = p: a challenger that can just meet the reserve wins the sale when the incumbent cannot. A stronger incumbent shrinks that jump. If the low class is more likely to fall below the reserve, a stronger incumbent can lower the spread, and the main result can fail. PROVED

The paper's own high-reserve example (Section 6.3, reserve 1.1) is such a case: the spread falls from 1.0125 at r = 1.2 to 1.005 at r = 3. Both economies trade there, so the fall in preparation in Table 4 (0.540 → 0.512) is the ordinary deterrence of Proposition A.3. The paper's text is correct, but it does not give this reason. COMPUTED

A good challenger can now lose. When the two distributions overlap, a high-class challenger sometimes loses to the incumbent. More entry then does not always mean more high-value ownership. Example with all conditions met (ρ = 0.95): entry rises from 0.950 to 0.970, but high-class ownership falls from 0.4229 to 0.4165. The ownership claim needs one extra condition. COMPUTED

More than two classes, and an investor who sees the exact value

  • With three or more classes, the price reveals what the challenger needs if the class payoff pairs (target proceeds, challenger profit) lie on one line. In equilibrium, the investor's own trading incentives restore this under the paper's assumptions. The main result extends when classes form two separated groups. PROVED
  • If the investor sees the exact value (a continuum), a range of investors prefers not to trade. Existence and uniqueness are not resolved. OPEN

Reading C: the challenger chooses its plan

  • A useful auction lemma: challenger profit is convex in its value and target proceeds are concave. At a fixed mean, a riskier plan helps the challenger and hurts the target by the same amount. PROVED
  • A stronger incumbent has no signed effect on the taste for risk: it rises in one example and falls in another. COMPUTED
  • The hybrid (nature draws the class, the price informs, then the challenger picks risk) keeps the mechanism under one extra condition. Its equilibrium is not solved. OPEN

What to change in the paper

  1. Add the split identity to Section 3.1 (one sentence and one display). No cost.
  2. Add an appendix result "Distribution classes" with a general prior. Use the value classes of Section 6.3 as the example. Keep the binary model in the main text, and say that it is the point-mass case.
  3. Use the selective-entry vocabulary in Section 2.2: "quality class", "pre-entry signal", "preparation cost (entry cost in the auction literature)". Say "class", not "type", because "type" already names the investor's information.
  4. Add one sentence to Section 6.3 (lines 1040–1050): at reserve 1.1 the low class is excluded, the spread falls in r, and the fall in preparation is fixed-experiment deterrence.
  5. State the ownership condition separately from entry.

Open: how large a gap in reserve admissibility the result tolerates (on one family, a gap of 0.10 works and 0.20 does not); incumbent distributions other than the uniform; the widest symmetric classes; an investor who sees the exact value on a continuum; and the Reading C hybrid.

2Noise and the discrete model

Alex: Laplace noise is unconventional. Give a very clear technical and economic justification. Also try a purely discrete model, as in Edmans et al., with trading in one direction only.

What the proofs need from the noise

Property of the noiseWhat it gives the paperNeeds Laplace?
Bounded likelihood ratio between any two ordersBeliefs stay in [m, M] under every strategy, so the investor always keeps an edgeNo
Bounded slope of the log density, |(log f)′| ≤ 1/b < 1A lower bound on marginal trading profit over the whole order interval, hence uniquenessNo
Full supportNo off-path order flows, so no belief refinementNo
Log density exactly piecewise linearClosed forms (12), the belief plateau at M, the tie at rCYes

The first three rows hold for every density whose log has a slope bounded by a constant below one. This class includes the logistic and hyperbolic secant laws, Student t laws, and Gaussian noise plus an independent Laplace part. Proposition 2 holds for every member, with the same proof. Only one change: condition (A2) must use the largest belief that the law actually reaches. PROVED

The "clean linear posterior"

Under full orders, the Laplace log-odds are exactly the Gaussian log-odds (variance b) between the two orders, and flat outside. So Laplace keeps Kyle's linear update where it matters and caps it where Gaussian noise would make a flow conclusive.

Under Gaussian noise, a huge flow is almost proof of information. The investor's edge then vanishes at extreme flows, and the global argument fails. Numerically, full orders are not an equilibrium: the low type sells only 0.93. No equilibrium with informative trading can have a price that is linear in flow, for any full-support noise. PROVED

Why Laplace in particular: it is the extreme member

  • For a given slope bound 1/b, Laplace has the thinnest tails of the class. At the same orders, it gives the most informative price in Blackwell's sense. PROVED
  • The logistic law of Section 5.1 is the Laplace law plus independent noise. Euler's product gives πx/sinh(πx) = ∏n 1/(1 + x²/n²), and the n = 1 factor is the Laplace characteristic function. PROVED

This corrects the paper. Appendix A.4 (main.tex lines 1877–1880) says that neither Laplace nor logistic noise Blackwell-dominates the other. At the common scale that the paper uses, Laplace dominates. Only at a common variance does neither law dominate. Online Appendix C.5 (lines 2631–2632) needs the same change. FIX

Economic justification

Three simple stories give Laplace noise exactly PROVED:

  1. Buy and sell volumes. Liquidity sales and liquidity purchases are independent exponential amounts with the same mean. Their difference is Laplace.
  2. Random volume. Noise demand is Gaussian, but its variance is random and exponential (for example, a random number of liquidity traders).
  3. Random horizon. Noise demand follows a random walk that stops at an exponential time, such as the next public event in the sale process. This story fits a sale process best.

The evidence supports fat tails relative to the Gaussian, with an exponential body at medium horizons. It does not show that noise demand itself is Laplace, because noise demand is not observable. We found no Kyle-type or feedback paper that assumes Laplace noise. The same property is standard in the learning literature as "bounded private beliefs" (Smith and Sørensen 2000). It also defines the Laplace mechanism in differential privacy.

Suggested text. "The model needs two properties of the noise: no order flow is conclusive, and a small change in an order moves the flow density only in proportion to its level. Gaussian noise is the common law that fails both. Laplace is the extreme member of the class of laws that have them, and it gives closed forms. Proposition 2 holds for the whole class."

A risk to address: the size of entry depends on the law

Laplace gives the largest entry of all the laws we computed. This is a numerical fact, not a consequence of Blackwell dominance: a more informative price does not always give more entry, because entry depends on whether the belief crosses a threshold. A referee may say the law was chosen to make the result large. Answer it in advance: report a second member next to the logistic number in Section 5.1, and say that the direction of the result does not depend on the law. COMPUTED

The discrete model

The template is Edmans, Goldstein and Jiang (2015, AER). A noise trader buys, sells, or does not trade one unit, each with probability 1/3. The informed investor trades one unit or stays out, and is present only with probability λ. Market makers see total flow and price at expected value. We built a buy-only version (short sales shut off) and a sell-only version. The challenger still sees only the price.

A new problem from the brute-force check: self-fulfilling prices. The challenger sees only the price. Two flows can then share one price, and a lower entry decision at that price can confirm itself. With the two-sided menu at r = 3 and λ = 0.8, two price schedules support the same full trades, with entry 0.467 and 0.417. Both satisfy the paper's tie rules. This happens for λ between about 0.79 and 0.88. A low-cost floor cL ≤ gL removes it. This is why Edmans, Goldstein and Jiang let the decision maker see order flow, not the price. COMPUTED

The check covered 495,450 cases across the three menus, five presence levels, two low costs, 45 trading costs and a fine grid of strengths. With equal noise, every case has an equilibrium. With unequal noise (0.1, 0.2, 0.7), 13,884 of 126,900 cases have no equilibrium under the paper's tie rule. The buy-only menu showed no extra price pool at the benchmark points.

What the discrete model costs.

  • Beliefs can reach 0 and 1. The extreme flows reveal the state. So the cheap challenger stays out after clearly bad news unless cL ≤ gL. The benchmark cL = 1 fails this, and benchmark entry moves 0.25 → 1/3 (not 0.375).
  • Part (iii) needs a much stronger incumbent. A belief of exactly 1 keeps expensive entry alive until gH < cH, that is, r > 7.97, not 3.6.
  • No feedback from entry to trading. With equal noise, the flows that bring in the challenger never pay the trader. This removes the coexistence result of Proposition 3 and the tail comparison of Section 5.1. Unequal noise brings feedback back, together with multiple equilibria, and under the paper's tie rule some cases have no equilibrium.
  • An always-present investor is degenerate. Every flow then reveals the state or says nothing. Use λ < 1, as Edmans, Goldstein and Jiang do.

Recommendation. Keep Laplace (stated as a class) as the main model. Add the buy-only discrete model, with equal noise and λ < 1, as a short companion. State the low-cost floor that rules out self-fulfilling prices. The companion shows that the main result does not need Laplace noise. It also shows what continuous noise adds: informative middle flows, hence feedback and coexistence.

3Who the investors are, and the role of t0

Alex: t0 is the target's proceeds when no one enters. Who are the investors? If they are target shareholders, should they not gain from the price change anyway? If they are outsiders, who can they be? Does the model need a new device?

Who is who

GroupIn the modelIn practice

The kind of information matters. Most documented informed trading before deals is about whether a bid will come (Meulbroek 1992; Augustin, Brenner and Subrahmanyam 2019). That trading is illegal, and it is the wrong information for this model: the challenger already knows what its own lenders and advisers know. The model needs information about the challenger's fit with the target, which outside research can produce.

Where t0 goes

Both t0 and the public entry effect cancel from the investor's trading edge (eq. 11). t0 behaves like the deterministic dividend of Section 6.1: it moves the value and the price by the same amount. What matters is the entry gain tθ − t0, which is positive for both challenger types. So every holder of the shares wants entry, even with a low-value challenger. PROVED

Do target shareholders gain?

Yes, but from entry, not from the price change. A shareholder who keeps the shares receives the sale proceeds, whatever the interim price. The price change itself is a transfer between traders. PROVED

  • The gain needs three conditions: the region of Proposition 2, where the prior alone does not justify expensive entry; price access; and a float that is not too small relative to the trade size (the investor's profit scales with trade size; at the benchmark, the class gains while the float is at least 1/1.832 ≈ 0.55 trading units). COMPUTED
  • The total hides a split. Holders who keep their shares gain and pay nothing. Shareholders who must trade pay the informed investor's profit. At an even split of the float, the trading group loses 0.012 while the class gains 0.117. COMPUTED
  • The sign can flip. When the prior already justifies expensive entry, an informative price deters some entry and lowers proceeds (by 0.19 to 0.25 at r1), although acquisition surplus rises. COMPUTED

If the informed investor is a shareholder: a stake

Give the investor ω shares before it trades. Its payoff becomes ωVT + q(VT − P) − k|q|. Because entry raises proceeds in both states, a stake rewards a purchase even after bad news: the investor may buy to pull in the challenger. This is a manipulation motive.

  • Weak economy: when the price is uninformative, one order cannot move entry, so no trade is an equilibrium for every stake. Its uniqueness is proved only for a stake below 0.043. Part (iii) at r2 is unaffected. PROVED
  • Strong economy: unique full orders are guaranteed for a stake below 0.0114 trading units at the benchmark. The slack in (A3) is thin (0.0024). PROVED
  • Numerically, full orders survive up to a stake of about 2.16 units. A branch with partial sales continues to about 2.50. COMPUTED
  • Large stakes: both types buy the same amount cannot be an equilibrium. The full equilibrium is not resolved. OPEN

This differs from Edmans, Goldstein and Jiang (2015, Section II.E). There the stake cancels, because the firm's action corrects course in both states. Here it does not cancel, because a sale on bad news deters entry, and entry raises the value in both states.

Recommended device. Keep the outside specialist with no net position, and name it. Read the noise as liquidity sales by shareholders minus liquidity purchases by outsiders. With exponential amounts, this is the Laplace noise of eq. (2). No proof changes. Add the stake as an extension with the thresholds above. Two draft paragraphs (for Sections 2.3 and 6.1) are in the memo Q3_story.md.

10How this was done, and the memos

Each new analytical result was derived at least twice, by separate members who did not coordinate. Every closed form was checked against direct numerical integration. An independent brute-force solver checked the discrete models. A separate reader opened the key papers in full and wrote quoted extracts, which corrected the citations. A final review checked the high-stakes claims again. Scripts only compute and print; no file of the paper was changed.
