# Deal Terms in Takeovers: the overlap that matters

30 October 2026. Research and source checks by Astra. No delegation.

**Read: full March 1, 2026 draft**, including the introduction, data, model, tables, and appendices. Authors: Alexander S. Gorbenko, Brett Green, and Shreye Mirani. The draft covers 673 completed US mergers from 2015–2021. Page references below use the page numbers printed in the [draft](https://www.brettgreen.info/drafts/deal_terms.pdf).

**Read: October 30, 2026 seminar abstract.** The [HKU abstract](https://www.hkubs.hku.hk/event/deal-terms-in-takeovers/) reports 820 completed mergers from 2015–2022. It reports a higher-price loss rate of 22%, compared with 21% in March. I did not obtain the corresponding later draft. The detailed scope assessment below applies to March; the later abstract does not establish its full scope.

The paper already collects much of the private process proposed for the shortlist. Its central contribution is the target's valuation of non-price terms. Its model uses the target's choice among offers to recover those values. It is not a full dynamic model of all bidder and seller actions.

Appendix B.2 instead assumes such commitment and obtains additional rankings among offers. It does **not** estimate how much commitment each target possesses. Two additional boundaries matter. Table 10, page 36, concerns time from announcement to completion, not time under private exclusivity. Table 11, page 37, concerns returns around withdrawn transactions, not returns at the first merger proxy. Neither table supplies the proposed outcome for E or H.

## Verdict convention and access limits

**Open** means that I found no direct predecessor for the specified empirical object in this search. It does not prove universal absence. **Partly occupied** means that existing work answers a substantial component, but a stated question remains. **Occupied** would mean that the proposed central question and main evidence already appear together.

**Read** means that I opened a full text or a paper abstract in the shared browser. Each entry states which version I read. **Unverified** means that I did not obtain either for that version. Search results alone do not receive a read label.

SSRN returned security-verification pages for Szydlowski and the current Kravet–Xu draft. I did not bypass those pages. I read Szydlowski's own research summary and an earlier Kravet–Xu conference abstract. These limits matter, especially for H. An AEA conference endpoint returned a download-start error; I stopped that route and used the inline AFA copy of Liu–Officer–Tu.

## A. Seller commitment — partly occupied

**Deciding fact:** Deal Terms already distinguishes a deadline-based final round from a revision round and explicitly changes the commitment assumption.

The observation that final bids can change is therefore occupied. The potentially new object is the effect of credible deadline enforcement on entry, initial offers, and seller revenue. A higher eventual price does not measure that effect. Bidders may alter their earlier offers when they expect another round.

Closest papers:

1. **Gorbenko, Green, and Mirani, Deal Terms in Takeovers (2026). Read, full March draft.** It models target choices under alternative assumptions about exclusion between formal rounds. It does not estimate deadline credibility or its equilibrium revenue cost. [Source](https://www.brettgreen.info/drafts/deal_terms.pdf), page 13 and Appendix B.2.
2. **Liu, Mierendorff, Shi, and Zhong, Auctions with Limited Commitment (AER, 2019). Read, full September 2018 draft.** It characterizes revenue when a seller can set today's reserve but cannot commit to future reserves. It supplies theory, not takeover evidence or an estimated adviser reputation. [Source](https://www.columbia.edu/~wz2269/workingpapers/limited_commitment/auction_limited_commitment_final_main.pdf).
3. **Hui Liu, Yao Luo, and Ruli Xiao, Dynamic Bidding under Limited Commitment: Evidence from Judicial Sales (IIOC, 2026). Read, conference abstract.** It estimates a dynamic auction model with seller noncommitment and quantifies inefficiency in judicial sales. Its public reserve reductions after failed sales differ from private takeover deadlines, but it defeats a first-estimate-in-any-market claim. [Source](https://editorialexpress.com/conference/IIOC2026/program/IIOC2026.html), session 20. The abstract, not a full paper, supports this assessment.
4. **Deng and Zhou, Recurring Auctions with Costly Entry: Theory and Evidence. Read, full October 19, 2025 draft.** It estimates foreclosure auctions and finds that repeated rounds can improve revenue and efficiency through sorted entry. Its mechanism allows beneficial repeat rounds, so a reopened sale need not reveal costly noncommitment. [Source](https://www.shanglyudeng.com/assets/papers/Recurring_Auctions_v8_final_submission.pdf).
5. **Larsen, The Efficiency of Real-World Bargaining: Evidence from Wholesale Used-Auto Auctions (ReStud, 2021). Read, NBER abstract.** It estimates or bounds values from auction sequences followed by bilateral negotiation and measures unrealized gains from trade. It does not isolate the cost of a seller's broken deadline. [Source](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20431).
6. **Golubov, Petmezas, and Travlos, When It Pays to Pay Your Investment Banker (JF, 2012). Read, AFA abstract.** It links top-tier advisers to better bidder outcomes in public acquisitions. It concerns adviser quality broadly, rather than a measured reputation for deadline enforcement. [Source](https://afajof.org/issue/volume-67-issue-1/).

The search covered takeover deadlines, adviser reputation, repeated property auctions, used goods, procurement, and spectrum auctions. I found no verified takeover study that estimates adviser-specific deadline credibility. I also found no exact procurement or spectrum counterpart to that object. These are bounded search results, not claims that those literatures contain no commitment problems.

**What remains:** a credible estimate of the anticipatory cost of soft deadlines, with an independent project boundary. The judicial-sales paper means that the contribution must come from the takeover mechanism and evidence, not merely structural estimation under limited commitment. The synthesis's pilot price increases do not supply that estimate.

## B. Process leaks — partly occupied

**Deciding fact:** Fidrmuc and Xia already align private initiation and first-NDA dates from merger backgrounds with trades and stock returns.

This fails the synthesis's proposed broad novelty test. The dates are not a new bridge between private processes and public markets. The narrower opportunity concerns each additional party's access, option-market information, and the seller's response to leak risk.

Closest papers:

1. **Fidrmuc and Xia, Target Insiders' Preferences When Trading Before Takeover Announcements: Deal Completion Probability, Premium and Deal Characteristics (EFM, 2022). Read, full accepted manuscript.** It combines 1,802 US targets with private initiation dates, first confidentiality agreements, and insiders' trades. It does not identify the incremental leak effect of each rival's NDA or the causal effect of leak risk on invitations. [Source](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/150697/1/WRAP-Target-insiders-preferences-trading-before-takeover-announcement-2021.pdf), introduction, section 3.1, and Table 1.
2. **Acharya and Johnson, More Insiders, More Insider Trading: Evidence from Private-Equity Buyouts (JFE, 2010). Read, full January 2009 draft.** It relates suspicious activity in several securities markets to the number of equity and debt finance participants. Its party counts describe finance syndicates, rather than the sequence of rival buyers under NDA. [Source](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~sternfin/vacharya/public_html/acharya_johnson.pdf).
3. **Szydlowski, Leaks and Takeovers (working paper). Unverified paper; author's 2020 research summary read.** The author describes a model in which a target leaks synergy information to recruit another bidder, with effects on initiation and competition. It already contains feedback from leaks to the sale process, but supplies no verified empirical NDA-date design here. [Author's summary](https://clsbluesky.law.columbia.edu/2020/01/27/leaks-and-takeovers/); [paper, access blocked](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3511097).
4. **Liu, Officer, and Tu, Negotiation, Auction, or Negotiauction?! Evidence from the Field (December 2023). Read, full draft.** It records private initiation, the winner's confidentiality date, participant counts, and successive offers in 841 deals. It does not estimate stock or option information arrival at each participant's access date. [Source](https://afajof.org/management/viewp.php?n=36876), data section and Appendix A.

Fidrmuc–Xia studies reported trades by target insiders. It does not directly identify illegal leaks by deal participants. Table 1 also reports abnormal target returns across the initiation-to-NDA and NDA-to-announcement stages. Thus, a stock-return version with those same two dates needs more than a different dependent variable. Their first agreement is also not necessarily every participant's first access event.

**What remains:** the marginal information cost of an additional informed rival, ideally separate from deal progress and finance-syndicate size. I found no verified paper that links the full sequence of rival NDA dates to option activity. Nor did I find causal evidence that expected market leaks change invitation breadth. Szydlowski already supplies a related theoretical channel; the missing contribution is its precise empirical counterpart.

## C. Standstills — partly occupied

DADW means “don't ask, don't waive.”

**Deciding fact:** the auction-commitment rationale for DADW already exists, but I found no verified causal study of its participation or price effects after the 2012 decisions.

Closest papers:

1. **Sautter, Auction Theory and Standstills: Dealing with Friends and Foes in a Sale of Corporate Control (2013). Read, full repository manuscript.** It analyzes DADW, bidder type, auction incentives, fiduciary exceptions, and the 2012 Delaware decisions. It is a legal and economic analysis, rather than a causal estimate from bidder-level process data. [Source](https://digitalcommons.law.lsu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1025&context=faculty_scholarship), especially sections III–IV.
2. **Choi and Talley, A Defense of Shareholder Favoritism (2001). Read, full technical draft.** It formally models side payments and standstill agreements between managers and a block shareholder. Its standstill buys a blockholder's restraint, rather than controls a modern NDA signatory's right to return to a sale. [Source](https://escholarship.org/content/qt6pw546ph/qt6pw546ph_noSplash_0ed8392128fcd009803d46c291d62bb8.pdf?t=krn8lg), sections 4–5.
3. **Dann and DeAngelo, Standstill Agreements, Privately Negotiated Stock Repurchases, and the Market for Corporate Control (JFE, 1983). Unverified, indexed publisher abstract only.** The abstract reports adverse shareholder wealth effects around standstills and premium repurchases. Those blockholder arrangements do not establish the effect of auction DADW clauses or the 2012 decisions. [Publisher source](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-405X%2883%2990014-4).

There is therefore a formal standstill model beyond Sautter. It is not a close model of the proposed modern clause. The separate empirical question remains plausible.

**Legal boundary, read primary transcript:** Ancestry does not announce a universal DADW ban. The court discusses the clause's possible role in a final auction round and insists on informed, proper use. See the [December 17, 2012 transcript](https://www.rlf.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/6454_In-re-Ancestry.com_.pdf), pages 223–229, especially 225–228. Sautter also explains the narrower Complete Genomics intervention. A single uniform treatment date for all Delaware standstills would misstate this history.

**What remains:** clause-specific changes in access, return bids, and prices, with evidence on waiver rights and actual enforcement. Mere NDA counts cannot supply the treatment. A credible design must distinguish ordinary standstills, DADW restrictions, fall-away clauses, and waivers.

## D. Informative exits — open

**Deciding fact:** no paper I verified estimates how one private bidder's exit changes other bidders' behavior after a common diligence event.

The open claim is about information in the timing and sequence of exits. It is not a claim to discover bidder attrition, common values, or learning during due diligence.

Closest papers:

1. **Gorbenko and Malenko, Strategic and Financial Bidders in Takeover Auctions (JF, 2014). Read, full paper.** It uses private bids and participation, with discussion of exit, return, and flexible sale procedures. It does not estimate clustered exit hazards around data-room access or responses to another bidder's withdrawal. [Source](https://www.mit.edu/~amalenko/stratfin.pdf), data discussion.
2. **Liu, Officer, and Tu, Negotiation, Auction, or Negotiauction?! (2023). Read, full draft.** It documents bidder attrition, transitions from auctions to bilateral talks, and the relation between process paths and final premiums. It does not isolate an information spillover from one exit to another bidder's decision. [Source](https://afajof.org/management/viewp.php?n=36876).
3. **Daley, Geelen, and Green, Due Diligence (JF, 2024). Read, full author manuscript.** It models information acquisition, delay, transaction execution, and termination under costly diligence. It does not document cross-bidder exit clusters in actual takeover processes. [Source](https://thomasgeelen.com/assets/articles/duediligence.pdf).
4. **Cusumano and Pieroth, Due Diligence in Common Value Auctions (October 2024). Read, full job-market draft.** It studies the seller's choice of information access before bids or after a winner emerges. It provides a common-value benchmark, not evidence that private exits transmit information among rival buyers. [Source](https://economics.ucr.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/1-24-25-Pieroth.pdf).
5. **Carlin, Liu, Officer, Pernoud, and Tu, Bidder Pools in Mergers and Acquisitions (2026). Read, NBER abstract.** It links correlated bidder values to optimal invitation counts and target surplus. Its unit of choice is the invited pool, rather than the timing of informed exits within that pool. [Source](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34846).

**What remains:** distinguish shared bad news from bidder-specific constraints and from the loss of competition. A cluster after a presentation may reflect the same information or the same response deadline. It does not, by itself, show that bidders learn from one another. A spillover claim also needs evidence that the remaining bidders knew about the exit.

Deal Terms adds direct overlap in diligence conditions and losing offers, but not the stated event-level outcome. This is the clearest unoccupied empirical object in the verified set. Its data and identification burden remains substantial.

## E. The price after exclusivity — partly occupied

**Deciding fact:** Liu–Officer–Tu already record actual exclusivity agreements and downward bid revisions, but do not anchor the price comparison at exclusivity's start.

Closest papers:

1. **Liu, Officer, and Tu, Negotiation, Auction, or Negotiauction?! (2023). Read, full draft.** It records whether the winner receives exclusivity, studies successive offers, and reports that about 17% of bid revisions reduce price. Its reported comparisons do not identify the winner's last bid immediately before exclusivity or relate price changes to actual exclusivity length. [Source](https://afajof.org/management/viewp.php?n=36876), pages 12–13, 24–25, and Tables 5, 7–8.
2. **Gorbenko, Green, and Mirani, Deal Terms in Takeovers (2026). Read, full March draft.** It values exclusivity requests as bid attributes and records revised formal offers. A request or requested duration does not establish the actual start, extension, or end of exclusive talks. [Source](https://www.brettgreen.info/drafts/deal_terms.pdf), page 8 and Table 12.
3. **Daley, Geelen, and Green, Due Diligence (JF, 2024). Read, full author manuscript.** It studies the buyer's information option after an offer is accepted, with effects on price, delay, and completion. Its baseline accepted price remains fixed; it does not estimate opportunistic price cuts during exclusive talks. [Source](https://thomasgeelen.com/assets/articles/duediligence.pdf), sections I–II.
4. **Higbee, Jennejohn, Jones, and Talley, Fix the Price or Price the Fix? Resolving the Sequencing Puzzle in Corporate Acquisitions (February 21, 2026). Read, full draft.** It combines practitioner evidence, a contract-search model, and legal variation to explain why headline prices become sticky before non-price terms. It does not estimate the specified exclusivity-date price path, but its evidence directly challenges a presumption of routine price cuts. [Source](https://joshuadhigbee.github.io/files/Fix_the_Price_or_Price_the_Fix.pdf), introduction and practitioner survey.

**What remains:** the distribution of price changes from the last genuine pre-exclusivity offer, with completed and abandoned exclusive negotiations distinguished. Neither a first final-round bid nor a first indicative offer is the required baseline. A duration correlation alone cannot separate buyer leverage from new adverse information or a difficult contract.

I found no verified paper that estimates this exact comparison and its relation to actual exclusivity length. The result could be price stability or increases. A paper should explain the outcome rather than assume that exclusivity causes retrading.

## F. Antitrust selects the buyer — partly occupied

**Deciding fact:** Deal Terms already studies regulatory conditions in private winning and losing bids and values their role in target choice.

Closest papers:

1. **Gorbenko, Green, and Mirani, Deal Terms in Takeovers (2026). Read, full March draft.** It estimates how regulatory and other contractual conditions affect the target's ranking of actual offers. It does not identify the antitrust-driven absence of potential buyers before an NDA or offer. [Source](https://www.brettgreen.info/drafts/deal_terms.pdf), sections 3–5.
2. **Nocke and Whinston, Merger Policy with Merger Choice (AER, 2013). Read, journal abstract.** It models optimal antitrust policy when firms choose among alternative mergers. It establishes endogenous merger selection in theory, without observing private bidder entry within a particular target sale. [Source](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.103.2.1006).
3. **de Bodt, Cousin, Officer, and Roll, The Deterrence Effect of M&A Regulatory Enforcements (JCF, 2024). Read, publisher-supplied abstract on RePEc.** It finds that DOJ and FTC interventions deter later acquisition activity among product-market competitors. Its unit is subsequent market activity, rather than the replacement of a strategic bidder by another buyer within one private sale. [Abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/corfin/v85y2024ics092911992400021x.html); [publisher](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S092911992400021X).

**What remains:** direct evidence that regulatory risk changes the feasible buyer set before public announcement. The sharp object is a buyer who declines, exits, or never receives an invitation, followed by a different allocation. Regressions of winner type on regulatory conditions would face substantial overlap with Deal Terms.

A regulatory-condition flag also need not measure actual antitrust risk. The condition may allocate risk differently across otherwise similar bidders. The new contribution requires buyer-specific exposure and process evidence, not simply the presence of a contractual condition.

## H. The proxy reveals the auction — partly occupied

**Deciding fact:** Kravet and Xu already study market responses to merger-proxy disclosure; the disclosed runner-up's option to return remains the narrower possible contribution.

Closest papers:

1. **Kravet and Xu, Are Merger Proxy Filings Informative? (2023); later titled Proprietary Costs, Litigation Risk, and the Informativeness of Merger Proxy Filings (2026). Read, 2023 conference abstract; unverified, full 2026 version.** The earlier abstract examines returns and bid–ask spreads around proxy disclosure, and the current indexed abstract retains a market-information question. Neither accessible abstract establishes a runner-up-bid analysis, but the current full paper remains an important unresolved overlap. [Read abstract](https://aaahq.org/portals/0/Spark%20Abstracts.pdf), session 6.02; [current draft record](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4556788).
2. **Guo, Liu, and Tu, Neglected Peers in Merger Valuations (RFS, 2023). Read, full August 2021 draft.** It links disclosed valuation comparables to later returns and acquisition outcomes, with evidence of limited investor attention. Its disclosed objects are comparable firms, rather than losing offers, live rival options, or the target's merger-arbitrage spread. [Read draft](https://portal.northernfinanceassociation.org/viewp.php?n=2240058268); [publication](https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article/36/8/3257/6984732).
3. **Gorbenko, Green, and Mirani, Deal Terms in Takeovers (2026). Read, full March draft.** It shows that losing offers can exceed the accepted price because their non-price terms differ. It does not study how the first public disclosure of those offers changes market beliefs or forecasts a later bid. [Source](https://www.brettgreen.info/drafts/deal_terms.pdf), page 10 and Table 5.

The broad claim that merger proxies reveal useful information is occupied. The exact PREM14A or SC 14D9 event may differ from earlier proxy samples. That distinction requires a first-disclosure check, not just a new form label. An earlier S-4, preliminary proxy, press report, or amendment can move the information date.

**What remains:** whether a newly disclosed, feasible rival offer changes the target's spread and predicts a later competing offer. A historical runner-up price alone is insufficient. Its conditions, withdrawal, finance capacity, and standstill status determine whether the rival can return.

The CCC channel also needs market prices to affect the rival's subsequent action. An event return alone establishes neither that feedback nor new rival entry. Do not claim that H is clear of prior work until the current Kravet–Xu paper is available.

## I. The private-equity floor — partly occupied

**Deciding fact:** Gorbenko and Malenko already relate credit conditions to financial bidders' inferred private valuations, using losing bids as well as winning bids.

Closest papers:

1. **Gorbenko and Malenko, Strategic and Financial Bidders in Takeover Auctions (JF, 2014). Read, full paper.** It associates wider credit spreads with lower financial-bidder valuations, with no significant equivalent coefficient for strategic bidders. It does not identify credit pass-through to a strategic winner specifically through an observed PE runner-up. [Source](https://www.mit.edu/~amalenko/stratfin.pdf), pages 2514–2517 and 2535.
2. **Martos-Vila, Rhodes-Kropf, and Harford, Financial versus Strategic Buyers (JFQA, 2019). Read, NBER working-paper abstract.** It links debt misvaluation to the relative prevalence of financial and strategic acquirers through governance and coinsurance differences. Its evidence concerns ownership and buyer type, rather than the offer of a particular losing PE bidder. [Source](https://www.nber.org/papers/w19378).
3. **Axelson, Jenkinson, Strömberg, and Weisbach, Borrow Cheap, Buy High? The Determinants of Leverage and Pricing in Buyouts (JF, 2013). Read, NBER abstract.** It links broad credit conditions to buyout leverage and prices. It concerns PE acquisitions, rather than a strategic buyer's payment when PE loses. [Source](https://www.nber.org/papers/w15952).
4. **Gorbenko, Green, and Mirani, Deal Terms in Takeovers (2026). Read, full March draft.** It records bidder type and finance conditions and shows that the highest cash price can lose. Its evidence means that the highest-price PE loser need not set the competitive constraint on a strategic winner. [Source](https://www.brettgreen.info/drafts/deal_terms.pdf), sections 3–5.

**What remains:** transmission from a credit shock through a feasible PE rival into a strategic winner's price or entry decision. The observed runner-up is an outcome of credit conditions, not a fixed treatment group. General credit conditions also affect strategic financing, target fundamentals, and the target's outside option.

I found no verified paper that estimates the exact PE-runner-up pass-through. The claim that credit affects PE demand is already well occupied, including within private takeover auctions. A useful contribution must identify the effect on another buyer or on entry within the same sale.

## J. Competition for the record — partly occupied

**Deciding fact:** prior work studies legal scrutiny and sale intensity, while Go-Shops Revisited directly separates nominal market checks from effective opportunities to compete.

Closest papers:

1. **Cain, Griffith, Jackson, and Davidoff Solomon, Does Revlon Matter? An Empirical and Theoretical Study (California Law Review, 2020). Read, full ECGI reprint.** It studies 1,913 transactions and links Revlon scrutiny to private competition, negotiation intensity, and shareholder outcomes. Its evidence extends beyond contact counts, but does not reconstruct each bidder's effective access window after Corwin and Trulia. [Source](https://www.ecgi.global/sites/default/files/working_papers/documents/caingriffithjacksonsolomonfinal_0.pdf).
2. **Gatti and Gelter, Barking without Biting: How Corwin Did Not Change M&A (October 2024). Read, full draft.** It studies C&J and Corwin through premiums, deal protections, auctions, go-shops, and other process measures. Its auction indicator records active solicitation, while its bad-process indicator records the absence of both a pre-market check and a go-shop. [Source](https://www.ecgi.global/sites/default/files/2024-10/barking-without-biting_0.pdf), Table 1 and process analyses.
3. **Subramanian and Zhao, Go-Shops Revisited (Harvard Law Review, 2020). Read, full author draft.** It documents weaker go-shop performance and examines match rights, effective time limits, banker conduct, and management conflicts. Its direct evidence concerns post-signing go-shops rather than a causal Corwin–Trulia analysis of pre-signing bidder access. [Source](https://laweconcenter.law.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Subramanian_1010.pdf), sections I–II.

The proposition that a formal market check can lack substance is occupied. Gatti–Gelter also makes a broad claim that Corwin weakened sale effort difficult to sustain without new evidence. Its limited effects do not prove that every bidder received an unchanged opportunity.

**What remains:** a bidder-level comparison between documented contact and a genuine chance to bid. Relevant evidence includes access to information, time before the deadline, permission to contact finance sources, and the right to improve an offer. This is more precise than contact counts or a go-shop indicator, but the precision must answer a new economic question.

Corwin and Trulia should not be treated as one legal event. The former concerns judicial review after a qualifying shareholder vote; the latter concerns disclosure-only settlements. Any joint design must distinguish the incentives they change. The verified papers do not supply the exact bidder-access comparison across both decisions.

## Implications for the shortlist

The broad novelty claims for B and H need revision. A's direct overlap with the supervision team is stronger than a shared dataset. E needs the actual exclusivity date and must allow price stability as its main fact. F and I need entry or cross-bidder transmission, beyond valuation differences among submitted offers. J needs a new measure of effective access with an economic consequence.

D retains the clearest open empirical object in the verified set. C retains a plausible clause-specific empirical question. Neither verdict establishes feasibility or a causal design. No project data extraction, estimation, model derivation, or test was performed for this report.
