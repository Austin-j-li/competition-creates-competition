# The big question: job-market paper candidates in M&A

No browser or web search was used, per the role brief. References that the phase 0 dossier does not list are marked "unverified". Scores are provisional. O = Originality, I = Importance, P = Practicality, F = Placement power and fit. Each score runs from 0 to 10.

Test for every idea: a faculty member hears the talk once. One week later, the faculty member can still state the question and the answer in a corridor.

## 1. Longlist

1. **Every Contact Leaks** — the seller creates its own price run-up when it invites bidders, and the run-up deters the entry the seller wants. O9 I9 P7 F10. Structural: yes.
2. **The Auction Ends Before the Price Is Set** — the winner cuts its price after the target grants exclusivity and sends the other bidders home. O9 I9 P8 F8. Structural: yes.
3. **Thirty Suitors, One Bid** — many bidders sign, read the same data room, and leave together, so the count of signatures overstates competition. O8 I9 P7 F9. Structural: yes.
4. **The Bidder Who Was Never Called** — the seller loses more from bidders it never invites than from weak bids by bidders it invites. O9 I9 P5 F8. Structural: yes.
5. **He Who Knocks Loses** — a bidder that starts a sale reveals its synergy, the target shops the bid, and the first bidder loses or overpays, so good sales never start. O7 I9 P5 F7. Structural: yes.
6. **Deadlines That Move** — a target that extends a bid deadline tells each bidder that no rival waits, and later bids fall. O7 I7 P8 F7. Structural: yes.
7. **Go-Shops That Do Not Shop** — the go-shop buys court protection for the board, not a second bid, and the first bidder charges for the window in its first price. O6 I7 P8 F6. Structural: yes.
8. **The Auctioneer Is Paid to Close** — the bank earns its fee on completion, so the bank runs the process for speed and certainty, not for the last dollar of price. O7 I8 P6 F6. Structural: no.
9. **Diligence as Espionage** — some strategic bidders enter to read a rival's books under a confidentiality agreement, then leave and compete with what they learned. O10 I8 P3 F6. Structural: no.
10. **Management Picks Its Next Boss** — managers steer the sale to the buyer that keeps their jobs and offers rollover equity, and the steering costs shareholders price. O7 I9 P4 F6. Structural: yes.

## 2. Top 3 pitches

### Pitch 1: Every Contact Leaks

**Question:** How much of the target's price run-up before announcement does the sale process itself cause, and does that run-up then deter bidder entry?

**One-sentence surprising answer:** A wide sale process can lower the final premium, because each new contact leaks the sale to the market and raises the price a new bidder must beat.

**30-second corridor story:** A board contacts forty buyers to create competition. Bankers, lawyers, and fund staff learn the target is for sale. The stock rises for weeks with no public news. The marginal buyer now must pay a premium on a price that already pays for the sale, so it stays out. The board created competition on paper and taxed it in the market.

**Why business schools care:** Boards, courts, and bankers treat a long contact list as proof of a good process. Delaware cases ask whether the board shopped the firm. No one prices the leak that the shopping itself creates. The answer changes a daily board choice: how many buyers to call, and in what order.

**The new fact or result:** Abnormal returns, volume, and option demand step up at the dates when the target contacts new waves of bidders, weeks before any announcement or press rumour. The step grows in the number of new contacts. Deals with a large process-made run-up then draw fewer new bidders after the run-up, and pay a lower premium over the pre-process price than a narrow process pays.

**Model:** The seller chooses how many bidders to contact, and when. Each bidder has a synergy value and a cost to prepare a bid. Each contact leaks to traders with some probability. A leak raises the stock price. The board and the fairness opinion judge any later bid against the new, higher price, so a later bidder needs a higher synergy to enter and win. Key primitives: the distribution of synergy values, the preparation cost, the leak probability per contact, and the weight the board puts on the current stock price as a floor. The seller trades the best value among more bidders against a higher floor for all bidders.

**Data:** The sale-process census gives dated contacts, confidentiality agreements, and bid dates for thousands of US public targets. CRSP daily prices and volume give returns around each contact wave. Single-stock option volume and open interest add a second leak measure; that history is not yet pulled and its licence is unverified, so the paper must work on stock data alone. SDC gives rumour dates, announcement dates, and deal terms. News dates separate a press rumour from a process leak. The hand-coded set of about 400 targets in the joint project serves as the first sample and as a check on the census.

**Identification:** The first test uses time inside one process. It compares market activity in the days after a contact wave with activity in the same target's quiet weeks, and with placebo dates in the same process. The second test uses the dose: a wave of ten contacts should move the market more than a wave of two. The third test needs variation in list width that the target does not choose for its own value. Bank habit supplies it: some banks run wide processes for every client. A bank's list width in its other deals predicts list width in this deal. The count of plausible strategic buyers in the target's industry supplies a second source.

**Structural flag:** Yes. Estimate the leak probability per contact, the synergy distribution from observed bids, and the entry cost from who enters after a given run-up. Counterfactuals: a leak-proof process with the same contact list; the profit-maximum list size for each target; a rule that contacts financial bidders before strategic bidders, or the reverse.

**The question no one else with the same data would ask:** Others will count bidders and bids. This paper asks what a contact does to the stock price before any bid exists, and treats the invitation itself as a disclosure to the market.

**Closest papers:** Keown and Pinkerton on pre-announcement run-up and insider trading (unverified) — they use the announcement window, not dated steps inside the process. Schwert on markup pricing (unverified) — he splits run-up and markup around public bids; this paper traces the run-up to contact waves and feeds it back into entry. Augustin, Brenner and Subrahmanyam on informed option trade before takeovers (unverified) — they detect informed trade; this paper names the process event that creates the information. Gorbenko and Malenko (JF 2024, in dossier) on who starts a sale — this paper asks what the sale, once started, tells the market.

**Main risk and kill test:** Filing dates for contact waves may be too coarse, often a month, to tie to daily prices. Kill test: take 50 deals with exact agreement dates. If abnormal volume in the five days after a wave does not exceed volume after placebo dates in the same deal, stop.

**Timeline to fall 2029:** Kill test on the first ledgers and the hand-coded set by summer 2027. Census merge with stock data and bank-habit measure by spring 2028. Model and reduced-form draft by fall 2028. Estimation and counterfactuals by spring 2029. Job-market draft by summer 2029.

### Pitch 2: The Auction Ends Before the Price Is Set

**Question:** How much value moves from target shareholders to the winner after the target grants exclusivity and the other bidders go home?

**One-sentence surprising answer:** The winning bid is an opening claim, not a price, and the target gives back part of it in the quiet weeks after it kills its own outside option.

**30-second corridor story:** On Friday the board picks a $50 bid and grants four weeks of exclusivity. The losers release their deal teams and let their financing lapse. The winner's diligence then finds one flaw per week. On the fifth Friday the board signs at $46 and calls $46 the best price available. It is. The board made it true on the first Friday.

**Why business schools care:** Finance treats the bid as the price and studies who bids what. Boards and courts judge the process at the moment they pick the winner. If the price in fact moves after that moment, the field measures the wrong object, and boards sign away their only lever before they use it. Every negotiated deal has this phase. Almost no paper prices it.

**The new fact or result:** The final merger price falls short of the winner's last bid before exclusivity in a large share of deals, and the shortfall grows in the length of exclusivity and in the speed at which rival bidders exit. Deals that keep a live second bidder, or enforce a short deadline, show no such shortfall. Targets that extend exclusivity lose more than targets that let it lapse once.

**Model:** After exclusivity, one buyer and one seller bargain over price. Each week, the buyer may learn a fact about the target that lowers its value, or claim one. Each week, the seller's outside option decays: rival bidders disband, their financing expires, and standstill terms bar a quick return. The seller accepts a cut when the cost of a restart exceeds the cut. Key primitives: the rate at which the outside option decays, the arrival rate and size of true value news in diligence, the seller's cost of a restart, and each side's patience. True news and a decaying outside option both cut price; only the second is a design flaw the board can fix.

**Data:** The sale-process census gives the winner's full bid path, the dates and length of exclusivity, extensions of exclusivity, rival exit dates, and the final price. The field set already records exclusivity changes and deadline outcomes. CRSP prices over the exclusivity window separate a market-wide fall in target value from a bargained cut. Compustat and earnings news in the window do the same for firm news. SDC gives the final terms and the time from signing to close. The hand-coded set of about 400 targets gives the first bid paths now.

**Identification:** Compare deals with formal exclusivity against deals that reach one bidder without a grant; the grant, not the single bidder, should start the decay. Within exclusivity deals, use length: a grant that spans a holiday period or a slow financing market runs longer for reasons apart from target quality. Use rival state at the grant date: a rival that stays warm, or re-enters late, holds the outside option up. Control for true news with the target's stock move net of the market and with earnings releases inside the window. If cuts track only true news, the effect dies under these controls; that is the correct test.

**Structural flag:** Yes. Estimate the decay rate of the outside option, the diligence news process, and the restart cost from the joint pattern of bid cuts, extensions, and restarts. Counterfactuals: a ban on exclusivity; a cap of two weeks; a paid standby second bidder. State the gain to target shareholders in dollars per deal.

**The question no one else with the same data would ask:** Others will study the bids that select the winner. This paper studies the weeks after selection, when no rival remains and the price still moves.

**Closest papers:** Gorbenko and Malenko (JF 2014, in dossier) and Gorbenko (JFE 2019, in dossier) estimate values from bids at the selection stage; this paper prices the post-selection stage they treat as final. Gorbenko, Green and Mirani, "Deal Terms in Takeovers" (working paper, in dossier, abstract unverified), reportedly model bid terms and conditions across bidders; this paper takes one winner's price path over time as its object, and no other. That border needs one clear talk with the supervisor before work starts. Boone and Mulherin on how firms are sold (unverified) describe process choice; they do not trace price after exclusivity. Bulow and Klemperer on auctions against negotiation (in dossier tracker) frame the value of one more bidder; this paper prices the loss of the last rival at the worst moment.

**Main risk and kill test:** Boards may fix the price in full before any grant, and later cuts may be rare or small. Kill test: in the hand-coded set, count deals in which the final price falls at least 2 percent below the winner's last pre-exclusivity bid, net of the market move. If that share is under 10 percent, stop.

**Timeline to fall 2029:** Kill test on the hand-coded bid paths by spring 2027. Census variables for exclusivity windows and rival state by winter 2027. Bargaining model draft by summer 2028. Estimation by winter 2028. Job-market draft by summer 2029.

### Pitch 3: Thirty Suitors, One Bid

**Question:** Why do dozens of bidders sign confidentiality agreements and then almost all leave without a bid, and what does their joint exit do to the price?

**One-sentence surprising answer:** The count of signatures that boards cite to courts is a vanity number, because all bidders read the same data room and the same fact kills most of them at once.

**30-second corridor story:** A board tells a Delaware court that forty parties took part. The truth sits in the dates. The data room opened on a Tuesday. By the next Tuesday, thirty-eight parties had read the same table on customer loss and had gone quiet. One bidder stayed, saw that all others had read the same table and left, and bid as a monopolist with a bad signal. The auction died in week one. The process ran for four more months.

**Why business schools care:** Courts, boards, and papers count contacts and agreements as competition. If exits come from one shared signal, that count has no value. The design stakes are direct: what the seller puts in the data room, in what order, and to whom, decides how many bidders survive to bid. Sellers now design that flow by habit.

**The new fact or result:** Exits cluster in time far beyond what separate exit choices would produce, and the clusters follow shared steps in diligence, such as data room access and management meetings. Stated exit reasons in one cluster name the same cause. In deals with one early cluster, the survivor's bid falls short of the level that the count of signers predicts, and the merger premium tracks the count of bidders still alive after the first shared step, not the count who signed.

**Model:** Each bidder holds a private synergy with the target. All bidders also face one shared fact about target quality that the data room reveals in stages. At each stage, a bidder pays a cost to stay. A bidder stays if its synergy can carry the shared fact. A bad shared fact sends out all bidders below one synergy line at the same date. The survivor then bids with two pieces of news: the shared fact is bad, and no rival could carry it. Key primitives: the spread of private synergies, the spread and arrival of shared facts, the cost to stay at each stage, and the share of target value that is common rather than private. The seller can stage, delay, or insure the shared facts.

**Data:** The sale-process census gives entry dates, exit dates, exit reasons, round bounds, and first bids for each party, plus counts for parties the filing does not name. Exit reasons already bound final valuations in the field design. CRSP and SDC add the premium and the outcome. The census does not record data room contents, so the build adds one light field: the date access opens and the dates of management meetings, taken from the same filing text. The hand-coded set of about 400 targets gives exit dates and bids for the first pass.

**Identification:** The core test is a time test. Simulate the exit path that separate, bidder-specific choices would produce, given each deal's entry dates and total exits. Compare the real path's clustering with that bound. A shared fact must produce more clustering, at shared dates, than the bound allows. Stated reasons then check the route: clustered exits should name shared causes, such as price or a business fact, while lone exits name bidder causes, such as financing or fit. Deals differ in how much they stage access; a deal that admits bidders to diligence in waves should show one cluster per wave. The joint paper with the supervisor studies bidders who submit informal bids and pass a cutoff. This paper studies parties who leave before any bid, and why they leave together. The samples and the objects differ.

**Structural flag:** Yes. Estimate the common share of value, the synergy spread, and stage costs from entry, clustered exit, stated reasons, and first bids. Counterfactuals: staged release of the worst fact after first bids; seller warranties or insurance on the shared fact; separate diligence paths for strategic and financial bidders. State how many extra bidders reach a bid, and what that does to price.

**The question no one else with the same data would ask:** Others will model each exit as one bidder's own choice. This paper asks whether the exits are one event that happens to many bidders at once.

**Closest papers:** Gorbenko (JFE 2019, in dossier) shows that participation drives the premium gap between buyer types; this paper asks why participation collapses before the bid stage his model starts at. Hansen on the auction of a company (unverified) treats bidder information costs in theory; this paper measures the shared information that ends participation. Povel and Singh on sale processes with costly bidder entry (unverified) let sellers subsidize entry; here the seller's own data room destroys entry. Boone and Mulherin on how firms are sold (unverified) count contacts as process breadth; this paper tests whether that count means anything.

**Main risk and kill test:** Filings may report exit dates in coarse blocks, which would fake clustering. Kill test: run the clustering test on the hand-coded set, then repeat it on the subset with exact dates only. If clustering vanishes in the exact-date subset, stop.

**Timeline to fall 2029:** Kill test on the hand-coded set by fall 2027. Access and meeting dates added to the census build by spring 2028. Model draft by fall 2028. Estimation by spring 2029. Job-market draft by summer 2029.

## 3. Where CCC fits

In my view "Competition Creates Competition" is a strong second paper, not the job-market paper: its core fact is conditional and no data yet measure a rival that reads the price before it pays to prepare, so a hiring committee will recall the title a week later but not a fact it can repeat, and on this rubric it scores near O8 I6 P5 F7 against the three pitches in this note. Its best use now is twofold: it stands alone as the theory paper that proves craft, and one of its stated tests — entry rises with incumbent strength only when the price can reach the rival before the rival's decision — can serve as a single sharp test inside Pitch 1, where market prices and dated entry exist in the same sample, without the whole job-market claim resting on an order-slot world.
