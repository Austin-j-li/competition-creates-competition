### Idea 1 — Can a seller credibly end a takeover contest?

**Question, in one sentence**

Does a seller’s inability to commit to the end of a contest reduce bidder preparation enough to lower shareholder proceeds?

**Why business schools care**

A board can improve its position after diligence costs become sunk by seeking another offer. Bidders anticipate that choice before they incur those costs.

This creates a conflict between the board’s best decision today and the process that maximizes value beforehand. Quantify that conflict, and the paper changes advice on final rounds, extensions, and late offers.

The question reaches corporate governance, information production, and market design. It also gives a broad finance audience a concrete decision: **when should a seller surrender discretion?**

**The new fact or result**

The target result is that discretion to extend a contest causes bidders to delay or abandon useful preparation. A credible end rule can therefore produce more serious competition before the deadline.

The contribution must measure the preparation that never occurs. A paper that only proves “commitment matters” would repeat established theory.

A particularly strong finding would separate productive evaluation time from strategic delay. The optimal policy would then depend on which type dominates.

**The model, in words**

Use a finite dynamic game with one seller and two or three prospective buyers. Buyers hold imperfect initial information about their acquisition values.

Diligence costs money and takes uncertain time. A buyer chooses when to begin, when to continue, and when to submit an executable offer. The seller chooses whether to accept an offer, extend the round, or solicit another buyer.

Buyers anticipate that continuation policy. Early preparation becomes less attractive when the seller can use it to create another contest.

The key primitives are value uncertainty, diligence costs, the speed of information arrival, the seller’s delay cost, and commitment capacity.

Keep the admission rule outside the central mechanism. **The solo question concerns future process policy; the joint paper concerns selection through informal bids.**

**The data and what must be built**

Add a compact layer to the census:

- Promises about finality, as communicated before preparation.
- Dates of substantive information access.
- Diligence milestones and requests for additional time.
- Original deadlines, extensions, and stated reasons.
- Firm offers, withdrawals, and subsequent invitations.

An NDA date alone cannot measure preparation.

Define the main sample at an observable sale milestone. Follow announced processes through both sale and failure. Reconstruct missing histories explicitly; do not interpret silence as zero participation.

**The identification**

An observed extension is endogenous. The seller extends because its information makes continuation attractive.

Estimate buyer behavior and the seller’s continuation policy jointly. Information-access dates and diligence milestones help distinguish slow evaluation from deliberate delay. Offers and exits constrain value and preparation costs. Continuation decisions constrain the seller’s delay cost.

The central identification task is to separate **expensive diligence** from **weak confidence in the deadline**. Both can generate little early participation.

Historical adviser behavior can inform beliefs, but it is not a clean instrument. Adviser choice also responds to the target’s needs.

Derive the identification argument before you build a large estimator. Where the data identify a set, report that set.

**The structural estimation and counterfactual**

**Structural: yes**, with a restricted dynamic model and allowance for partial identification.

Compare three regimes:

1. A fixed end date.
2. Extensions under rules announced in advance, tied to observable diligence milestones.
3. Discretionary extensions and reopened rounds.

Recompute bidder preparation under each regime. Simply remove late bids from the observed record, and the central mechanism disappears.

Report seller proceeds, serious participation, delay, allocation, and acquisition surplus net of diligence costs. Keep the counterfactual conditional on initiated sale opportunities. The data need not support a claim about all potential mergers.

**The question no one else with the same data would ask**

**How much competition disappears before the first bid because buyers expect the seller to change the process afterward?**

**The closest papers and the difference**

- **Povel and Singh, “Takeover Contests with Asymmetric Bidders,” RFS, 2006.** They already establish the importance of commitment and deal protection. Your contribution must quantify endogenous preparation lost through anticipated continuation. 
- **Daley, Geelen, and Green, “Due Diligence,” JF, 2024.** They model dynamic diligence, excessive delay, and bidder heterogeneity. Your object is how the seller’s future discretion changes preparation before costs become sunk. 
- **Gentry and Stroup, “Entry and Competition in Takeover Auctions,” JFE, 2019.** They estimate uncertainty and endogenous entry. You would identify a dynamic source of entry costs and evaluate commitment rules. 
- **Calcagno, de Bodt, and Demidova, “Takeover Duration and Negotiation Process,” EFM, 2021.** They connect duration, information, and potential competition. Your contribution requires the effect of anticipated rule changes on information production. 
- **Liu, Officer, and Tu, “Negotiation, Auction, or Negotiauction?! Evidence from the Field,” December 2023 draft.** Their data already document fluid processes and bid revisions. Richer chronology alone is insufficient. 

**The main risk and quickest test that would kill it**

The fatal risk is that filings disclose realized extensions but omit the promises and preparation decisions that preceded them.

Audit 60–80 histories with explicit deadlines. Stop if substantive access and prior promises remain mostly unobservable.

Also stop if the minimal model cannot distinguish deadline credibility from slow information arrival. Tight priors cannot repair that failure.

### Idea 2 — Competition for the record

**Question, in one sentence**

Does legal scrutiny induce boards to document more participants while giving those participants less genuine opportunity to compete?

**Why business schools care**

Governance rules aim to improve decisions, but firms choose the evidence that outsiders can inspect. A sale process can serve both shareholder value and the board’s defense against a challenge.

The paper would ask whether those objectives diverge inside the process itself. It could change how boards, shareholders, and courts assess competitive sales.

The economic object is **the opportunity to produce a viable rival offer**. Contact counts and formal market checks are imperfect measures of that opportunity.

**The new fact or result**

The target result is an opposite response of nominal and effective competition: more documented activity, but fewer feasible paths to a serious bid.

A strong fact would involve buyers that demonstrate interest, request necessary access or time, and face restrictions that prevent an executable offer.

A short window alone proves nothing. Efficient sequential search can also produce late contacts and few bids.

The theory must explain when scrutiny encourages genuine competition and when it encourages cheaper evidence of effort.

**The model, in words**

A board values shareholder proceeds, speed, and a possible private benefit from a preferred buyer. It chooses contacts, information access, and the end of the process.

A buyer pays for diligence only when sufficient time and a credible chance of success justify that expense.

An external reviewer observes documented actions. It can assess the quality of competitive opportunities only at a cost.

Under some conditions, the board substitutes visible contacts for costly opportunities that could displace its preferred buyer. The distortion disappears when the reviewer can observe process quality fully.

Do not invent a legal rule that requires a fixed bidder count. The mechanism concerns imperfect evaluation of conduct.

**The data and what must be built**

Construct bidder-level opportunity histories:

- Initial contact.
- Substantive data access.
- Time available before the relevant decision.
- Requests for additional access or time.
- The board’s response.
- Serious offers after an earlier exclusion.

Add legal eligibility, incorporation, controller status, and filing versions.

Preserve the original proxy and supplements separately. Corroborate a subset with bidder disclosures and court records. This paper requires evidence about conduct, not just narrative length.

**The identification**

Use a legal change only after you define its actual exposure.

For example, *Corwin* concerns certain postclosing claims after a fully informed, uncoerced shareholder vote. It does not create a universal reduction in scrutiny. Define exposure from information available before the outcome, rather than eventual judicial protection. 

Compare changes in recorded participation and credible opportunities across the relevant groups. Address prior trends, transaction composition, and changes in disclosure practices.

The principal threat is unusually direct: **the legal change may alter the record without altering the process**. *Trulia* concerns disclosure settlements, which makes this concern central. Analyze it separately rather than bundle both decisions into one treatment. 

The strongest evidence would combine the legal comparison with documented, serious bidders that could not obtain workable access.

**The structural estimation and counterfactual**

**Not applicable in the core proposal.**

Build a theory paper with careful empirical verification. The theory compares review based on observable activity with review based on credible competitive opportunities.

Do not promise a numerical welfare effect from litigation costs that the data do not identify. An empirical effect on credible access would already be consequential.

**The question no one else with the same data would ask**

**Which contacts exist to create a possible buyer, and which exist to create a defensible record?**

**The closest papers and the difference**

- **Cain, Griffith, Jackson, and Davidoff Solomon, “Does Revlon Matter? An Empirical and Theoretical Study,” 2020.** They link scrutiny to more bidders, stronger negotiations, and higher premiums. You would examine the economic content of the process measures themselves. 
- **Gatti and Gelter, “Barking without Biting: How Corwin Did Not Change M&A,” 2024 working paper.** They find limited changes in conventional process and contract measures. Your hypothesis concerns changes inside a nominally unchanged market check. 
- **Subramanian and Zhao, “Go-Shops Revisited,” Harvard Law Review, 2020.** They document restrictions that undermine price discovery. Your contribution must explain the equilibrium response to scrutiny across sale procedures. 
- **Antoniades, Calomiris, and Hitscherich, “No Free Shop,” 2016.** Litigation incentives and costly go-shops already appear here. Your new object is the allocation of feasible competitive opportunity. 
- **Jeon and Lee, “Effective Post-Signing Market Check or Window Dressing?,” 2014.** They generally support effective market checks. Your evidence must distinguish the conditions behind effective and ceremonial procedures. 

**The main risk and quickest test that would kill it**

The fatal risk is that your measure captures disclosure quality or efficient search.

Audit about 100 histories for substantive access, deadlines, extension requests, and refusals. Compare original filings with supplements.

Kill the project if the proposed opportunity measure depends mainly on missing text. Also kill it if efficient search explains the distinctive pattern without any governance distortion.

**Timeline to fall 2029**

**November 2026–March 2027:** construct the opportunity measure and audit legal exposure. **Rest of 2027:** establish the central empirical result and solve the model. **2028:** challenge the disclosure explanation and complete the paper. **First half of 2029:** finance and governance presentations. **Summer 2029:** finalize.

### Idea 3 — What rejected bids teach the next sale

**Question, in one sentence**

Does disclosure of unsuccessful bidder research change the willingness of buyers to investigate other firms for sale?

**Why business schools care**

A sale process produces information that can remain useful after that transaction ends. The public initially observes a selected outcome: the winning buyer and price.

A later filing can reveal rejected offers, withdrawals after diligence, and failed searches. Those outcomes may contain information about other acquisition opportunities.

The paper would establish an information externality across corporate sales. Its policy question concerns what process histories should disclose, and when.

**The new fact or result**

The target fact is a change in participation elsewhere after the first disclosure of losing histories, beyond the effect of the original deal announcement.

The theory should separate two effects. Shared information can reduce duplicate diligence. It can also suppress research that would uncover an unusually valuable bidder–target match.

The interesting result is a condition under which broader disclosure improves private decisions but reduces discovery of valuable matches.

An abnormal return at a filing date would not establish this contribution. The outcome must include another buyer’s research, entry, exit, or offer revision.

**The model, in words**

Use two targets with a common component of acquisition value. Each bidder–target match also has an independent component.

Buyers pay to discover their match values. The first sale generates a history of offers and withdrawals. A disclosure rule determines which parts become public before buyers investigate the second target.

Observers must distinguish a low offer, a withdrawal after diligence, and a refusal to begin diligence. Each event conveys different information.

The primitives are the common-value share, match dispersion, research costs, the first sale’s selection process, and the disclosure rule.

The model must account for the fact that disclosed bids come from selected participants.

**The data and what must be built**

Identify the **first public document** that reveals each process history. That may be a PREM14A, S-4, or SC 14D-9. A later definitive proxy may contain no new information.

Link that release to related sale processes already active. Recover subsequent contacts, preparation, withdrawals, and offers.

Define exposure through product or technology links established before the disclosure. Use the source target’s earlier announcements and news to determine what was already public.

Retain date intervals. Exclude ambiguous event order rather than invent daily precision.

**The identification**

Separate two events: announcement of the successful deal and first disclosure of its losing history.

Compare more-exposed and less-exposed active sales around the second event. Exclude releases accompanied by a new offer, price revision, or other major source news.

The mechanism requires three observable distinctions:

- Responses occur after the new history becomes public.
- Responses depend on the content of failed research.
- Responses are stronger where the common acquisition-value component should matter.

Filing dates are not instruments. Shared sector news and endogenous release timing remain serious threats.

Use evidence from the source announcement, earlier public records, and recipient processes to challenge those explanations. Without that separation, the paper becomes another study of merger-wave correlation.

**The structural estimation and counterfactual**

**Not applicable in the core proposal.**

The theory compares winner-only disclosure with fuller process histories. It also compares release during related active sales with release after those decisions.

A structural extension would need to identify common values separately from match values and selective research. The census alone does not deliver that separation.

The initial paper should establish the externality convincingly before it assigns a monetary welfare effect.

**The question no one else with the same data would ask**

**Does the public record of unsuccessful research reduce the amount of new research elsewhere in the market for corporate control?**

**The closest papers and the difference**

- **Guo, Liu, and Tu, “Neglected Peers in Merger Valuations,” RFS, 2023.** This is the closest empirical predecessor. It studies information about comparable firms in merger filings. Your contribution requires responses to failed bidder research inside other active sales. 
- **Moran, “Information Revelation in Merger Waves,” RCFS, 2017.** Acquisition actions reveal information about other deals. You would isolate disclosure of unsuccessful research after the successful outcome is already known. 
- **DeLong and DeYoung, “Learning by Observing,” JF, 2007.** They study information spillovers from bank acquisitions. Your object is information that changes acquisition research before another deal concludes. 
- **Fuchs, Öry, and Skrzypacz, “Transparency and Distressed Sales under Asymmetric Information,” 2016.** Public offer histories can impair subsequent trade. You would study costly discovery across different corporate sale processes. 

**The main risk and quickest test that would kill it**

The fatal risk is that the history contains little new information, or reaches too few related active processes.

Audit 50–100 first disclosures against the preceding public record. Count related active sales with usable decision dates.

Kill the project if the overlap is sparse. Also kill it if responses attach to the original deal announcement rather than the later disclosure.

**Timeline to fall 2029**

**November 2026–March 2027:** novelty and overlap audit. **Rest of 2027:** construct disclosure events and recipient histories. **First half of 2028:** establish the behavioral response and solve the model. **Second half of 2028:** complete the draft. **2029:** external presentations, identification revisions, and the market version.
