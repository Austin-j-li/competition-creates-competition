# Open search: three questions beyond the usual takeover auction

30 October 2026. Ordinary web search was available and used. References below link to papers, publisher records, or authors' accounts. Verification covers the cited argument, not every proof. Proposed results remain research hypotheses.

**First choice: Antitrust Selects the Buyer Before the Auction.** It connects private sale decisions to a major policy question: who acquires firms that still sell? The main obstacle is measurement of the rejected alternatives. The deadline paper is the strongest alternative if those alternatives cannot be recovered.

One literature update matters immediately. The March 2026 draft of **Deal Terms in Takeovers** studies 673 completed mergers and values non-price offer terms. Its scope includes regulatory conditions, exclusivity, and diligence. The [30 October seminar abstract](https://www.hkubs.hku.hk/event/deal-terms-in-takeovers/) describes an expanded sample of 820 mergers. Thus, the boundary must follow the economic question, not an older sample description. A solo paper cannot claim novelty from those terms or from a larger census. [March draft](https://www.brettgreen.info/drafts/deal_terms.pdf).

### Literature screen for the alternatives

- **Buyers Have Queues.** Keil et al., *Temporal Dynamics in Acquisition Behavior* (Journal of Management Studies, 2023), already connect acquisition activity to managerial capacity. The new object is the price externality across simultaneous private contests, including unsuccessful participation. The distinctive question is whether a buyer's delay elsewhere lowers this target's price. Anonymous losing bidders prevent construction of that network. Named winners alone do not recover it. [Published paper](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/joms.12849).
- **The Cost of Becoming Publicly For Sale.** Szydlowski's *Leaks and Takeovers* already links strategic leaks to entry and takeover incentives. Andres, Bazhutov, Cumming, and Limbach already study productivity losses after takeover rumors. The remaining question concerns the target's endogenous acceptance threshold after disclosure damages its standalone business. A useful paper must connect real damage to concessions, not repeat an announcement-return exercise. That requires an independent sample of disclosed processes that never reach agreement. [Szydlowski's account](https://clsbluesky.law.columbia.edu/2020/01/27/leaks-and-takeovers/); [Andres et al.'s account](https://clsbluesky.law.columbia.edu/2021/03/17/the-ma-rumor-productivity-dip/?noamp=mobile). The latter paper's current publication details are **unverified**.
- **Cheap Diligence, Common Mistakes.** Pernoud and Gleyze already make bidder information endogenous. Carlin et al. already connect correlated values to bidder-pool choice. The new question concerns correlated information errors from a common diligence supplier, holding underlying values fixed. Neither a 2023 indicator nor generic AI exposure measures actual bidder use. Without use records or a partner experiment, this remains a theory option with weak empirical access. [Pernoud–Gleyze, March 2026](https://agathepernoud.com/Pernoud_Gleyze_InfoCompetition.pdf); [Carlin et al., 2026](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34846).
- **Standstills and the Price of Returning.** Sautter's 2013 article already explains the trade-off between stronger initial offers and later competition. The new question is whether the option to return changes investment in diligence before the first bid. Contract text and waiver dates need separate collection. Legal changes require primary-source verification before any causal design; none is established here. [Sautter, *Auction Theory and Standstills*](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2207693).
- **The Cost of Opening the Data Room.** Kim and Zheng already estimate information and operating costs of additional bidders. Gorbenko, Green, and Mirani also discuss proprietary leakage through diligence. A residual question concerns whether staged access preserves competitive pressure while protecting particular secrets. The census does not measure the information disclosed in enough detail. The generic version should be rejected. [Kim–Zheng, verified February 2018 draft](https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/t2-my/my-prd/wp-content/uploads/sites/2637/2017/10/JMP_20180205.pdf); [Deal Terms](https://www.brettgreen.info/drafts/deal_terms.pdf). The latest Kim–Zheng draft is **unverified**.
- **Who Gets an Invitation?** Carlin, Liu, Officer, Pernoud, and Tu's *Bidder Pools in Mergers and Acquisitions* directly occupies this territory. Their 2026 paper links correlated valuations to optimal pool size and target surplus. Dynamic replacement of bidders could differ, but the broad invitation question lacks enough novelty. [NBER paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34846).
- **CCC.** Its closest benchmark remains endogenous information production and real decisions, alongside endogenous auction entry. The specific transfer of information incentives between rival profits and target proceeds supplies its distinction. Its practical score reflects the existing manuscript and the absent empirical implementation. [Pernoud–Gleyze](https://agathepernoud.com/Pernoud_Gleyze_InfoCompetition.pdf); [Gentry–Stroup](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304405X18302964). The final paragraph gives the portfolio decision.

## 2. Top three pitches

### 1. Antitrust Selects the Buyer Before the Auction

**Question.** How does anticipated antitrust review change which buyers enter a sale process and who ultimately acquires the target?

**Why business schools care.** Antitrust policy can change ownership even when it prevents no sale. A target can replace a horizontal acquirer with a financial or less related buyer. That substitution changes acquisition prices, buyer rents, and the allocation of productive assets. Private equity then benefits partly from the competitive constraints of other buyers. This is a durable question for corporate finance, rather than a forecast about the politics of 2029.

**The new fact or result.** The proposed result is a decomposition of regulatory effects into lost participation, weaker offers, and substitution toward alternative buyers. The crucial fact would concern strategic buyers who disappear before a formal offer, followed by concessions to a less exposed buyer. A further distinction matters: did the buyer refuse entry, or did the target exclude it? Aggregate merger counts and completed-deal review outcomes cannot answer those questions. These are proposed findings, not facts established by this search.

**Model.** A target chooses which feasible buyers to approach. Each buyer chooses whether to incur diligence costs and submit an offer. Buyers differ in acquisition value, review probability, expected review duration, and the cost of remedies or failure. The target compares expected proceeds with its standalone option. Regulatory exposure therefore affects invitations, entry, and offers. Begin with a small set of buyer types and cash offers. Treat contractual risk allocation as a recorded state; estimation of the value of contract clauses belongs to the existing joint work.

**Data.** Use the proposed US public-target census, initially for 2009–2025, with SDC, CRSP, and Compustat. Add dated FTC and DOJ case records and predetermined measures of buyer–target product overlap. Build a supplement for named candidates, target refusals, buyer refusals, and explicit regulatory concerns before formal offers. The existing Antitrust field concerns bid conditions; it does not supply this supplement. Separate financial buyers with related portfolio companies from buyers without that exposure. Anonymous parties retain reported types and exposure bounds; never infer their identities from the winner.

**Identification.** The preferred variation is an external enforcement decision that changes perceived review risk while another target's process remains active. Exposure must use the candidate's product overlap before that decision. Compare more and less exposed candidates within the same process, before and after the decision. Industry demand news, buyer finance, and direct product-market effects remain competing explanations. A presidential transition alone does not identify the channel.

Observed candidate refusals and target exclusions distinguish supply from selection. Offer levels and ranks constrain acquisition values; exposed entry responses constrain review costs relative to those values. Separate approval probabilities from delay costs only with additional review-duration and outcome information. Otherwise, estimate a composite expected regulatory burden. The main estimand concerns candidates in disclosed processes, including those that later fail after a filing. It cannot recover firms that abandon all sale plans before any disclosure.

**Structural flag: yes, provisionally.** Estimate buyer-type values, entry costs, and expected regulatory burdens, with the target's invitation decision. The counterfactual changes review delay while holding substantive approval standards fixed. It asks how prices and ownership change when delay falls and entrants adjust. A second decomposition fixes the bidder set to isolate the participation channel. Report shareholder surplus and ownership; consumer welfare requires a separate product-market model.

**The question no one else with the same data would ask.** How much of a financial buyer's acquisition profit comes from a strategic buyer who never submits an executable offer?

**Closest papers.**

- **de Bodt, Cousin, Officer, and Roll (2024), *The Deterrence Effect of M&A Regulatory Enforcements*, Journal of Corporate Finance.** They study effects on subsequent acquisition activity. The contribution here must identify substitution among buyers of the same target, before an announcement. [Paper](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S092911992400021X).
- **Gentry and Stroup (2019), *Entry and Competition in Takeover Auctions*, JFE.** They estimate pre-entry uncertainty and its effect on sale methods. This paper adds a specific regulatory wedge and the target's response through invitations. A generic entry-cost estimate would repeat their contribution. [Paper](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304405X18302964).
- **Gorbenko and Malenko (2014), *Strategic and Financial Bidders in Takeover Auctions*, JF.** They establish bidder valuation heterogeneity. This paper asks how policy changes which types become observable bidders. [Author manuscript](https://www.mit.edu/~amalenko/stratfin.pdf).
- **Gorbenko, Green, and Mirani (2026), *Deal Terms in Takeovers*, working paper.** Regulatory conditions and buyer selection among formal offers overlap directly. The solo contribution must sit before formal offers and explain missing competitors. [March draft](https://www.brettgreen.info/drafts/deal_terms.pdf).
- **Seldeslachts, Clougherty, and Barros (2009), *Settle for Now but Block for Tomorrow*, Journal of Law and Economics.** They study deterrence through merger frequencies across jurisdictions. The new object is ownership substitution within a private sale. [Institutional publication record](https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/settle-for-now-but-block-for-tomorrow-the-deterrence-effects-of-m).

**Main risk and kill test.** The candidate set may be anonymous, selectively disclosed, or visible only after the relevant choice. In the first six weeks, manually review 60 filings selected across years and industries before inspection of their narratives. Seek dated regulatory exclusions or refusals, usable candidate exposure, and processes active around external decisions. Stop this route if the evidence consists mainly of boilerplate conditions on the winning offer. Also stop if exposure cannot be assigned independently of outcomes. Those failures destroy the proposed distinction from Deal Terms.

**Timeline to fall 2029.** By February 2027: decide data feasibility and identify a defensible enforcement design. By December 2027: complete the candidate supplement and the first within-process evidence. By September 2028: finish the model and structural estimates. By March 2029: produce the full paper and counterfactuals. Use spring and summer 2029 for seminars and revision. This schedule assumes this is the sole new main project.

### 2. The Price of One More Round

**Question.** Does a target lose value because bidders expect it to reopen supposedly final requests for offers?

**Why business schools care.** Boards and advisers routinely decide whether to accept an offer or ask for more. Another round can raise the price today but teach bidders to delay serious offers tomorrow. The paper would connect a basic board decision to the cost of weak commitment. It also challenges a common interpretation of detailed ledgers: more rounds need not mean more effective competition.

**The new fact or result.** The proposed fact is that anticipated reopening shifts substantive preparation and executable offers toward later dates. A target then gains from some individual extensions while losing from the policy that makes those extensions predictable. The distinctive counterfactual compares a rule announced before participation with the same rule imposed after bidders prepare. The difference measures the incentive effect, not just the direct cost of delay. A correlation between process duration and price would not establish this result.

**Model.** Bidders choose when to prepare and whether to submit a serious offer. The target then chooses whether to stop, extend the deadline, or solicit another offer. Key primitives are preparation costs, arrival of diligence information, the target's daily delay cost, and bidders' outside options. Start with one possible extension and a fixed invitation set. Reopening is an economic choice, not an unexplained admission shock. This keeps the question separate from the joint paper's informal-to-formal selection mechanism.

**Data.** Use the census's deadline announcements, revisions, bids, access events, and exits. Add a narrow manual record of what each bidder knew before the original deadline. Separate target bid deadlines from offer expiration dates and contractual completion dates. The current “Enforced” label records a target action after a due date; it does not establish irreversible commitment. Likewise, a new round is not necessarily an extension. Use source dates and date bounds, not synthetic sort dates. Begin with cash contests that contain a clearly announced final request and comparable offers.

**Identification.** Separate three observed margins: late preparation, late arrival of already prepared offers, and later price revision. Diligence access and explicit readiness statements distinguish the first two where the narrative permits. Repeated decisions within a process link the target's choice to the available offer improvement and elapsed time. Those choices place restrictions on the target's delay cost. Participation and the timing of serious offers restrict preparation costs jointly with valuation information from bids.

The difficult primitive is the bidder's expectation of reopening. Realized extensions do not measure that expectation. Candidate information includes prior written assurances, expressly conditional deadlines, and the adviser's observable history before this sale. Adviser history is not an instrument: adviser choice and client quality are endogenous. Use it only within an explicit model of beliefs and selection. Separate common diligence news from discretionary extensions. If preparation costs, news arrival, and commitment remain observationally equivalent, retain an identified set rather than impose a precise estimate through priors.

**Structural flag: yes, provisionally.** A small dynamic model can estimate or bound preparation costs and target delay costs from both sides' decisions. Its counterfactual compares an announced fixed deadline with an announced extension rule that permits a specified response to new information. Entry and preparation must adjust under each rule. A calculation that merely deletes late bids from the observed process does not answer the question. The counterfactual ranks process rules; it does not assume that a board can legally refuse every later superior offer.

**The question no one else with the same data would ask.** How much do the target's successful requests for another bid cost it before any bidder submits the first serious offer?

**Closest papers.**

- **Skreta (2015), *Optimal Auction Design under Non-Commitment*, Journal of Economic Theory.** The seller's inability to commit is established theory. The proposed contribution is costly preparation, observable process decisions, and the measured value of commitment in takeovers. [Paper record and abstract](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10092296/).
- **Bulow and Klemperer (2009), *Why Do Sellers (Usually) Prefer Auctions?*, AER.** They compare simultaneous auctions with sequential entry when participation is costly. This paper studies endogenous reopening within a chosen process. [Author manuscript](https://www.nuffield.ox.ac.uk/economics/papers/2009/w5/wdaforweb29jun09.pdf).
- **Calcagno and Falconieri (2014), *Competition and Dynamics of Takeover Contests*, Journal of Corporate Finance.** They connect private negotiations to the option of an auction. The added margin is preparation timing under a seller's expected deadline policy. [Paper](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0929119914000145).
- **Gentry and Stroup (2019), *Entry and Competition in Takeover Auctions*, JFE.** Entry uncertainty already supports structural comparisons of sale procedures. The new primitive must be commitment, with separate evidence from preparation timing. [Paper](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304405X18302964).
- **Gorbenko, Green, and Mirani (2026), *Deal Terms in Takeovers*, working paper.** They study offer attributes and target preferences. This paper studies the target's sequential procedure, with contractual terms held as observed states. [Draft](https://www.brettgreen.info/drafts/deal_terms.pdf).

**Main risk and kill test.** The narratives may record calendar changes without either preparation timing or credible expectations. First review 40 processes with explicit final requests. Identify cases with readiness evidence before a deadline and a later discretionary change. Stop if most extensions follow fresh common information, or if no evidence distinguishes readiness from submission. Also stop if the simplest admissible model cannot distinguish commitment costs from ordinary delay costs. More rounds alone cannot justify a solo paper next to the joint project.

**Timeline to fall 2029.** By February 2027: settle the measurement and the boundary with informal bids. By September 2027: establish the preparation-versus-submission facts and a tractable model. By September 2028: complete estimates or informative bounds and the policy comparison. By March 2029: finish the paper. Reserve the final six months for presentation and revision.

### 3. When a Rival's Exit Kills the Sale

**Question.** When does an informed bidder's departure deter the remaining buyers even though they face less competition?

**Why business schools care.** A board can lose several buyers after one buyer walks away. The conventional competitive effect points toward greater interest from survivors. An information effect can point the other way. The distinction changes how boards interpret failed auctions, disclose participation, and sequence access to information. It also changes what a bidder count measures: one expert participant can support other buyers' confidence.

**The new fact or result.** The proposed finding is that a disclosed diligence-based exit reduces survivors' willingness to continue at an unchanged entry hurdle. Exits caused by a bidder's own constraints should produce a different response. The most useful result would locate when the information loss exceeds the gain from less competition. A fall in surviving bids is insufficient: less competition alone can reduce offers. Clustered exits are also insufficient because all bidders may receive the same bad information.

**Model.** Buyers learn about a common target component and their own synergies through costly diligence. An early expert can leave because its information is bad or because its private cost rises. Remaining buyers observe only the departure or a disclosed reason. The target controls what truthful process information it releases and when it releases it. Key primitives are common-value exposure, expert signal precision, private exit costs, and the credibility of disclosure. Focus the model on observable process messages and reversible participation. Do not add a stock market merely to connect it to CCC.

**Data.** Use the census's source-supported withdrawals, bidder types, diligence access, price feedback, revised projections, and re-entry events. Build a supplement for messages about rival participation: recipient, content, and date. The filing's later account of an exit does not prove contemporaneous knowledge among other bidders. Distinguish explicit withdrawals from inferred non-submission and target exclusion. Anonymous identities are acceptable within a contest if the filing identifies the relevant expertise or access. CRSP and IBES help locate public target news; single-stock options and TAQ are unnecessary.

**Identification.** The central comparison concerns survivor behavior after it learns of a departure, not simply after the departure occurs. Use episodes with explicit communication dates and unchanged disclosed projections, required offer terms, and access. Compare expert diligence exits with exits attributed to a buyer's own constraint. Examine whether recipients suspend preparation or decline a still-available opportunity. The mechanism predicts a stronger response when the departing bidder has superior information about a common component of value.

These comparisons remain vulnerable to selective disclosure and unreported common news. Where separate notification dates exist, compare recipients around those dates within the process. Do not treat notification order as random without evidence. This is a theory paper with demanding empirical discrimination, not an announced causal estimate from a generic event study. Its empirical claim must survive the competitive benchmark, common-news explanation, and the target's own decision to exclude bidders.

**Structural flag: no; not applicable for the initial paper.** The current assets do not identify the buyers' information sets or signal precision well enough for a credible structural counterfactual. A useful theoretical comparison concerns immediate disclosure of participation against delayed disclosure, with bidders aware of the rule. Any later structural estimate requires additional communication evidence; the census alone is insufficient.

**The question no one else with the same data would ask.** Which bidder sustains competition because other buyers trust its continued presence, even when it is unlikely to win?

**Closest papers.**

- **Milgrom and Weber (1982), *A Theory of Auctions and Competitive Bidding*, Econometrica.** Informative dropout is foundational, so “exits reveal information” is not a contribution. The proposed addition concerns costly preparation, ambiguous reasons, and target-controlled communication before executable offers. [Paper](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/spr09/cos444/papers/milgrom_weber82.pdf).
- **Gorbenko and Malenko (2024), *Auctions with Endogenous Initiation*, JF.** A bidder's approach already conveys optimism about common value. This paper must establish an exit mechanism with distinct process predictions, rather than reverse the sign of that result. [Published paper](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jofi.13288).
- **Lauermann and Wolinsky (2017), *Bidder Solicitation, Adverse Selection, and the Failure of Competition*, AER.** Seller solicitation already conveys information and can undermine competition. The distinctive object here is information from another buyer's endogenous departure and its observed transmission. [Published abstract](https://ftp.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.20131057).
- **Pernoud and Gleyze (March 2026), *How Competition Shapes Information in Auctions*, working paper.** Buyers already acquire information about rivals and their own values. This proposal needs a result about endogenous exits and communication that their mechanism does not deliver. [Current author draft](https://agathepernoud.com/Pernoud_Gleyze_InfoCompetition.pdf).
- **Li, Szydlowski, and Yu (2025), *Dynamic Information Design in an Entry Game*, Journal of Economic Theory.** Dynamic disclosure already separates entry and exit incentives. Here, the seller communicates endogenous bidder actions within a contest; it does not choose experiments about an outside project. [Paper](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S002205312500064X).

**Main risk and kill test.** The central information event may be unobservable. In six weeks, manually inspect 60 narratives with explicit withdrawals and later rival actions. Seek a usable subset with documented notification, a source-supported exit reason, and evidence about new target information. Stop if the filing never reveals when survivors learned of the exit. Stop the theoretical route if it produces only standard common-value dropout with renamed agents. Without both a distinct result and observable transmission, this is a section of another paper, not a job-market paper.

**Timeline to fall 2029.** By February 2027: decide whether communication evidence exists. By December 2027: complete the core model and distinguish its predictions from the five papers above. By September 2028: complete source-based empirical verification on a dedicated sample. By March 2029: produce a full draft. Use spring and summer 2029 for external criticism and revision.

## 3. Where CCC fits

**Keep CCC as the second paper while the leading candidates face their early stop rules.** Its strongest contribution is the transfer of information incentives from rival profits to target proceeds. Its job-market weakness is the missing evidence that a rival learns its own acquisition prospects from a target price before preparation. The discrete market, preparation threshold, and ownership conditions also limit how directly its theorem answers a broad M&A question. Its next contribution should establish that economic channel in actual sale processes. CCC can regain first place if the new candidates fail their measurement checks or if its distinctive channel gains convincing evidence.
