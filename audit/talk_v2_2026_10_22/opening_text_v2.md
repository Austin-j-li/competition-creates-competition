## Frame 2. The traditional answer

TITLE: The traditional answer: strength deters entry

SUBTITLE: Strength cuts the gain from entry

BODY:

- The bidder already in, the incumbent, is ready to bid.
- The next bidder, the challenger, must first prepare: diligence, finance, approvals.
- A stronger incumbent raises the price a winning challenger pays.
- So preparation pays less, and fewer challengers enter.
- Entry is a choice: costs decide who shows up.

TAKEAWAY: Strength deters: entry pays less, so fewer challengers come.

CITATIONS: Fishman 1988; Hirshleifer and Png 1989 | Samuelson 1985; Levin and Smith 1994

## Frame 3. What that answer leaves out

TITLE: What that answer leaves out: the stock price

SUBTITLE: The price is live before entry

BODY:

- That logic holds what the challenger knows fixed.
- Yet the target's stock keeps trading during the sale.
- Stock prices affect takeovers, and firms learn from prices.
- A stronger incumbent may change what the price reveals.

TAKEAWAY: Left open: how strength changes what the price reveals before entry.

CITATIONS: Edmans, Goldstein and Jiang 2012 | Foucault and Frésard 2014 | Bond, Edmans and Goldstein 2012

## Frame 4. This paper

TITLE: This paper: competition creates competition

SUBTITLE: —

BODY:

- An outside investor knows the challenger's quality, and trades the stock.
- A stronger incumbent moves a fixed value gap to target shareholders.
- So the stock price gets more sensitive to the challenger's quality.
- The investor trades on it: the price turns informative.
- Good news makes costly preparation pay: entry rises.

TAKEAWAY: The channel is information. Hold what the challenger learns fixed, and a stronger incumbent cannot raise entry.

## Speaker script

**Frame 1.** Start with a plain question about merger contests. Does a stronger bidder scare off its competitor, or draw in more competition? Real contests build in private first, and preemptive bidding shows how strength shapes who else comes. The answer also bears on how targets are sold, by auction or by negotiation.

**Frame 2.** Traditional theory gives a clear answer: strength deters. The bidder already in is the incumbent, and the next bidder is the challenger, which must pay to prepare before it can bid. A stronger incumbent raises the price a winning challenger pays, so preparation earns less and entry falls. The auction-entry literature adds the margin: who enters is itself a choice, driven by costs.

**Frame 3.** That answer holds fixed what the challenger knows. In a real sale the target's stock keeps trading, and prices already affect takeovers and guide real firm decisions. So strength may do two things at once: cut the gain from entry, and change what the price reveals. That second effect is the open question.

**Frame 4.** Here is the paper's answer: competition creates competition. An outside investor knows the challenger's quality and trades the stock, and a stronger incumbent shifts a fixed value gap toward target shareholders. The price then reacts more to quality, turns informative, and good news makes costly preparation pay. Hold learning fixed and the effect dies: the channel is information.
