# Frame 1: recommended lines (after round 3)

Question: Does a stronger bidder in a merger auction scare off its competitor, or draw in more competition?

## Recommended lines (round-3 option A, edited)

1. A competitor pays to check the books before it can bid.
2. Preemptive bidding: a first bidder opens high, so the competitor stays out.
3. To the target, one more bidder beats the best negotiation.

Fallback for line 3 (keeps the round-2 point): Whether more bidders come shapes the choice: auction, or talks with one.

Gray line: Fishman 1988 | Bulow and Klemperer 1996

The three lines go from cost to strategy to value:
- Line 1: coming in costs money, so entry is a real choice.
- Line 2: a first bidder can use strength to stop that choice.
- Line 3: the seller gains much from one more bidder.

## Sources for each line

- Line 1: main.tex section 2.1. A buyer must check contracts, liabilities and technology, and
  arrange money and approvals, before a binding offer. A buyer that does not prepare stays out.
- Line 2: Fishman (1988), as main.tex line 155 and talk.tex line 748 use it.
- Line 3: Bulow and Klemperer (1996), as main.tex line 1372 uses it. An auction with one more
  bidder gives the seller more expected revenue than the best negotiation with the bidders already
  there. This replaces round-3 line 3 ("Who else comes decides auction versus talks"). The paper
  does not support that causal claim.

## Speaker script

Think of a real sale. Before a second buyer can bid, it pays to look: it checks the target's
contracts, debts and technology, and it lines up the money. Imprivata's 2016 sale shows the steps:
one buyer approached first, the target called other buyers, and their early offers depended on
more checks. So whether a competitor comes is a real decision. A first bidder can open high to make
that decision easy, and the competitor stays out. That decision matters to the seller: an auction
with one more bidder beats the best negotiation with the bidders already there.
