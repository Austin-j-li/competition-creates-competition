## 4. The leading example

The example is the paper's example in symbols: one good-fit value, one poor-fit value, both above the reserve, and one order slot. It is nontrivial in Li's sense (p.3, rule 7(c)). A purchase moves the belief but never to certainty, so the "price reveals fit" misreading is false in the example.

How it threads through the talk:

1. **Slide 2.** It is stated in words: two fits, one order, a purchase that points but cannot prove.
2. **Slide 6.** The first buyer's value R sits between the two fit values. A good fit wins and pays R. Against a poor fit, the first buyer wins and pays the poor-fit value. The spread is R minus the poor-fit value; the good fit keeps its value minus R. The sum is the fixed fit gap, so a larger R moves the gap to shareholders. The general statement follows.
3. **Slide 7.** A purchase lifts the belief from π toward M, and a liquidity purchase keeps it below one.
4. **Slide 9.** Speech only: "A weak first buyer's value seldom exceeds the poor-fit value. The spread is thin, so trade does not pay."
5. **Slides 10–11.** The first numbers appear, on the figure and the control table.

No example number appears before Slide 10. This keeps the author's rule.

## 5. Where the results go

**Core (must stay):**

- **Proposition 1** (Slide 6). Keep "never raises" and "never narrows". Part (a) holds for every pair of classes. Part (b) needs the class ranking. Strict signs go to B5.
- **Belief band and exclusion bound** (Slide 7). **Trading bound** (Slide 8).
- **Proposition 2** (Slide 9). Face conditions in words, each faithful to the paper's B-form:
  - (H1) Against the weak first buyer, the high bill does not pay at the prior. Against the strong first buyer, it pays at the top belief M.
  - (H2) Against the weak first buyer, no order can cover k. Against the strong first buyer, a correct order covers k under every profile.
  - (H3) Against the very strong first buyer, the high bill does not pay even at M.

  The exact forms B_0(π) < c_H < B_1(M(π)), Δ_0 < k < ρκ(π)Δ_1 and B_2(M(π)) < c_H go to a hidden slide directly after Slide 9. The τ-form, τ(r_1) < M(1/2) < τ(r_2), is the example's Corollary 1 (Slide 10) and needs the good fit to earn more. Do not present it as the general statement. Scope line: class ranking; each F strengthens the one before (first order); other primitives fixed. "Floor + high-bill buyers who see a purchase" is exactly ρ + (1−ρ)(b + πλ): under full orders a purchase has chance b + πλ. The S14 ownership line stays in small type.
- **Fixed-information control** (Slide 11, Proposition A.2). "Can only lower entry"; "falls by exactly the chance that the bill sits between buyer profit at the two strengths". Scope: fixed law of class and signal, fixed class value laws, an independent cost law.

## 7. Opening script and conclusion

**Opening, about 60 seconds (speech):**
"Thank you. Picture a listed company that has just made a sale public. One buyer has done its homework and is ready to bid. A second buyer still decides whether to pay for due diligence. While it decides, the target's stock keeps trading. The standard answer, from Fishman, says a stronger first buyer keeps the second buyer out: a stronger rival leaves less profit to pay for diligence. That answer holds when what the second buyer knows stays fixed. In this talk, the second buyer reads the stock price first. A stronger first buyer also makes the price more informative about whether the second buyer is a good fit. Under three conditions, which I will state, that information brings the second buyer in. As strength grows, entry first rises and then falls back. Here is the model."

**Conclusion slide (Slide 14), proposed face:**

Reuse S26 and add one line (the only new text):

- Title: Competition can create competition
- A stronger first buyer taxes the second buyer's profit.
- The same strength pays for information about that buyer.
- Entry follows the winner of that race.
- **New:** Freeze the information, and strength only deters.
- Chain line and last line as on S26.
