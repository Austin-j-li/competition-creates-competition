## 3. Real disagreements

1. **The size of the question buffer.** Piazzesi plans 67% of the slot (60 of 90 minutes, p. 1), which matches the faculty reader's 30 of 45. Parberry plans about 89% (x − 5, p. 6). Hall's "about 20 slides" for 90 minutes (p. 4) implies speech for less than half the slot, at two minutes for each slide. Hansen treats 75 minutes as flexible from 60 to 90 (item 1). The faculty reader's ratio sits at the generous end of this range.
2. **Preview the results, or not.** Cochrane, as quoted by Karaivanov (p. 4), says "Don't 'preview' results. It wastes time". Cox's "Big Five" (Karaivanov p. 14), Shapiro (slide 6) and Hansen (item 7) say preview. Hansen's reason is that some of the audience will leave early.
3. **Where the literature goes.** Parberry (p. 3) and La Ferrara put it in the introduction. Jackson (p. 2) postpones "much of this discussion … until the end". Piazzesi (p. 2) keeps it on a backup slide.
4. **Answer now, or defer.** Jackson (p. 6) lets the speaker postpone questions on assumptions "until the end". Piazzesi (p. 1) postpones only until "the setup is on the table". Storesletten (#10) defers speculative questions only. Startz (p. 6) allows a short deferral if the speaker returns to the question.
5. **Sell, or teach.** Meager says that a speaker "in front of a room … [is] in sales". Tabarrok asks the speaker to "convey your excitement". Shengwu Li (rule 2) says "teaching, not advocacy". Piazzesi takes a middle line: "first the bad news, then the good news" (p. 3).

A side note: Rubinstein (Q4) prefers "not to have" a conclusion, because "our models are like fables". La Ferrara wants a last line that "says 'it matters!'". Rubinstein writes about papers, so this point does not transfer to talks with certainty.

## 4. Specific advice

**(a) A 45-minute slot with interruptions.**
- *Core minutes.* The 30-minute core has direct support from Piazzesi (p. 1) and indirect support from Hall (p. 4). At two minutes for each slide (Parberry p. 8: 1.5 to 2 minutes), 30 minutes hold about 15 core slides.
- *Setup time.* Piazzesi's Sargent rule caps the time before the setup at 10 minutes for a 90-minute slot. Scaled to a 30-minute core, the setup starts by about minute 4 to 5.
- *Main result.* Hansen (item 7) and Quint's Ricardo note (p. 4) say "the important stuff first". Karaivanov (p. 15) says that after the "intuitive appetizer … you should still have 2/3 of the presentation time to go".
- *Cut order.* Parberry (p. 6): "If you are short of time, the first thing to cut is the Technicalities section."
- *Checkpoints and buffers.* No source gives a checkpoint method. Storesletten (#8) says only "keep track of time and cut less relevant stuff". Any checkpoint scheme is my synthesis, not sourced advice.

**(b) The equilibrium definition and the main theorem.** No source fixes a one-slide or two-slide rule. Three sources say the same thing about the theorem:
- State the theorem formally, then "pause, and explain the relationships between the formal theorems … and the informal description" (Parberry p. 3).
- Show proofs as a "sketch … emphasizing the proof structure" (Parberry p. 4). Go deeper only when the proof gives the insight (Jackson p. 4).
- Give one key lemma, presented carefully, after the body of the talk (Parberry p. 4).

Jackson (p. 4) warns against the "solve for equilibrium" walk-through. For the definition, Parberry (p. 3) says to introduce all terms early and "remind the audience of the definitions at critical points later".

**(c) A market or pricing stage inside a game.** No source covers this case. The nearest advice is:
- Hall (p. 4): the "concreteness of a bullet on a slide often results in chaotic questioning".
- Jackson (p. 3): present special cases, not full generality.
- Parberry (p. 2): use a top-down order.

My inference from these sources: show what the market *does* in the game before its parts. Here, one order moves the belief inside a band. Then open the parts.

**(d) A hostile or premature question about an assumption.**
- *Before the setup:* "Let's postpone this discussion … after the setup is on the table" (Piazzesi p. 1).
- *On a weak assumption:* agree first without hesitation. Then give the purpose of the assumption, and end on a positive note (Piazzesi p. 3).
- *Sort the question* (Storesletten #10). A clarifying question gets a short answer. A question on the mechanism gets a full answer. A question about a different model gets a deferral. A question that doubts the main result needs a firm defence: "You must fight back."
- *Reply calmly.* "Always give friendly answers even if the questions seem hostile … the other people in the audience will … be on your side" (Hall p. 4). "Don't try to do too much new theory on your feet" (Hall p. 4).
- *Explain, do not give in.* A question is not a request for change. Give a one-sentence reason for the choice (Meager).
- *Take a hostile exchange offline* (Parberry p. 10). Naming the weaknesses in the conclusion makes antagonistic questions less likely (Parberry p. 4).
