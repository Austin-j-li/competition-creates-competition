The guides also differ in stance. Li, p.1, says “Teaching, not advocacy”. Startz, p.1, says “sell yourself”, within a job-market context. Startz also says “You’re there to teach”. ### Quint's own auction lectures

[The teaching page](http://www.ssc.wisc.edu/~dquint/speciallectures/home.htm) links four February 2023 Tokyo lectures. This review covers [Lecture 1](http://www.ssc.wisc.edu/~dquint/speciallectures/special%20lecture%201.pptx) and [Lecture 2](http://www.ssc.wisc.edu/~dquint/speciallectures/special%20lecture%202.pptx). References below count the title as file slide 1; printed numbers are one lower.

Lecture 1 puts an overview on slide 2 and the sale question on slide 3. Slide 4 specifies the object, buyers, risk neutrality, independent private values and seller commitment. It then states voluntary participation and equilibrium requirements. Slides 5–6 introduce allocation, payments and truthful reports. Slides 7–9 connect these choices to bidder payoffs. There is no separate timeline. Commitment precedes play; the mechanism determines the game. The monopolist example arrives on slide 15, after the revenue result on slides 10–13. This lecture does not put that example first.

Lecture 2 starts from the previous lecture's primitives on slide 2. Slides 4–6 show second-price payoff cases. Slide 7 announces the entry example. Slide 8 states players, entry cost, simultaneous entry, value revelation after entry, and symmetric mixed equilibrium. Slide 9 defines expected surplus. Thus the payoff cases precede the entry model; the explicit equilibrium arrives with that model.
