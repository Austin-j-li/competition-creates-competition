\section{Welfare and sale terms}\label{sec:welfare}

\subsection{Access to prices}\label{sec:access}

Access to prices improves acquisition outcomes at a fixed level of competition. Hold the incumbent at \(F_1\) and
compare the equilibrium of Proposition~\ref{prop:ccc} with that of an economy in which the challenger does not see the
price. Under the hypotheses of Proposition~\ref{prop:ccc}, both economies have unique full orders, and price access
raises expected target proceeds and acquisition surplus net of preparation costs
(Proposition~\ref{prop:access}).
Acquisition surplus excludes transfers between bidders, shareholders and traders, and
Appendix~\ref{app:welfare} states the results of this section in full.

The two economies generate the same orders. The trading bound of Section~\ref{sec:informed} forces full orders even
when the challenger cannot use the price and entry stays at \(\rho\), so trading costs coincide. Price access
changes only which high-cost challengers prepare: it adds preparation after a purchase. Each added preparation
contributes its gross profit minus its cost, and also the sales that the reserve would otherwise block; a challenger
prepares only when its expected gross profit covers its cost, so each term is positive.

In the benchmark at \(r_1\), expected proceeds rise from \(\reg{p2_base_hidden_proceeds}\) to
\(\reg{p2_base_r1_proceeds}\), a gain of \(\reg{p2_access_proceeds_gain}\), and net surplus rises by
\(\reg{p2_access_surplus_gain}\). With research at the cap (Section~\ref{sec:research}), the
gains are the same.

Target shareholders gain because a shareholder who holds through the sale receives \(V_T\), not the interim price.
The investor's expected gross profit, \(\reg{p2_access_gross_transfer}\) at \(r_1\), is a transfer from
liquidity traders. If one order unit is \(z\) shares per share of float, initial shareholders gain in total relative
to an economy without the investor at every \(z<\reg{p2_access_break_even}\), even if they bear the whole transfer.

Inside the recruitment region of Proposition~\ref{prop:ccc}, where high-cost preparation does not pay at the prior,
price access adds preparation. Where it already pays at the prior, an informative price deters some preparation: with
the strong benchmark and \(c_H=\reg{access_out_cH}\), price access changes proceeds by
\(\reg{p2_outside_proceeds_gain}\) and net surplus by \(\reg{p2_outside_surplus_gain}\)
(Appendix~\ref{app:welfare}).

The information in the price, not its level, separates the two economies. A deterministic external dividend of
\(\reg{p2_access_proceeds_gain}\) in the hidden-price economy equalizes the mean prices, and it leaves the orders
and entry unchanged.

\subsection{The payment rule}\label{sec:payment}

The effect of competition on the target-payoff spread depends on the sale payment.
Proposition~\ref{prop:payment} isolates this dependence at the payment stage, in a
verifiable-value institution with zero reserve, point-mass classes and an incumbent whose values lie between the
low and the high class value, \(0<\ell<\bar r<h\). After diligence, a sale to the next buyer at its value is an
enforceable fallback, and the seller and the winner divide the surplus above the fallback by Nash bargaining, with
seller weight \(\omega\in[0,1)\). A stronger incumbent weakly lowers the challenger's profit, and its effect on the
spread weakly has the sign of \(1-2\omega\). This effect is strict when \(\E[(R-\ell)_+]\) changes strictly and
\(\omega\ne1/2\); at \(\omega=1/2\) the spread equals \((h-\ell)/2\) at every incumbent distribution.

In the benchmark, the spread under this rule changes by \(\reg{pay_gap_coef}\,(1-2\omega)\) from \(r_0\) to \(r_1\).
The second-price auction is the case \(\omega=0\): it puts the whole weight on the runner-up's value.
\citet{BulowKlemperer1996} compare an auction with one more bidder and an optimal negotiation by expected revenue;
here the comparison concerns how competition moves the spread.

To see why, take \(R>\ell\). A high-class challenger wins and pays \((1-\omega)R+\omega h\), whose sensitivity to
\(R\) is \(1-\omega\). A low-class challenger loses, and the incumbent pays \((1-\omega)\ell+\omega R\), whose
sensitivity is \(\omega\). Competition widens the difference exactly when the first sensitivity exceeds the second.

\subsection{Reserves}\label{sec:reserves}

A reserve affects both the payment required to win and the information in the target's shares. Once the reserve can
exclude bidders, three events separate. \emph{Preparation} is the event that the challenger pays its preparation
cost. A \emph{sale} occurs when at least one bidder submits an admissible bid. \emph{Two admissible bidders} meet
when both the incumbent and a prepared challenger meet the reserve. In the benchmark of Section~\ref{sec:benchmark},
the reserve lies below both challenger values, so every prepared challenger is admissible.

A reserve that excludes the low class can widen the class difference in target proceeds, because the low class then
adds nothing to proceeds. Informed trade can then pay against both incumbents, and the price is informative at both
strengths. Proposition~\ref{prop:reserves} gives the consequence. If informed trade is full at both strengths and only a purchase recruits at each,
entry is flat in incumbent strength, and strict deterrence needs the stronger incumbent to push gross profit at the
top posterior below the high cost.
