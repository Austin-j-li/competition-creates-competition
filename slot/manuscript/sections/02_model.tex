\section{The model}\label{sec:model}

\subsection{The decision interval}\label{sec:interval}

The model applies to a listed target whose sale opportunity is publicly understood while its stock still
trades and while a further bidder can still come in. A disclosed approach, an announced strategic review, or
an open contest can create such an interval if the opportunity becomes public before the buyer set is fixed;
only then can a prospective buyer watch the price during the period that matters.
Section~\ref{sec:empirical} states how to date this interval in a given transaction.

Within the interval, one buyer is already prepared. It may be the party that approached the target or a
bidder that has completed diligence, and its strength is what the public can infer about its acquisition
value from the disclosed record. Another buyer, the challenger, has not decided whether to prepare. Preparation is required
for an executable proposal: before a buyer can submit a binding offer, it must verify the target's
contracts, liabilities, and technology, arrange financing and approvals, and commit evaluation capacity that
has other uses. Those costs are sunk once incurred, and they differ across buyers, since prior familiarity,
capacity already in place, or a small deal relative to the buyer's size make preparation cheap for some
buyers and expensive for others. A challenger that does not prepare stays out of the sale, so its cost is an
entry cost: payment reveals its value and permits a bid.

The investor and the challenger know different things. The challenger knows its own integration plans, while a specialist
investor may know what the target's customers do, how its technology compares with alternatives, or how its
product demand will change. These are different facts about the same acquisition match, so a more accurate
signal about one can leave the other unknown. The model gives the investor exact knowledge of the
challenger's quality class, though not of its value, and suppresses the challenger's initial information;
Section~\ref{sec:signals} removes both extremes, and Section~\ref{sec:research} makes the investor's
information the result of costly research. The identity of the challenger is common knowledge, and the market
is uncertain only about how well the challenger fits the target.

Public incumbent strength is a distribution, not a bid. The record can reveal that a strategic acquirer with
clear synergies is at the table, or that the prepared party is a financial buyer with a leveraged capital
structure, and in each case it need not reveal what either buyer would pay; strategic and financial bidders
differ systematically in their valuations \citep{GorbenkoMalenko2014}. The model varies that distribution and
keeps the incumbent's realized value private until it bids. Disclosure records also document a costly
preparation stage: Imprivata's definitive proxy separates an unsolicited approach, outreach to potential
buyers, and indications of interest conditional on further diligence \citep{Imprivata2016}, and takeover
competition develops before public bids \citep{BooneMulherin2007,GentryStroup2019}.
Section~\ref{sec:empirical} states how to test whether prices draw buyers into that preparation stage.

\subsection{Values, preparation, and the sale}\label{sec:values}

The target's known standalone value is normalized to zero, and acquisition values and preparation costs are
measured per target share. A common standalone component added to every ownership outcome shifts prices and
payoffs by a constant and leaves incentives unchanged.

All strategic agents are risk neutral. The target share is the only traded claim with information about the
acquisition match, and neither bidder trades it. The sale mechanism binds all target shares, so shareholder
tendering and holdout stay outside the model. The incumbent's value distribution is public when the sale
opportunity becomes visible.

Two potential acquirers face the target. The incumbent has already prepared, incurs no further entry cost,
and knows its value before it bids. Its acquisition value \(R\) has a continuous distribution \(F\) on
\([0,\bar r]\), conditional on public information when the sale process begins, and \(F\) is extended by
unity above \(\bar r\). A first-order stochastic shift of \(F\) strengthens the incumbent. In the main case
\(R\) is uniform on \([0,r]\), and an increase in \(r\) strengthens the incumbent.

The challenger belongs to one of two quality classes, \(j\in\{H,L\}\), with prior \(\pi=\Pr(j=H)\in(0,1)\).
Given its class, its acquisition value \(\theta\) has a distribution \(G_j\) on \([0,\infty)\) with a finite
mean; atoms are allowed. The probability that a class-\(j\) value falls below the reserve \(p\) is
\(G_j(p^-)\), and \(D(y)=G_L(y)-G_H(y)\) is the difference between the two class distributions at \(y\).

The high class, or high-value class, dominates the low class in the sense of first-order stochastic
dominance. Both class supports lie above the reserve, so that every value meets it:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:classes}
G_H(y)\le G_L(y)\quad\text{for every }y,\qquad G_H(p^-)=G_L(p^-)=0.
\end{equation}
I call the two restrictions in \eqref{eq:classes} the class ranking.

Under the class ranking~\eqref{eq:classes}, competition widens the class difference in target proceeds under
every incumbent distribution (Proposition~\ref{prop:returns}). Appendix~\ref{app:payoffs} characterizes the
weakest restriction of this kind: when neither class has a value atom at the reserve, the
spread rises under every strengthening exactly when the high class dominates above the reserve and the two
classes are equally likely to fall below it, which I call equal admissibility.
Section~\ref{sec:reserves} studies a reserve that excludes the low class, where equal admissibility fails.
The structure is the selective-entry structure of the auction literature: the class indexes the distribution
of the challenger's value, preparation reveals the value, and the stock price is the pre-entry signal.

The benchmark is the point-mass case: \(G_H\) puts all its mass on \(h\), \(G_L\) puts all its mass on
\(\ell\), and \(\pi=\reg{prim_pi}\). In the benchmark the class and the value coincide, and values, the
reserve, and the uniform incumbent's strength satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:order}
0<p<\ell<r<h.
\end{equation}

The general statements need no normalization of money: a common positive rescale of every value, the reserve,
and every preparation, trading and research cost rescales prices and payoffs by the same factor, while
orders, beliefs, entry, and ownership do not change. The benchmark measures money in units of the low value,
so \(\ell=\reg{prim_ell}\) there.

The challenger initially knows neither its class nor its value. After it observes the stock price, it
privately learns its preparation cost \(C\in\{0,c_H\}\), with \(\Pr(C=0)=\rho\), \(0<\rho<1\), and
\(c_H>0\). The low cost is zero: a zero-cost challenger is a ready buyer whose diligence costs nothing at the
margin. A payment of \(C\) reveals \(\theta\) and permits a bid, and a challenger that does not pay stays
outside the sale.

The pair \((j,\theta)\), the incumbent's value, the preparation cost, and the draws of the order slot in
Section~\ref{sec:slot} are mutually independent. The cost therefore carries no information about the class
or the value, and conditional on the class, the challenger's value is independent of the order.

Before trade, the seller publicly commits to a cash second-price auction with reserve \(p>0\). The highest
admissible bidder acquires the target and pays the larger of the reserve and the highest competing admissible
bid. A bid equal to the reserve is admissible, and there is no sale without an admissible bid. Each prepared
bidder whose value meets the reserve bids its value; this bid is weakly dominant
(Appendix~\ref{app:payoffs}).

A prepared challenger whose value is below the reserve does not bid. Without a challenger, the incumbent
buys at the reserve when its value meets it, so expected no-entry proceeds are \(t_0=p[1-F(p)]\): the board
collects its announced walk-away price from a lone prepared bidder whose value meets it. Because \(F\) is
continuous, acquisition-value ties have probability zero.

\subsection{Trading: the order slot}\label{sec:slot}

An investor observes the challenger's class \(j\). It is an outside sector specialist, such as a
sector-focused long-short fund. It holds no net position in the target before trade, has no control rights,
and cannot acquire the target. Its information concerns the challenger's fit with the target, and it forms
that view from its own research on the target's customers, technology, and demand.

Trade takes place in one order slot, a single public order opportunity that comes before the challenger's
decision. With probability \(\lambda\in(0,1)\) the informed investor fills the slot: it sees the class
\(j\) and chooses an order \(q\in\{-1,0,1\}\), and a nonzero order costs \(k>0\). With probability
\(1-\lambda\) a liquidity trader fills the slot instead and, for exogenous reasons apart from the sale,
sells, abstains, or buys one unit with probabilities \(\beta\), \(1-2\beta\), and \(\beta\), where
\(0<\beta\le1/3\).\footnote{Only the posterior bound at the zero order uses \(\beta\le1/3\), and through it
the ceiling of Section~\ref{sec:inference}; Propositions~\ref{prop:ccc} and~\ref{prop:coexistence} hold for
every \(\beta<1/2\) (Appendix~\ref{app:beliefs}).} A liquidity sale comes from a current shareholder who
sells for cash, a liquidity purchase from an outside investor, and an abstention means that the opportunity
passes with no order. No one observes who filled the slot, and the identity draw is independent of the class,
the costs, and the incumbent's value.

Competitive market makers see the order \(X\in\{-1,0,1\}\), set the price \(P=\E[V_T\mid X]\), where
\(V_T\) is the terminal payoff of a target share, and absorb the order at that price; each share held at the
sale, market-maker inventory included, receives \(V_T\). If the investor fills the slot with order \(q\),
its order is the whole order that market makers see, so it trades at the price \(P(q)\) and earns
\begin{equation}\label{eq:profit}
q\,[V_T-P(q)]-k|q|.
\end{equation}
The unit is a normalized small trading unit. The cost \(k\) is a separate friction, such as an execution
cost or a cost to carry the position, and it is distinct from adverse-selection price impact, which the
competitive price already generates. The investor's order after bad news is a short sale, and \(k\) includes
the cost to borrow shares.

A liquidity order in a given direction arrives with probability \(b=(1-\lambda)\beta\), and the ratio
\(u=\lambda/b\) is the informed presence mass relative to the liquidity mass in one direction; the number
\(1+u\) is the largest likelihood ratio that one order can carry. Section~\ref{sec:inference} shows that,
under every investor strategy, the posterior after every order lies between a bottom posterior \(m(\pi)\)
and a top posterior \(M(\pi)\):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bounds}
M(\pi)=\frac{\pi(1+u)}{\pi(1+u)+1-\pi},\qquad
m(\pi)=\frac{\pi}{\pi+(1-\pi)(1+u)}.
\end{equation}
The bounds follow from one property of the slot: each class reaches each order through a liquidity trader,
so the investor keeps a residual advantage after every order.

The slot uses the alternative-arrival structure of \citet{DowGorton1997}, in which an informed order and a
liquidity order of the same size are alternative arrivals in one opportunity; in
\citet{AdmatiPfleiderer2009}, a liquidity shock forces the informed holder's own sale, so a sale occurs on
path for both motives. The unit order menu, the presence probability, and the symmetric unit cost follow
\citet{EdmansGoldsteinJiang2015}.%
\footnote{The unit menu \(\{-1,0,1\}\) keeps every order on path, so no off-path belief enters the analysis.
A menu of continuous order sizes needs a convention for the sizes that no class uses in equilibrium, as in
\citet{EdmansGoldsteinJiang2015}. Another route keeps every size on path through additive liquidity flow:
with Laplace noise of scale \(s\) and full unit orders, the largest likelihood ratio of a favorable flow is
\(\eu^{2/s}\), where \(\eu\) is Euler's number, printed upright to separate it from entry \(e(x)\), so the top posterior equals the slot's \(M(\pi)\) when
\(1+u=\eu^{2/s}\). The two set-ups then share the posterior bounds, and they differ in the probability with
which each class reaches the top posterior.}
Risk-neutral market makers with zero expected profit price each order, as in \citet{GlostenMilgrom1985} and
\citet{Kyle1985}. Here the terminal payoff depends on the price through preparation.

Market makers observe the order \(X\) and nothing else. The challenger observes the price \(P\) and its own
cost \(C\), but not \(X\), its class, its value, or the incumbent's value; the investor observes the class
\(j\) but not the value \(\theta\), and the incumbent knows its own value. The sequence is as follows. The
seller announces the sale rule. The investor learns the class, the slot draw selects the investor with
probability \(\lambda\) and a liquidity trader otherwise, and the selected trader places its order. Market
makers set the price from the order, and the challenger observes the price and its cost and decides whether
to prepare. Prepared bidders then learn their values and submit their bids, and ownership and financial
payoffs are realized. Section~\ref{sec:research} adds a research stage before the investor learns the class.

\subsection{Equilibrium}\label{sec:equilibrium}

An equilibrium specifies, for each class, a distribution over the three orders; the market makers' posterior
\(\mu(x)=\Pr(j=H\mid X=x)\) and price \(P(x)\) after each order \(x\); the challenger's belief at each
price; its preparation choice at each price and cost; and truthful bids. The investor can mix, and
wrong-signed orders are allowed. Every order has positive probability in both classes, because a liquidity
trader can place it, so Bayes' rule gives a posterior after every order. Prices are competitive. A
high-cost challenger prepares when gross profit at its belief exceeds \(c_H\) and stays out when it falls
short, and a zero-cost challenger, whose gross profit is an expected auction rent and never negative,
prepares after every price. The investor's order maximizes
\[
U_j(q)=q\,\bigl[t_0+e(q)(t_j-t_0)-P(q)\bigr]-k|q|
\]
at the candidate's prices and entry, where \(e(q)\) is entry after the order \(q\), the probability that
the challenger prepares after the price \(P(q)\); \(t_j\) is expected target proceeds after entry in class
\(j\), and \(t_0\) is expected target proceeds without entry. Definition~\ref{def:equilibrium} in
Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} states the six conditions in full.

For a known challenger value \(\theta\), expected target proceeds after the challenger prepares are
\(t(\theta)\), and the challenger's expected gross acquisition profit is \(g(\theta)\); as before, \(t_0\)
is expected target proceeds without entry. The class objects are expectations over the class distribution,
\(t_j=\E_j[t(\theta)]\) and \(g_j=\E_j[g(\theta)]\) for \(j\in\{H,L\}\), and the target-payoff spread is
\(\DT=t_H-t_L\). Bids are truthful, so these objects depend on the incumbent's distribution and the reserve
but not on any belief. At a belief \(\mu=\Pr(j=H)\), the challenger's expected gross profit is
\(B(\mu)=g_L+\mu\,(g_H-g_L)\), because its value depends on the price only through its class. When the
incumbent's distribution matters, I write \(g_j(F)\), \(\DT(F)\), and \(B_F\); for the uniform incumbent on
\([0,r]\), I write \(g_j(r)\), \(\DT(r)\), and \(B_r\).

The investor's objective takes the expected terminal payoff of a target share given class \(j\) and order
\(q\) as \(t_0+e(q)(t_j-t_0)\), because entry depends only on the price and the cost, and the cost is
independent of the class and the order. The same independence, with Bayes' rule, turns the competitive price
into
\begin{equation}\label{eq:price}
P(x)=t_0+e(x)\,\bigl[t_L-t_0+\DT\,\mu(x)\bigr].
\end{equation}
The ex ante payoff of the investor multiplies \(U_j\) by \(\lambda\), and this factor leaves the best order
unchanged.

When ties arise, an indifferent investor may mix over its best orders, and an indifferent high-cost challenger
prepares; Table~\ref{tab:a4-ties} in Appendix~\ref{app:coexistence} records each conclusion that another tie
rule changes.

A unilateral deviation by the investor changes only its own order and faces the candidate equilibrium's
price function and entry rule, so the deviation payoff is \(U_j(q)\) read at the candidate's \(P(q)\) and
\(e(q)\). A comparison across economies, such as across incumbent strengths, solves for the equilibrium
again in each economy.

Entry \(\Ent\) is the probability that the challenger prepares, and high-class ownership \(\Own_H\) is the
probability that a challenger of class \(H\) acquires the target. The probability that a prepared challenger
of class \(H\) wins against the incumbent distribution \(F\) is
\(W_H(F)=\E_H\bigl[F(\theta)\1\{\theta\ge p\}\bigr]\).
