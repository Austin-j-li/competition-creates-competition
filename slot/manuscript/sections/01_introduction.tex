\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

At fixed information, a stronger incumbent reduces a prospective challenger's expected return from entry into a
takeover contest. The challenger must pay preparation costs before it can submit an executable offer, and a
stronger rival lowers what that preparation earns \citep{Fishman1988,HirshleiferPng1989}. I study a force in
the opposite direction. Competition can make the target's stock more informative about the challenger, and when a
potential buyer observes that price before it commits to acquisition preparation, the information effect can
outweigh the loss of acquisition profits. A stronger incumbent can then attract another buyer and raise the
probability that a challenger from the high class acquires the target.

The setting is a listed target with a public sale opportunity. One buyer is already prepared, and the challenger
has not committed to the diligence that an executable proposal requires; Section~\ref{sec:interval} describes the
decision interval in which the challenger decides while the target's stock trades. Incumbent strength is the public
distribution of the prepared bidder's value; a stronger incumbent shifts it upward, and the challenger decides
before any offer is announced.

The challenger and target shareholders hold two claims on the same acquisition surplus: the challenger keeps
what remains if it prepares, bids, and wins, and target shareholders hold the traded claim, what the cash
second-price auction pays them. The challenger's value comes from one of two quality classes, high and low, the
high class dominates the low class, and the classes fix the expected value gap between them. By
Proposition~\ref{prop:returns}, a stronger incumbent never raises the probability that the
challenger wins, and when the incumbent wins it pays the challenger's bid, so at least as much of the gap reaches
target proceeds; a challenger that wins pays at least as much in expectation, so no more of the gap remains as
acquisition profit at every belief. Target shares therefore become at least as sensitive to information about the
challenger at the same time as the challenger's own return to preparation falls weakly.

An outside specialist investor with no net position in the target knows the challenger's class and trades on that
sensitivity. The trading stage is one order slot, with the alternative-arrival structure of
\citet{DowGorton1997}: with some probability the investor fills the slot, and otherwise a liquidity trader fills
it with a sale, a purchase, or no order. No one observes who placed the order, and a liquidity order can match
the investor's direction, so no order reveals the class. Under every investor strategy the posterior after every
order stays inside fixed bounds, and the investor keeps a residual advantage after every order; a larger gap in
target proceeds can make that advantage worth the cost of trade. Target shareholders gain when the price recruits
entry, because a holder through the sale receives sale proceeds, not the interim price.

The main result, Proposition~\ref{prop:ccc}, compares three economies on a nonempty open set of parameters: one with a weak incumbent, one
with a strong incumbent, and one with a very strong incumbent against which recruitment stops. In each economy the
equilibrium outcome is unique. With the weak incumbent, the investor's residual advantage cannot
cover the trading cost, the price reveals nothing, and only a zero-cost challenger prepares. With the strong
incumbent, the investor buys after good news and sells after bad news, and a purchase brings in a high-cost
challenger, so entry rises even though acquisition profit falls at the prior and at every other fixed belief that
gives the high class positive weight. When a prepared high-class
challenger beats the strong incumbent for sure, the probability that a challenger from the high class acquires the
target rises with entry; if, in addition, a low-class challenger beats the weak incumbent with positive probability,
the high class also wins a larger share of the takeovers that go to a challenger. With the very strong incumbent, the
price stays informative, but even the most favorable price fails to justify preparation at the high cost, so entry
returns to its floor.

The reversal comes from the change in information that competition induces. Market makers anticipate the
preparation that each price induces, so the reversal holds under rational pricing; when the investor's orders
stay fixed at their informative level while the incumbent strengthens, a stronger incumbent cannot raise entry
(Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}).

As an illustration, in the example of Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}, entry rises from \reg{entry_r0} at the weak
incumbent to \reg{entry_r1_B} at the strong incumbent, high-class ownership rises from \reg{own_r0} to
\reg{own_r1_B}, and the high class's share of the takeovers that go to a challenger rises from
\reg{share_H_r0} to \reg{share_H_r1_B}. Over the same change, gross acquisition profit at the prior falls
from \reg{Bprior_r0} to \reg{Bprior_r1_B}, while the target-payoff spread rises from \reg{Delta_r0} to
\reg{Delta_r1}. In the example, high-cost preparation stops above the entry ceiling, the strength
\(r_C\approx\reg{rC_B}\). At the example's auction and costs and the prior \(1/2\), the hypotheses of the main
result hold exactly when \(\reg{u_lo_frac}<u<\reg{u_hi_frac}\), where \(u\) is the informed presence mass relative
to the liquidity mass in one direction; equivalently, the top posterior at the prior \(1/2\) lies between the
preparation thresholds of the strong and the very strong incumbent, \(\reg{M_lo_frac}\) and \(\reg{M_hi_frac}\)
(Corollary~\ref{cor:ccc-example-short}).

When target proceeds and acquisition profit are both higher with a high-class challenger, the investor's orders, the
posteriors and the challenger's preparation depend on the auction only through two numbers: the target-payoff spread
and the preparation threshold, the belief that a high-cost challenger needs before it prepares
(Section~\ref{sec:twonumbers}). A stronger incumbent never narrows the spread, and it never lowers the threshold while
a challenger known to be in the low class earns at most the high cost. When an indifferent investor may mix
over its best orders and an indifferent high-cost challenger prepares, a quiet market, in which the investor does not
trade, and an informative market coexist exactly when the threshold lies above the prior and within reach of a
purchase, and the spread is wide enough that a purchase that just reaches the threshold covers the trading cost but
narrow enough that no order against an uninformative price earns more than it
(Proposition~\ref{prop:coexistence}; Section~\ref{sec:coexistence}). In the informative market, entry depends on the
incumbent only through the spread: it rises with the spread while class \(H\) mixes between a purchase and
abstention, and it stays constant once class \(H\) buys for sure. Along any
path of strengthening incumbents, the strengths at which a quiet market coexists with a sure purchase form one
interval. The whole set of strengths with coexistence is one interval when the high class's gross profit exceeds the
high cost against the weakest incumbent and the spread grows, in proportion, at least as fast as that excess shrinks.
As an illustration, in the example at the prior \(1/2\) the strengths with coexistence form the interval from
\(r_A\approx\reg{rA}\) to \(r_N\approx\reg{rN}\), and class \(H\) buys for sure from \(r_F\approx\reg{rF_B}\)
(Corollary~\ref{cor:example-coexistence-short}).

When the investor pays for research that can make it informed, the prior is even and an indifferent investor may mix
over its best orders, research and entry rise together with the target-payoff spread until research reaches its cap,
in the equilibrium in which a purchase after successful research recruits a high-cost challenger
(Proposition~\ref{prop:research}; Section~\ref{sec:research}). The investor chooses the probability of success up
to a cap, at a quadratic cost.
Where the spread exceeds twice the trading cost and the preparation threshold lies above the prior, this equilibrium
exists exactly when the posterior after a purchase, at the research the investor chooses, reaches the threshold. The
low class's selling on bad news leaves the rise in place: the low class abstains while a sale cannot pay its cost
and then sells only as often as keeps its sale at break-even, which adds nothing to the value of research; once a
sure sale pays, the sale's profit adds a second return to research. When class \(H\) outbids the incumbent,
high-class ownership rises with research. As an illustration, in the example the recruiting equilibrium has research
rising from \reg{res_lambda_r155} at the strength \reg{str_r155} through \reg{res_lambda_r160} at \reg{str_r160} to
\reg{res_lambda_r165} at \reg{str_r165}, and entry from \reg{res_entry_r155} through \reg{res_entry_r160} to
\reg{res_entry_r165} (Corollary~\ref{cor:research-short}); zero research is also an equilibrium at these three
strengths. In the recruiting equilibrium, research reaches its cap at the strength about \reg{res_rcap} and stays
there up to the entry ceiling. In the example, the unique outcome has no
research against the weak incumbent and research at the cap against the strong incumbent.

The rise in entry survives other laws of preparation costs, with or without atoms, when every low cost lies below
gross profit at the bottom posterior against the strong incumbent and every high cost lies between gross profit at
the prior against the weak incumbent and gross profit at the top posterior against the strong incumbent
(Section~\ref{sec:costs}). For uniform incumbents, every prior and every pair of classes that satisfy the class
ranking and differ above the reserve admit incumbent strengths and costs at which the reversal holds
(Section~\ref{sec:classes}); with more than two classes, it holds under a separation condition. At the prior
\(1/2\), it also survives private signals that the challenger and the investor hold about different facts, under
conditions on the costs (Section~\ref{sec:signals}); in the signal example, with its own costs
and strengths, the challenger's signal is more accurate than the investor's, and the rise in that example needs both
signals.

Under the hypotheses of Proposition~\ref{prop:ccc}, access to prices raises expected target proceeds and
acquisition surplus net of preparation costs against the strong incumbent (Section~\ref{sec:access}). Sale terms
shape both what a buyer pays when it wins and what
the stock price can reveal before anyone prepares. With a single value in each class and the incumbent's highest
value between them, an alternative bargaining institution shows how the payment rule matters: the effect of
competition on the target-payoff spread depends on the weight the rule places on the runner-up's value
(Section~\ref{sec:payment}). A reserve that excludes the low class can make the price informative against both
incumbents, and if informed trade is then full and only a purchase recruits at both strengths, entry is flat in
incumbent strength (Section~\ref{sec:reserves}).

When the investor always buys after good news and always sells after bad news and the prior lies below the
preparation threshold, a purchase raises the price by more than a sale lowers it, once each impact is weighted by how often its order
occurs, exactly when the purchase recruits the challenger; the test needs only prices and order frequencies
(Section~\ref{sec:empirical}). The difference comes from the entry component of the price gap between a purchase and
a sale; in the example at the strong incumbent, that component is \reg{ba_share_r1_B} of the price gap. Under the
same trading pattern, a prepared challenger wins more often after a purchase than after a sale whenever the
incumbent's value sometimes falls where a high-class value beats it with a higher probability than a low-class
value, and where entry stays at its floor after every order, orders predict the winner under the same condition,
although they do not predict entry. Under the same trading pattern, order counts identify the informed investor's
presence when the prior is known and differs from one half,
and at the prior \(1/2\) the sale impact identifies it, given the payoff scale and entry at its floor after a sale
and after no order.

The paper belongs to the literature on the real effects of financial markets \citep{BondEdmansGoldstein2012}.
The closest antecedent is \citet{DowGoldsteinGuembel2017}, where a firm's investment decision feeds back into
the incentive to produce information about its stock; here the sale rule divides acquisition surplus between a
traded claim and a challenger that decides whether to prepare. \citet{EdmansGoldsteinJiang2015} show that corrective
real decisions can discourage trade on bad news under rational pricing; here the real decision is the challenger's
entry itself.

Firms learn from peers' stock prices \citep{FoucaultFresard2014}, and target prices affect takeover activity
\citep{EdmansGoldsteinJiang2012}; in this model, a prospective challenger learns about its own acquisition value
from the target's price before it commits.\footnote{Bidders also learn from announcement returns when they decide
whether to complete a deal \citep{Luo2005}. That decision comes after the entry margin of this paper.}

The auction-entry literature makes the bidder pool endogenous
\citep{Samuelson1985,McAfeeMcMillan1987,LevinSmith1994,GentryStroup2019}. Under selective entry, a bidder's
pre-entry information indexes the distribution of its value, and the payment of the entry cost reveals the value
\citep{RobertsSweeting2013}.\footnote{One limit of this framework is a bidder that knows only the common value
distribution before entry \citep{LevinSmith1994}. The other limit is a bidder that already knows its value
\citep{Samuelson1985}. Selective entry also affects the choice of sale procedure \citep{RobertsSweeting2013}, and
auction formats affect bidders' incentives to acquire information \citep{Persico2000}.} Here the pre-entry
information is the target's stock price, a public price that moves with the orders of an investor who observes
the challenger's quality class. I keep the direct deterrence force of the auction-entry literature and add a
market that operates before preparation, with the informed trader outside the auction and trading a payoff that
differs from the challenger's profit.\footnote{Recent work studies how bidders learn about their own values and
about competitors \citep{PernoudGleyze2026}, post-auction feedback in security-payment design
\citep{LiuBernhardt2022}, and bidder-pool choice with correlated values \citep{CarlinEtAl2026}. In takeovers,
\citet{BettonEtAl2014} study negotiations with stock-market feedback, and \citet{LinMaYangZhu2025} model payment
choice and trade within an initiated deal; I hold cash consideration fixed and study entry.}

The investor in this paper is an outside trader and earns only the trading residual. An informed trader can also hold a stake before a control decision: an acquirer can use
liquidity trade to conceal share purchases before a bid \citep{KyleVila1991}, large holders trade on their
information \citep{Edmans2009}, and arbitrageurs' positions can affect tendering
\citep{CornelliLi2002}.\footnote{Large holders also monitor or intervene as prices feed back to firm decisions
\citetext{\citealp[Section~II.E]{EdmansGoldsteinJiang2015}; \citealp{Maug1998}; \citealp{BackEtAl2018}}.
\citet{GoldsteinGuembel2008} study manipulation through the real decision that responds to the price.}

two numbers through which trading sees the auction. Section~\ref{sec:results} states the main results and introduces
the example. Sections~\ref{sec:research} to~\ref{sec:empirical} cover research, robustness, welfare and sale terms,
and empirical implications, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes. Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} states
Propositions~\ref{prop:coexistence} and~\ref{prop:research}, the three corollaries and the results of
Sections~\ref{sec:robustness} to~\ref{sec:empirical} in full, and proves every result.
