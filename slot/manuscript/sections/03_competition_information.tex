\section{Competition and information}\label{sec:payoffs}

\subsection{Acquisition profits and target value}\label{sec:profits}

The auction determines both the return to preparation and the claim that informed traders trade. Consider a
prepared rival with value \(\theta\ge p\). If it wins, it pays \(\max\{p,R\}\); if it loses, the
incumbent pays \(\theta\). The target therefore receives \(\max\{p,\min(R,\theta)\}\), and the rival
keeps \((\theta-\max\{p,R\})_+\). A prepared rival with \(\theta<p\) cannot bid, and the target then
receives the reserve if the incumbent's value meets it, exactly as without entry. For every value at or above
the reserve, the two expected amounts add up to the value:
\[
t(\theta)+g(\theta)=\theta,\qquad \theta\ge p.
\]

The second-price rule passes the whole value of an admissible rival to the target and the rival
together. An admissible value also raises proceeds to at least the reserve, so \(t_j\ge t_0\) for both
classes.

A stronger incumbent never raises the probability that a prepared rival wins, and when the incumbent wins it
pays the rival's bid.
Proposition~\ref{prop:returns} shows that it weakly lowers acquisition profit at every fixed belief and weakly
raises the sensitivity of target proceeds to the quality of the rival, for every continuous incumbent distribution; the
second effect uses the class ranking~\eqref{eq:classes}.

\begin{proposition}[Competition and the two returns to information]\label{prop:returns}
Let the incumbent's value have a continuous distribution \(F\) on \([0,\bar r]\), extended by unity above
\(\bar r\). For any classes \(G_H,G_L\) with finite means,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:spread}
\begin{aligned}
\DT(F)&=pF(p)D(p^-)+\int_p^\infty[1-F(y)]\,D(y)\,dy,\\
g_j(F)&=\int_p^\infty[1-G_j(y)]\,F(y)\,dy,\qquad j\in\{H,L\}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Let \(\tilde F\le F\) be a continuous first-order stochastic strengthening of \(F\).
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=2em]
\item For every pair of classes, the strengthening weakly decreases \(g_H\) and \(g_L\), and hence gross
acquisition profit at every fixed belief.
\item Under the class ranking~\eqref{eq:classes}, the strengthening weakly increases the target-payoff spread.
\end{enumerate}
The decrease in \(g_j\) is strict when \(\int_p^\infty[1-G_j(y)][F(y)-\tilde F(y)]\,dy>0\). The increase in
(b) is strict when \(\int_p^\infty[F(y)-\tilde F(y)]\,D(y)\,dy>0\).
\end{proposition}

At one incumbent value \(R\) that meets the reserve, the target receives
\(\min\{R,\max\{\theta,p\}\}\), and the expected class difference in proceeds is \(\int_p^RD(y)\,dy\). Each
slice \(D(y)\,dy\) of the class value gap at a level \(y>p\) reaches the target when the incumbent outbids
the rival at that level and pays its bid, and it stays with the rival otherwise.

A stronger incumbent moves slices to the target, and under the class ranking it never narrows the spread and never
raises acquisition profit. The
reserve adds one term: below the reserve, only an admissible rival buys, at \(p\), and the class
difference is \(pD(p^-)\). A stronger incumbent fails the reserve less often, so that term shrinks, and it
can outweigh the moved slices when the low class is more often inadmissible. Under the class
ranking~\eqref{eq:classes}, every value meets the reserve, and the term is zero.
Appendix~\ref{app:payoffs} gives the proof and adds a part (c): when neither class has a value atom at the reserve,
the spread weakly increases under every strengthening of every continuous incumbent distribution if and only if
the high class dominates above the reserve and the classes have equal admissibility (Section~\ref{sec:values}).

The sum of the two lines of \eqref{eq:spread} under the class ranking~\eqref{eq:classes} is
\[
\DT(F)+g_H(F)-g_L(F)=\E_H[\max\{\theta,p\}]-\E_L[\max\{\theta,p\}].
\]
The identity holds for every incumbent distribution, and its right side does not depend on the incumbent. A
stronger incumbent leaves the value gap between the classes unchanged; it moves the gap from the rival's
profit to target proceeds.

A stronger incumbent therefore never narrows the spread and never raises gross acquisition profit at any belief.
Whether entry rises depends on how this change in payoffs affects the information in the price.

\subsection{What the rival learns from the price}\label{sec:inference}

The rival sees one price. A liquidity order in the same direction is always possible, and this bounds
what any order can reveal. Each class reaches a given nonzero order through a liquidity trader with
probability \(b\), and the informed trader adds at most \(\lambda\), so the likelihood ratio of the order
lies between \(1/(1+u)\) and \(1+u\). By Bayes' rule, the posterior after every order lies in the interval
\([m(\pi),M(\pi)]\) of \eqref{eq:bounds}, under every strategy of the informed trader, mixed or pure; when the high class
buys and the low class sells, the posteriors after a sale, no order, and a purchase are \(m(\pi)\), \(\pi\),
and \(M(\pi)\). The rival's belief at a price averages the posteriors of the orders with that price, so
it obeys the same bounds. No price can therefore support a preparation decision that needs a belief above
the top posterior. This gives the exclusion bound: when \(g_H\ge g_L\), gross profit does not fall with
the belief, so once gross profit at the top posterior falls below \(c_H\), no order recruits a high-cost
rival, in or out of equilibrium.

When \(g_H>g_L\), gross profit covers the high cost exactly when the belief reaches the preparation
threshold \(\tau=(c_H-g_L)/(g_H-g_L)\): \(B(\mu)\ge c_H\) if and only if \(\mu\ge\tau\). Along a path I write
\(\tau(r)\). A zero-cost rival prepares after every price, so entry after every order
is at least \(\rho\), and competitive pricing gives \eqref{eq:price}. When the classes differ in target
proceeds, the price rises strictly with the posterior. Suppose two orders have one price. Entry after them
is the same, because entry depends on the order only through the price, and at least \(\rho\); the price
formula then gives the two orders the same posterior. The rival therefore reads the market makers'
posterior from the price, and its gross profit at the price \(P(x)\) is \(B(\mu(x))\), so high-cost
preparation follows exactly the orders whose posterior reaches \(\tau\).
Appendix~\ref{app:beliefs} gives the formal statements and their proofs.

The zero-cost rival gives each step its floor: entry stays at least \(\rho\), so the price reveals the
posterior, and the gross return of a correctly signed order stays at least a fixed share of the spread, the
trading bound of Section~\ref{sec:informed}. Its preparation does not respond to the price, so information
can only add entry.

\subsection{Informed trading}\label{sec:informed}

Rational pricing removes the anticipated increase in proceeds from the informed trader's advantage. What remains is
the gross return of a purchase in class \(H\) and of a sale in class \(L\),
\[
K_H=e(1)\,\DT\,[1-\mu(1)],\qquad K_L=e(-1)\,\DT\,\mu(-1),\qquad \rho\,\kappa(\pi)\,\DT\le K_H,\,K_L\le\DT,
\]
with \(\kappa(\pi)=\min\{m(\pi),1-M(\pi)\}\). A wrong-signed order earns at most \(-k\), so it never pays.
Class \(H\) buys when \(K_H\) exceeds \(k\) and abstains when it falls short, and class \(L\) compares
\(K_L\) with \(k\) in the same way.

Market makers price the entry response, and the posterior bounds of Section~\ref{sec:inference} leave some
uncertainty about the class after every order. The informed trader's gross return is the entry probability
times the spread times that residual uncertainty. The lower bound uses the entry floor \(\rho\) and the
posterior bounds, and the spread bounds the return from above. Both bounds hold under every candidate profile, mixed or
pure, and their comparison with \(k\) decides when information survives in equilibrium.

Since \(1-M(\pi)=m(1-\pi)\), the bound takes the form \(\kappa(\pi)=m(\min\{\pi,1-\pi\})\). It is largest
at the prior \(1/2\), where it equals \(m(1/2)=1/(2+u)\), and smaller at every other prior. The class that
the prior favors binds, because the price already leans toward it.

The no-entry proceeds \(t_0\) cancel from each return, because they move the price and the terminal payoff
together; the terms that matter are the entry gains \(t_H-t_0\) and \(t_L-t_0\), which are nonnegative.

Each return depends only on the posterior and entry after its own order, so entry after the zero order
enters no payoff, and the informed trader's problem splits by class.
Appendix~\ref{app:beliefs} gives the formal statement and proof of the trading bound, and the coexistence results of
Section~\ref{sec:coexistence} rest on this split.

\subsection{What the market sees of the auction}\label{sec:twonumbers}

When the spread is positive and class \(H\)'s gross profit exceeds class \(L\)'s, the informed trader's orders, the market
makers' posteriors and the rival's preparation depend on the auction only through the target-payoff spread
\(\DT\) and the preparation threshold \(\tau\). The spread sets what an informed order earns at each posterior and
level of entry, and the threshold sets which posteriors recruit a high-cost rival. Two auctions with the same
spread and the same threshold, and with every other primitive and the tie rules the same, therefore have the same
equilibrium orders, posteriors and entry, and, when the informed trader chooses research, the same research. Prices add the
levels \(t_0\) and \(t_L\), and ownership adds which bidder wins, so results on ownership carry class \(H\)'s chance
of beating the incumbent.

A stronger incumbent never narrows the spread (Proposition~\ref{prop:returns}). It never lowers the threshold while a
rival known to be in class \(L\) earns at most its high preparation cost, \(g_L\le c_H\): it never raises class
\(L\)'s profit and never widens the profit gap between the classes, so a rival needs at least as favorable a
belief before preparation at the high cost pays. Along a path, once \(g_L\le c_H\) holds, it holds against every
stronger incumbent. Coexistence in Section~\ref{sec:coexistence} and research in Section~\ref{sec:research} are
statements about these two numbers, and Appendix~\ref{app:beliefs} gives the formal statements and their proofs.
