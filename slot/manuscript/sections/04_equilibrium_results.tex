\section{Equilibrium results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Competition creates competition}\label{sec:ccc}

The main result compares three economies that differ only in incumbent strength. Against the weak incumbent the
price carries no information and only a zero-cost challenger prepares; against the strong incumbent the price is
informative and a purchase recruits a high-cost challenger; against the very strong incumbent the price stays
informative, while entry returns to its floor. I write \(F_0\), \(F_1\) and \(F_2\) for the three incumbent
distributions.

\begin{proposition}[Competition creates competition]\label{prop:ccc}
Let the classes satisfy the class ranking~\eqref{eq:classes}, let \(F_1\) be a continuous first-order stochastic
strengthening of the continuous incumbent distribution \(F_0\), and let \(F_2\) strengthen \(F_1\) in the same sense. Fix
\(0<\pi<1\), \(0<\rho<1\), \(k>0\) and a slot \((\lambda,\beta)\), and suppose that the high cost lies between gross
profit at the prior against \(F_0\) and gross profit at the top posterior against \(F_1\), that the trading cost lies
between the target-payoff spread at \(F_0\) and the fraction \(\rho\kappa(\pi)\) of the spread at \(F_1\), and that
gross profit at the top posterior against \(F_2\) falls short of the high cost:
\begin{align*}
&\text{(H1)}\qquad B_{F_0}(\pi)<c_H<B_{F_1}(M(\pi)),\\
&\text{(H2)}\qquad \DT(F_0)<k<\rho\,\kappa(\pi)\,\DT(F_1),\\
&\text{(H3)}\qquad B_{F_2}(M(\pi))<c_H.
\end{align*}
Each economy then has a unique equilibrium outcome.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=2em]
\item \emph{The weak incumbent \(F_0\).} The investor does not trade, and both classes abstain with probability one.
Every posterior equals \(\pi\) and entry equals \(\rho\) after every order, and every order has the price
\(t_0+\rho\,[t_L-t_0+\DT(F_0)\pi]\), so the price carries no information.
\item \emph{The strong incumbent \(F_1\).} The investor trades in both directions: class \(H\) buys and class \(L\)
sells, each with probability one. The posteriors after a sale, no order and a purchase are \(m(\pi)\), \(\pi\) and
\(M(\pi)\), and only a purchase recruits the high-cost challenger, so entry after the three orders is \(\rho\),
\(\rho\) and \(1\), and \(P(-1)<P(0)<P(1)\). Entry is
\[
\rho+(1-\rho)(b+\pi\lambda)>\rho.
\]
Entry given class \(H\) is \(\rho+(1-\rho)(b+\lambda)\). High-class ownership rises from \(\pi\rho W_H(F_0)\)
to \(\pi[\rho+(1-\rho)(b+\lambda)]W_H(F_1)\) if and only if
\[
[\rho+(1-\rho)(b+\lambda)]\,W_H(F_1)>\rho\,W_H(F_0).
\]
\item \emph{The very strong incumbent \(F_2\).} The investor again trades in both directions, with posteriors
\(m(\pi)\), \(\pi\) and \(M(\pi)\) after a sale, no order and a purchase. Entry equals \(\rho\) after every order, and
the three prices differ, so the price stays informative while entry returns to \(\rho\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

The high-cost window (H1) sets entry through acquisition profit: gross
profit at the prior falls short of the high cost against \(F_0\), so an uninformative price recruits no high-cost
challenger, while gross profit at the top posterior exceeds the high cost against \(F_1\), so a purchase can recruit
one. The trading-cost window (H2) fixes trading before any order profile is known: by the trading bound of
Section~\ref{sec:informed}, every correct order earns at most \(\DT(F_0)-k<0\) against the weak incumbent and at
least \(\rho\kappa(\pi)\DT(F_1)-k>0\) against the strong incumbent, so a correct order cannot cover the trading cost
in the first economy and covers it in the second. The ceiling (H3) stops recruitment against \(F_2\), where gross
profit at the top posterior falls short of the high cost (the ceiling of Section~\ref{sec:inference}).

The trading-cost window is nonempty exactly when \(\DT(F_0)/\DT(F_1)<\rho\kappa(\pi)\), that is, when competition
widens the target-payoff spread enough between the two incumbent distributions. The condition involves the two
distributions, the classes, \(\rho\), the order slot and \(\pi\), and no unit of money, and a prior away from one
half narrows the window, since \(\kappa(\pi)\) falls when the price already leans toward the favored class. For
uniform incumbents, (H1)--(H3) are strict inequalities between continuous functions of
\((r_0,r_1,r_2,c_H,k,\rho,\pi,\lambda,\beta)\), so they define an open set; Section~\ref{sec:benchmark} gives its
slice through the benchmark.

The additional entry favors the high class, which places a purchase with probability \(b+\lambda\), while the low
class places one with probability \(b\). Ownership needs its own condition when the high class can lose to the
incumbent, because a stronger incumbent then lowers \(W_H\). The condition in part~(ii) holds whenever the stronger
incumbent leaves the high class a good chance to win, \((1+\rho)W_H(F_1)\ge2\rho W_H(F_0)>0\), and the high class
places a purchase more often than not, \(b+\lambda>1/2\); in the point-mass benchmark \(W_H=1\), so ownership rises.

Part~(iii) completes the pattern, in which entry first rises and later falls as the incumbent strengthens. Against
the very strong incumbent even the top posterior fails to justify high-cost preparation, while trading stays
informative, and Section~\ref{sec:coexistence} describes the strengths between the three economies.
Appendix~\ref{app:prop2} states the three equilibrium tuples and gives the proof. Every comparison behind the
result is strict, so the unique outcome in each economy is the same however ties are broken.

\subsection{The benchmark and the fixed-information control}\label{sec:benchmark}

The benchmark has point-mass classes \(h=\reg{prim_h}\) and \(\ell=\reg{prim_ell}\) at the prior
\(\pi=\reg{prim_pi}\), with reserve \(p=\reg{prim_p}\), zero-cost probability \(\rho=\reg{prim_rho}\), high cost
\(c_H=\reg{prim_cH}\) and trading cost \(k=\reg{prim_k}\). The incumbent is uniform on \([0,r]\), at the strengths
\(r_0=\reg{prim_r0}\), \(r_1=\reg{prim_r1}\) and \(r_2=\reg{prim_r2}\), and money is measured in units of the low
value.

In the benchmark slot the largest likelihood ratio of an order is \(1+u=\eu\), with \(\eu\) printed upright
to separate it from entry \(e(x)\), and the liquidity probability is the exact decimal
\(\beta=\reg{beta_B}\). The presence probability is therefore \(\lambda=u\beta/(1+u\beta)=\reg{lambda_B}\), with
\(b=\reg{b_B}\), and the posterior bounds at the prior are \(M(1/2)=\eu/(1+\eu)=\reg{M_half_B}\) and
\(m(1/2)=\reg{m_half_B}\). Every computation keeps \(\lambda\) unrounded, and every statement that depends on
\(\eu\) uses interval arithmetic; Appendix~\ref{app:parameters} lists these declarations.

Both ends of the band are preparation thresholds: the lower end \(\tau(r_1)\) is the belief that a purchase needs
to recruit a high-cost challenger against the strong incumbent, and the upper end \(\tau(r_2)\) is the belief at
which a purchase would still recruit one against the very strong incumbent. The band between the two thresholds
has the width it has because the threshold rises slowly with strength: a high-class challenger outbids every
incumbent on the path, and its expected payment \(r/2+p^2/(2r)\) rises by less than one half per unit of strength,
a change that is small next to the gap \(g_H-g_L\) in Table~\ref{tab:payoffs}.

Gross profit at every belief falls strictly with strength above \(\ell\), so the ceiling (H3) holds at every
strength in \((r_C,h)\), where the entry ceiling \(r_C=\reg{rC_B}\) is the strength at which \(\tau(r)=M(1/2)\);
parts~(i) and~(ii) of Proposition~\ref{prop:ccc} do not use (H3). Table~\ref{tab:margins} in
Appendix~\ref{app:parameters} records every hypothesis and its positive margin at the benchmark.

Figure~\ref{fig:beliefs} shows the preparation threshold along the path: the prior lies below it at every
strength, so an uninformative price never recruits a high-cost challenger, and the threshold rises with strength
until it meets the top posterior at the entry ceiling \(r_C\), just below \(r_2\). Panel~(b) shows the band of

Holding the information in the price fixed while the incumbent strengthens removes the reversal. The experiment
fixes the law of the class and of a signal that the challenger observes, the class value laws and the cost law.
Given the class, the signal tells the challenger nothing more about either value: its own value depends on the
signal only through the class, the incumbent's value is independent of the class and the signal, and the two
values are independent; the cost is independent of the class, both values and the signal. A stronger incumbent then can only lower entry, for every such experiment and every independent cost law
(Proposition~\ref{prop:control} in Appendix~\ref{app:robustness} gives the formal condition): it lowers gross
profit at every belief, so it can only remove a preparation incentive, and entry falls by exactly the probability
that the cost lies between gross profit at the two strengths.

In the order slot the experiment fixes \(\lambda\), \(\beta\), \(\pi\), the cost law and both order distributions,
and competitive prices then reveal the same posterior after each order at every strength
(Appendix~\ref{app:beliefs}).

\subsection{Coexistence}\label{sec:coexistence}

Proposition~\ref{prop:ccc} compares the weak and strong economies at \(r_0\) and \(r_1\), and between those
strengths an informative equilibrium coexists with an uninformative one. I now describe entry along the whole
benchmark path \(r\in[r_0,r_2]\) at the prior \(1/2\), for every slot in the band of
Proposition~\ref{prop:region}(a); Appendix~\ref{app:coexistence} lists the complete equilibrium set.

Coexistence starts at \(r_A\), where a purchase that just reaches the preparation threshold and recruits a
high-cost challenger earns exactly the trading cost. Full purchases start at \(r_F\), where a full purchase that
recruits earns the trading cost, and no informed trade ends at \(r_N\), where a unit order against an uninformative
price earns it, so no informed trade stops being an equilibrium above \(r_N\). The remaining strength in the statement is
the entry ceiling \(r_C\) of Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}, above which even a purchase at the top posterior cannot
recruit a high-cost challenger. Of the strengths that Figure~\ref{fig:coexistence} marks, \(r_A\), \(r_N\) and
\(r_J\) do not depend on the slot. The figure also marks \(r_J\) and the sale threshold \(r_S\), which bound
the mixed patterns that the figure draws with the informative branch; Appendix~\ref{app:coexistence} defines
every marked strength and every pattern in the figure.

\begin{proposition}[Coexistence on the benchmark path]\label{prop:coexistence}
Take the benchmark auction, the prior \(1/2\) and any slot in the band of Proposition~\ref{prop:region}(a). An
equilibrium exists at every strength on the path from \(r_0\) to \(r_2\), and Appendix~\ref{app:coexistence} lists
the whole equilibrium set.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=2em]
\item No informed trade and an informative equilibrium coexist exactly on \([r_A,r_N]\).
\item On the informative branch that coexists with no trade, entry is
\(\rho+(1-\rho)\min\{b\DT(r)/(2k),\,b+\lambda/2\}\). It is continuous, rises strictly on \([r_A,r_F]\) and then
stays constant on \([r_F,r_N]\), keeps this value along full purchases up to \(r_C\), and equals \(\rho\) above
\(r_C\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

At the benchmark slot the marked strengths are \(r_A=\reg{rA}\),
\(r_F=\reg{rF_B}\), \(r_N=\reg{rN}\), \(r_J=\reg{rJ}\), \(r_S=\reg{rS_B}\) and \(r_C=\reg{rC_B}\). Entry on the
informative branch is \(\reg{entry_rA_B}\) at \(r_A\), \(\reg{entry_mb_r140_B}\) at \(\reg{str_r140}\) and
\(\reg{entry_mb_r145_B}\) at \(\reg{str_r145}\), and from \(r_F\) it stays at \(\reg{entry_r1_B}\) until the entry
ceiling \(r_C\). The low class abstains up to \(r_N\), mixes on \((r_N,r_S)\), and sells with probability one from
\(r_S\).

Figure~\ref{fig:coexistence} shows entry along the path, and coexistence comes from the entry response itself.
Against an uninformative price with entry \(\rho\), a unit order earns \(\rho\DT/2\), which stays below \(k\) up to
\(r_N\), so no informed trade survives as an equilibrium until \(r_N\); an informative equilibrium changes the
entry response to a purchase and with it the return to trade, so it can hold at the same strengths.

Entry rises strictly along the informative branch while the high class mixes, because the purchase rate adjusts.
At \(r_A\) a purchase that just reaches the preparation threshold earns exactly \(k\); above \(r_A\) the high
class mixes and the posterior after a purchase stays above the threshold, so the purchase earns
\(\DT[1-\mu(1)]=k\) and a wider target-payoff spread needs a more informative purchase. The purchase rate
\(b\,[\DT(r)/k-2]\) therefore rises with \(r\), and entry rises with it, until the purchase rate reaches
\(\lambda\) at \(r_F\); from there entry stays constant.

Above \(r_N\) no informed trade fails as an equilibrium. On \((r_N,r_J)\) the investor can also mix in both
directions without recruitment, and from \(r_J\) on a full purchase is the only high-class order.

Coexistence also holds away from the even prior. At the benchmark slot the informative branch starts at the same
strength \(r_A\) for every prior between \(\approx\reg{piA_lo_B}\) and \(\approx\reg{piA_hi}\), and it starts
later, where the prior reaches the preparation threshold, for priors up to \(\approx\reg{pimax}\). Below the first
of these priors even the top posterior cannot reach the threshold once a purchase can pay for itself, and from the
last prior on the uninformative equilibrium fails before the prior falls below the threshold;
Corollary~\ref{prop:priors} in Appendix~\ref{app:coexistence} gives the coexistence set at every prior.
