## Title — Competition Creates Competition: Stock Prices and the Discovery of Takeover Bidders

*0.5 minute · ends at 0:30*

Thank you. I am Austin Li, from the Department of Economics at University College London. This talk concerns a public sale before the bidder set closes. One bidder is already prepared. I call it the incumbent bidder, or the incumbent. A potential rival bidder, the rival, must pay to prepare. Between those two stages, the target stock price can affect who enters the sale.

## Frame 1 — Does a stronger incumbent always keep the rival out?

*2.5 minutes · ends at 3:00*

A listed company announces that it is open to a sale. The incumbent has done its homework. The rival must decide whether to pay for months of diligence. Meanwhile the stock trades, and a sector fund that follows the company's customers trades it too.

Does a stronger incumbent always keep the rival out?

The seller cares about discovery. A public sale tries to find one more bidder before the bidder set closes. That bidder can compete for the target and affect what shareholders receive. Bulow and Klemperer show why one extra bidder can carry first-order value. Here the sale rule stays fixed. The question is whether a stronger incumbent helps discover the rival or ends the search.

The rival cares about the diligence bill. It must check contracts, liabilities and technology, secure finance and approvals, and commit scarce capacity. Those costs come before an executable offer. A stronger incumbent can reduce the return from that work. But the rival can also learn from the stock price before it pays. The price may help it decide whether good synergy justifies the cost.

The market matters because prices move before bids move. The sector fund can know facts about customers and demand that bear on the rival's synergy. Its trade can put some of that knowledge into the price. The price can then change the decision to prepare.

The seller needs discovery, the rival needs a reason to pay, and the market can supply news before commitment. The question connects all three.

## Frame 2 — The received answer: strength cannot raise entry

*1.5 minutes · ends at 4:30*

The received answer starts with the return to diligence.

The rival pays before it can bid. A stronger incumbent can make a profitable win less likely and raise the payment when the rival wins. The same work then earns weakly less. Some preparation decisions can cease to pay.

Fishman gives the classic takeover deterrence argument. Hirshleifer and Png also study costly preparation. In Fishman, the first bidder chooses a preemptive bid. Here strength is a public distribution of value, and the realized value stays private until bids.

The logic holds when what the rival knows stays fixed. At each fixed belief, a stronger incumbent weakly lowers expected rival gross profit. With the information, values and cost law fixed, entry cannot rise.

I keep that force. I add a target stock price before preparation and let its information respond to competition. The next step is to explain why a stronger incumbent can make that price say more.

## Frame 3 — Where the paper sits: price feedback meets costly entry

*1.0 minute · ends at 5:30*

Dow, Goldstein and Guembel are the closest antecedent: real decisions feed back into information production. The trading set-up comes from Edmans, Goldstein and Jiang.

Fishman supplies deterrence; Roberts and Sweeting supply selective entry. Here the price supplies information before costly preparation.

Edmans, Goldstein and Jiang also connect target prices to takeover activity. Kyle and Vila study an acquirer's share purchases. Here an outside informed trader starts with no net position.

The new link is the sale. It splits one value between shareholders, whose claim trades, and the rival, who decides whether to enter. Competition changes how much the price tells the rival.

## Frame 4 — This paper: a stronger incumbent can recruit the rival

*1.5 minutes · ends at 7:00*

A stronger incumbent weakly cuts the rival's profit at each belief. It can also widen the shareholder spread, the difference in shareholder proceeds between good and poor synergy. That wider spread can make informed trade worth its cost. The price can then recruit the rival.

Three results carry the argument. First, on an open set of inputs, entry rises from the weak to the strong incumbent, then returns to its floor, with a unique outcome at each of the three strengths. Second, one shift never raises rival profit and never narrows the shareholder spread. Third, freeze the information, and strength cannot raise entry. The ordinary deterrence force remains throughout.

Costly research extends the mechanism where a purchase recruits: more research brings more entry. A visible price also raises expected proceeds and net surplus against the strong incumbent. A weighted price-impact comparison then locates recruitment in the purchase impact.

I will set out the model, explain the mechanism and main result, state its fixed-information control, then turn to extensions and the prediction.

## Frame 5 — Players and the sale rule

*1.5 minutes · ends at 8:30*

Standalone value is zero. Values and costs are per share. All agents are risk neutral.

The incumbent is already prepared and faces no further cost. Its value R follows public continuous distribution F, from zero to r bar, its upper bound. R stays private until bids. Strength means a first-order stochastic shift toward higher values.

The rival sees the price, then decides whether to prepare. Its class j is H, good synergy, or L, poor synergy. Prior pi is the chance of H, between zero and one. Value theta has law G sub j, with finite mean. The class ranking puts G sub H weakly below G sub L at every value level y. Every value meets reserve p. The sale and the rival's identity are public.

Cost C is zero with probability rho, the entry floor; otherwise it is positive high cost c sub H. Payment reveals theta and permits a bid.

The seller commits before trade to a cash second-price auction with positive reserve p. The type-value pair, R, C and exogenous slot draws are independent. Neither bidder trades target shares.

## Frame 6 — Timing: one trading slot, then preparation

*2.0 minutes · ends at 10:30*

The sequence puts the stock price before the preparation decision.

The seller first commits to reserve p. Next comes the order X: minus one is a sale, zero is no order, and one is a purchase. Then market makers set stock price P. The rival sees P, learns cost C, and decides whether to prepare. Bids and payoffs follow.

Presence lambda, between zero and one, is the chance that the informed trader receives the order opportunity. It observes class j and chooses order q from those same three alternatives. A nonzero order costs positive k, the order cost.

With probability one minus lambda, a liquidity trader receives the opportunity instead. It sells or buys with probability beta each. It places no order with probability one minus twice beta. Beta is positive and at most one third. No one observes which trader placed the order.

Competitive market makers see only the order X. Price P of X equals expected V sub T conditional on X. V sub T is the eventual sale proceeds per share. That expectation includes the preparation response to the price.

One opportunity thus admits informed trade and liquidity trade of the same size. A purchase can carry news, but a liquidity purchase can copy it. This uncertainty will preserve the informed trader's advantage.

## Frame 7 — Information and choices

*2.0 minutes · ends at 12:30*

Each player sees a different part of the sale.

The incumbent knows value R and bids R if it meets reserve p. Its value distribution F is public, but the realization is private.

The informed trader sees class j, good or poor synergy. It does not see the rival's value theta. It chooses order q and pays order cost k on a nonzero order. It holds no initial net position and cannot acquire the target.

The informed trader is an outsider with sector expertise, such as a sector-focused long-short fund that researches the target's customers, technology and demand.

The liquidity trader has no private information about synergy. Its probabilities for a sale, no order and a purchase are beta, one minus twice beta and beta.

Market makers observe the order X and cannot identify the trader. They choose competitive price P of X, expected sale proceeds given that order.

The rival sees the price, then its cost C. It learns theta only after preparation, and then bids theta. Its own diligence reveals value after the price can affect its commitment.

Order belief mu of x means the probability of type H, good synergy, conditional on the order X equal to x. Market makers use that belief. The rival instead conditions on the price. If several orders share a price, its belief averages across those orders. We keep these beliefs distinct until the price-revelation result applies.

## Frame 8 — Payoffs

*2.0 minutes · ends at 14:30*

Take a prepared rival with value theta at least reserve p. The number line separates what shareholders receive from what the rival keeps.

Target proceeds t of theta average the larger of p and the smaller of incumbent value R and theta. Gross auction profit g of theta averages the positive part of theta minus the larger of p and R. The positive part means the maximum of that amount and zero. Both expectations are over R.

The sum, t of theta plus g of theta, equals theta. Profit excludes preparation cost. The second-price rule divides one value between these two claims.

Class proceeds t sub j and class profit g sub j average these amounts within class j. H means good synergy; L means poor synergy. Without entry, proceeds t sub zero equal reserve p times one minus F evaluated at p.

Shareholder spread Delta T equals t sub H minus t sub L. Rival gross profit B at belief mu equals g sub L plus mu times the difference g sub H minus g sub L.

The informed trader earns signed order q times the difference between proceeds V sub T and price P of q, less k times absolute q. A purchase earns proceeds minus price; a sale earns price minus proceeds. No order earns zero.

## Frame 9 — Equilibrium with competitive prices

*1.5 minutes · ends at 16:00*

This is a perfect Bayesian equilibrium with break-even prices set by competitive market makers.

Price P of x equals expected V sub T given the order x. Start with t sub zero, then add entry e of x times a bracket. The bracket is t sub L minus t sub zero, plus spread Delta T times belief mu of x. Entry e is at least rho.

Bayes' rule determines beliefs after orders and prices. Liquidity trade makes every order possible in each class.

Zero cost prepares. High cost c sub H prepares when rival gross profit B covers it, including equality. An indifferent informed trader may mix. Each prepared bidder bids its value. Each strength needs a fresh equilibrium.

## Frame 11 — Beliefs stay in a band, and the price reveals the belief

*1.5 minutes · ends at 19:30*

Define liquidity mass b as beta times the quantity one minus lambda. Define u as lambda divided by b.

Band floor m has numerator pi; its denominator adds pi to the product of one minus pi and one plus u. Top belief M has numerator pi times one plus u; its denominator adds one minus pi.

For every order plan, m is at most belief mu, and mu is at most M. Beta at most one third extends this band to no order. Price beliefs obey it too.

With positive spread, a positive rival gross-profit gap and positive entry floor rho, equal prices imply equal order beliefs. The price reveals mu, not the synergy class.

Threshold tau equals high cost minus poor-synergy profit, divided by the rival gross-profit gap. At tau, rival gross profit equals high cost. Under the default rule, high cost prepares at or above tau. Entry is rho below tau and one otherwise. An order can leave the belief unchanged.

## Frame 12 — A correct order earns between a fixed share and all of the spread

*1.5 minutes · ends at 21:00*

## Prepared answers

### Who is the informed trader? — B5

It is an outsider with sector expertise, such as a sector-focused long-short fund. It holds no net position before trade, has no control rights and cannot acquire the target. Its own research concerns the target's customers, technology and demand. It learns the rival's synergy class, good or poor, rather than its realized value. It earns only its trading payoff. Neither bidder trades target shares.

### Don't bidders already look at the price?

Yes. The rival reads the stock price before preparation throughout the main model. Strength changes the shareholder spread and whether informed trade pays. That changes what the price reveals about synergy. The fixed-information control keeps price access and freezes the news the rival can recover. The comparison concerns price content at unchanged access.

### So what?

The bidder set can depend on information produced outside the sale. A stronger incumbent can make that information worth more to an informed trader and draw a rival into preparation. Under the main result's conditions, price access also raises expected shareholder proceeds and acquisition surplus net of preparation costs against the strong incumbent. Thus the information before diligence helps explain who contests the sale.

### Where would deterrence show up in data?

Look where no target stock price reaches the rival before diligence, or where the information before preparation stays fixed. Hold other information, synergy values and costs fixed. Strength then cannot raise entry. Strict deterrence occurs when some costs fall above profit against the stronger incumbent but at or below profit against the weaker incumbent. Date public strength before diligence and measure preparation; public bids capture less.

### Is the equilibrium unique between the weak and the strong incumbent? — B3

There is no general uniqueness result for those intermediate strengths. The main result gives unique outcomes at three economies under strict conditions. Under the default tie rules, an uninformative market with entry at its floor can coexist with an informative market where a purchase recruits the high-cost rival. The threshold must exceed the prior but remain reachable, and the spread must lie within the coexistence bounds. B3 states those bounds.

### Why is each outcome unique? — B2

The bounds hold under every order plan, including mixtures. Against the weak incumbent, even the gain cap falls below the order cost. Against the strong incumbent, even the gain floor exceeds it, so both correct orders must occur. Their beliefs fix preparation. Against the very strong incumbent, full orders persist, but profit at the top belief cannot cover the high cost. These strict comparisons fix the equilibrium outcome under all four tie-rule pairs. Orders reveal a belief about synergy, not the synergy class itself.

### Does Proposition 4 give a unique equilibrium? — B12

It gives one research value for the equilibrium where good synergy buys after success and only a purchase recruits the high-cost rival. The spread must exceed twice the order cost, and the purchase belief must reach the threshold at that research value. The root is unique; the whole equilibrium set need not be. Other equilibria can coexist. Research rises strictly with the spread below the cap and stays fixed at the cap. A single interval of strengths needs the paper's extra continuity and recruitment-margin conditions.

### Does price access always help sellers? — B4

The strict gain uses Proposition two's conditions at the strong incumbent F one. Both economies have the same full informed orders. Access adds high-cost preparation after purchases, which raises expected proceeds and net acquisition surplus. Outside that region, the prior can already justify preparation. Price access can then deter preparation after sales and reduce expected proceeds. The paper does not claim a general proceeds gain.

### Why record no order? — B13

The order opportunity must exist before we observe its outcome. A purchase, a sale and no order all count as outcomes. No order supplies the reference price for both impacts. A sample of executed trades omits that reference and changes the order frequencies. Fix the opportunity in advance, date it before substantive diligence, and record no order as zero. Public offers measure a narrower outcome than preparation.
