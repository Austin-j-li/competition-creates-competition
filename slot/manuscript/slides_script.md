## Title — Competition Creates Competition: Stock Prices and the Discovery of Takeover Bidders

*0.5 minute · ends at 0:30*

Thank you. I am Austin Li, from the Department of Economics at University College London. This talk concerns a public sale before the bidder set closes. One bidder is already prepared: the incumbent bidder, or the incumbent. A potential rival bidder, the rival, must pay to prepare. Between those two stages, the target stock price can affect who enters the sale.

## Frame 1 — Does a stronger incumbent always keep the rival out?

*2.0 minutes · ends at 2:30*

A listed company announces that it is open to a sale. The incumbent has done its homework. The rival must decide whether to pay for months of diligence. Meanwhile the stock trades, and an informed trader that follows the company's customers trades it too.

Does a stronger incumbent always keep the rival out?

The seller cares about discovery. A public sale tries to find one more bidder before the bidder set closes. Bulow and Klemperer show why one extra bidder can carry first-order value. Here the sale rule stays fixed. The question is whether a stronger incumbent helps discover the rival or ends the search.

The rival cares about the diligence bill. It must check contracts, liabilities and technology, and secure finance and approvals, before an executable offer. A stronger incumbent can reduce the return from that work. But the rival can also learn from the stock price before it pays.

The market matters because prices move before bids move. The informed trader's order can put some of its knowledge into the price. The price can then change the decision to prepare.

The seller's proceeds can depend on rival preparation, the rival needs a reason to pay, and the market can supply news before commitment. The question connects all three.

## Frame 2 — The received answer: strength cannot raise entry

*1.5 minutes · ends at 4:00*

The received answer starts with the return to diligence.

The rival pays before it can bid. A stronger incumbent can make a profitable win less likely and raise the payment when the rival wins. The same work then earns weakly less. Some preparation decisions can cease to pay.

Fishman gives the classic takeover deterrence argument. In Fishman, the first bidder chooses a preemptive bid. Here strength is a public distribution of value, and the realized value stays private until bids.

The logic holds when what the rival knows stays fixed. With the information, values and cost distribution fixed, entry cannot rise.

I keep that force. I add a target stock price before preparation and let its information respond to competition. The next step is to explain why a stronger incumbent can make that price say more.

## Frame 3 — Where the paper sits: price feedback meets costly entry

*1.0 minute · ends at 5:00*

Dow, Goldstein and Guembel are the closest antecedent: real decisions feed back into information production. The trading slot takes its alternative arrival from Dow and Gorton, and its unit orders, presence and order cost from Edmans, Goldstein and Jiang's 2015 paper.

Fishman supplies deterrence; Roberts and Sweeting supply selective entry. Here the price supplies information before costly preparation.

Edmans, Goldstein and Jiang's 2012 paper connects target prices to takeover activity. Kyle and Vila study an acquirer's share purchases. Here an outside informed trader starts with no net position.

The new link is the sale. It splits one value between shareholders, whose claim trades, and the rival, who decides whether to enter. Competition changes how much the price tells the rival.

## Frame 4 — This paper: a stronger incumbent can recruit the rival

*1.5 minutes · ends at 6:30*

A stronger incumbent weakly cuts the rival's profit at each belief. It can also widen the shareholder spread, the difference in shareholder proceeds between good and poor synergy. That wider spread can make informed trade worth its cost. The price can then recruit the rival.

Three results carry the argument. First, on an open set of inputs, entry rises from the weak to the strong incumbent, then returns to its floor, with a unique outcome at each of the three strengths. Second, one shift never raises rival profit and never narrows the shareholder spread. Third, freeze the information, and strength cannot raise entry.

Extensions follow: costly research, gains for sellers from a visible price, and a price-impact prediction.

I will set out the model, explain the mechanism and main result, state its fixed-information control, then turn to the extensions.

## Frame 5 — Players and the sale rule

*1.0 minute · ends at 7:30*

Standalone value is zero. Values and costs are per share. All agents are risk neutral.

The incumbent is already prepared. Its value R follows public continuous distribution F, and R stays private until bids. Strength means a first-order stochastic shift toward higher values.

The rival sees the price, then decides whether to prepare. Its class j is H, good synergy, or L, poor synergy. Prior pi is the chance of H. Value theta has distribution G sub j. The class ranking puts G sub H weakly below G sub L at every value level, and every value meets reserve p.

Cost C is zero with probability rho, the entry floor; otherwise it is positive high cost c sub H. Payment reveals theta and permits a bid.

The seller commits before trade to a cash second-price auction with positive reserve p. Neither bidder trades target shares.

## Frame 6 — Timing: one trading slot, then preparation

*2.0 minutes · ends at 9:30*

The sequence puts the stock price before the preparation decision.

The seller first commits to reserve p, and the incumbent's distribution F is public. Next comes the order X: minus one is a sale, zero is no order, and one is a purchase. Then market makers set stock price P. The rival sees P, learns cost C, and decides whether to prepare. If it prepares, it learns theta and bids theta. The incumbent bids R, and payoffs follow.

Presence lambda is the chance that the informed trader receives the order opportunity. It observes class j and chooses order q from those same three alternatives. A nonzero order costs k, the order cost.

With probability one minus lambda, a liquidity trader receives the opportunity instead. It sells or buys with probability beta each, and otherwise places no order. Beta is at most one third. No one observes which trader placed the order.

Competitive market makers see only the order X. Price P of X equals expected sale proceeds V sub T given X. That expectation includes the preparation response to the price.

A purchase can carry news, but a liquidity purchase can copy it. This uncertainty will preserve the informed trader's advantage.

## Frame 8 — Payoffs

*1.5 minutes · ends at 12:30*

Take a prepared rival with value theta at least reserve p. The number line separates what shareholders receive from what the rival keeps.

Target proceeds t of theta average the larger of p and the smaller of incumbent value R and theta. Gross auction profit g of theta averages the positive part of theta minus the larger of p and R. Both expectations are over R.

The sum, t of theta plus g of theta, equals theta. The second-price rule divides one value between these two claims.

Class proceeds t sub j and class profit g sub j average these amounts within class j. Without entry, proceeds t sub zero equal reserve p times one minus F evaluated at p.

Shareholder spread Delta T equals t sub H minus t sub L. Rival gross profit B at belief mu equals g sub L plus mu times the difference g sub H minus g sub L.

The informed trader earns its signed order times proceeds minus price, less k on a nonzero order.

## Frame 9 — Equilibrium with competitive prices

*1.5 minutes · ends at 14:00*

Price P of x starts with t sub zero, then adds entry e of x times a bracket. The bracket is t sub L minus t sub zero, plus spread Delta T times belief mu of x. Entry e is at least rho.

Liquidity trade makes every order possible in each class, so Bayes' rule determines every belief.

Zero cost prepares. High cost prepares when rival gross profit covers it, including equality. An indifferent informed trader may mix. Each strength needs a fresh equilibrium.

## Frame 11 — Beliefs stay in a band, and the price reveals the belief

## Frame 12 — A correct order earns between a fixed share and all of the spread

*1.5 minutes · ends at 18:00*

These are gross gains before order cost k, for any order plan with Bayes beliefs, competitive prices and truthful bids.

## Prepared answers

### Who is the informed trader? — B5

It is an outsider with sector expertise, such as a sector-focused long-short fund. It holds no net position before trade, has no control rights and cannot acquire the target. Its own research concerns the target's customers, technology and demand. It learns the rival's synergy class, good or poor, and no further news about its value. In the point-value example, the class fixes the value. It earns only its trading payoff. Neither bidder trades target shares.

### Don't bidders already look at the price?

Yes. The rival reads the stock price before preparation throughout the main model. Strength changes the shareholder spread and whether informed trade pays. That changes what the price reveals about synergy. The fixed-information control keeps price access and freezes the news the rival can recover. The comparison concerns price content at unchanged access.

### So what?

The bidder set can depend on information produced outside the sale. A stronger incumbent can make that information worth more to an informed trader and draw a rival into preparation. Under the main result's conditions, price access also raises expected shareholder proceeds and acquisition surplus net of preparation costs against the strong incumbent. Thus the information before diligence helps explain who contests the sale.

### Where would deterrence show up in data?

Look where no target stock price reaches the rival before diligence, or where the information before preparation stays fixed. Hold other information, synergy values and costs fixed. Strength then cannot raise entry. Strict deterrence occurs when some costs fall above profit against the stronger incumbent but at or below profit against the weaker incumbent. Date public strength before diligence and measure preparation; public bids capture less.

### Is the equilibrium unique between the weak and the strong incumbent? — B3

There is no general uniqueness result for those intermediate strengths. The main result gives unique outcomes at three economies under strict conditions. Under the default tie rules, an uninformative market with entry at its floor can coexist with an informative market where a purchase recruits the high-cost rival. The threshold must exceed the prior but remain reachable, and the spread must lie within the coexistence bounds. B3 states those bounds.

### Why is each outcome unique? — B2

The bounds hold under every order plan, including mixtures. Against the weak incumbent, even the gain cap falls below the order cost. Against the strong incumbent, even the gain floor exceeds it, so both correct orders must occur. Their beliefs fix preparation. Against the very strong incumbent, full orders persist, but profit at the top belief cannot cover the high cost. These strict comparisons fix the equilibrium outcome under all four tie-rule pairs. Orders reveal a belief about synergy, not the synergy class itself.

### Does Proposition 4 give a unique equilibrium? — B12

It gives one research value for the equilibrium where good synergy buys after success and only a purchase recruits the high-cost rival. The spread must exceed twice the order cost, and the purchase belief must reach the threshold at that research value. The root is unique; the whole equilibrium set need not be. Other equilibria can coexist. Research rises strictly with the spread below the cap and stays fixed at the cap. A single interval of strengths needs the paper's extra continuity and recruitment-margin conditions.

### Does price access always help sellers? — B4

The strict gain uses Proposition two's conditions at the strong incumbent F one. Both economies have the same full informed orders. Access adds high-cost preparation after purchases, which raises expected proceeds and net acquisition surplus. Outside that region, the prior can already justify preparation. Price access can then deter preparation after sales and reduce expected proceeds. The paper does not claim a general proceeds gain.

### Why record no order? — B13

The order opportunity must exist before we observe its outcome. A purchase, a sale and no order all count as outcomes. No order supplies the reference price for both impacts. A sample of executed trades omits that reference and changes the order frequencies. Fix the opportunity in advance, date it before substantive diligence, and record no order as zero. Public offers measure a narrower outcome than preparation.
