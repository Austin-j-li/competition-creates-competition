\subsection{Competition creates competition}\label{app:prop2}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:ccc}]
Write \(F_1\) for a strengthening of \(F_0\) and \(F_2\) for a
strengthening of \(F_1\). For \(i\in\{0,1,2\}\), write \(B_i=B_{F_i}\), \(\Delta_i=\DT(F_i)\), and \(t_{0,i}\),
\(t_{j,i}\), \(g_{j,i}\), \(w_i(\mu)\) for the objects at \(F_i\). Also write \(M=M(\pi)\), \(m=m(\pi)\) and
\(\kappa=\kappa(\pi)\). Under the class ranking~\eqref{eq:classes} every value meets the reserve, so
\(W_H(F)=\E_H[F(\theta)]\), the probability that a prepared high-class challenger outbids the incumbent. Value ties
have probability zero because \(F\) is continuous.

The proof has seven steps. Steps 1, 3 and 5 state each economy's equilibrium tuple and derive its equilibrium
conditions. Bounds on every candidate give uniqueness.

\emph{Step 0: implied inequalities.} Proposition~\ref{prop:returns}(a) gives \(B_1(\pi)\le B_0(\pi)\), so (H1)
gives \(B_1(\pi)<c_H<B_1(M)\). Since \(M>\pi\), \(B_1(M)-B_1(\pi)=(M-\pi)(g_{H,1}-g_{L,1})>0\). Hence
\(g_{H,1}>g_{L,1}\), the threshold \(\tau_1\) at \(F_1\) is defined, and \(\pi<\tau_1<M\). By (H2),
\(\Delta_1>k/(\rho\kappa)>0\). Proposition~\ref{prop:returns}(b) gives \(\Delta_2\ge\Delta_1\), so
\(\rho\kappa\Delta_2>k\). Proposition~\ref{prop:returns} also gives \(g_{H,2}\ge g_{L,2}\), so
\(B_2(\mu)\le B_2(M)<c_H\) for every \(\mu\le M\). The zero-cost challenger prepares after every price, so
Lemma~\ref{lem:prices}(a) gives \(e\ge\rho\) in every candidate of each economy.

\emph{Step 1: the weak incumbent.} Fix any candidate at \(F_0\). By Lemma~\ref{lem:trading}(a) and the upper bounds
in \eqref{eq:a2-bounds}, each correctly signed order earns at most \(\Delta_0-k<0\), and each wrong-signed order
earns at most \(-k\). Abstention is therefore the unique maximizer of \(U_H\) and of \(U_L\), and (E5) gives
\(\sigma_H=\sigma_L=\delta_0\) under both investor rules. Then \(f_H(x)=f_L(x)=(1-\lambda)\eta(x)+\lambda\1\{x=0\}\),
so (E1) gives \(\mu(x)=\pi\) at every order, and (E2) gives \(\bar\mu(\bar P)=\pi\) at every price. Since
\(B_0(\pi)<c_H\), (E3) gives \(\zeta(\bar P)=0\) with no tie, so \(e(x)=\rho\). Condition (E4) and
\eqref{eq:price} then fix one price for every order. Every equilibrium at \(F_0\) is therefore the tuple
\[
\mathcal E_{\rm N}(F_0):\quad
\begin{aligned}
&\sigma_H=\sigma_L=\delta_0,\quad \mu(x)=\pi,\quad P(x)=\bar P_0:=t_{0,0}+\rho\,[t_{L,0}-t_{0,0}+\Delta_0\pi],\\
&\bar\mu(\bar P_0)=\pi,\quad \zeta(\bar P_0)=0,
\end{aligned}
\]
with truthful bids.

\emph{Equilibrium conditions for \(\mathcal E_{\rm N}(F_0)\).} (E1): \(f_H=f_L\), so the posterior at every order is \(\pi\). (E2): the
single price pools all orders, and the odds of \(\bar\mu(\bar P_0)\) are
\(\pi\sum_xf_H(x)/[(1-\pi)\sum_xf_L(x)]=\pi/(1-\pi)\). (E3): \(B_0(\pi)<c_H\), strictly. (E4): by
\eqref{eq:a2-terminal} with \(e\equiv\rho\), \(\E[V_T\mid X=x]=t_{0,0}+\rho\,w_0(\pi)=\bar P_0\). (E5): at
\(\mathcal E_{\rm N}(F_0)\), \(K_H=\rho\Delta_0(1-\pi)<k\) and \(K_L=\rho\Delta_0\pi<k\), so \(U_H(1)<0=U_H(0)\) and
\(U_L(-1)<0=U_L(0)\), and the wrong-signed payoffs are at most \(-k\). (E6): Appendix~\ref{app:payoffs}. Entry is
\(\rho\), and high-class ownership is \(\pi\rho W_H(F_0)\).

\emph{Step 2: the strong incumbent, orders in every candidate.} Fix any candidate at \(F_1\). By
\eqref{eq:a2-bounds}, \(K_H\ge\rho\kappa\Delta_1>k\) and \(K_L\ge\rho\kappa\Delta_1>k\). By
Lemma~\ref{lem:trading}(a)--(b), the purchase is the unique maximizer of class \(H\), and the sale is the unique
maximizer of class \(L\). Every equilibrium at \(F_1\) has \(\sigma_H=\delta_1\) and \(\sigma_L=\delta_{-1}\), under
both investor rules. These bounds use only the nonzero orders, so they hold for every \(\beta<1/2\).

\emph{Step 3: the strong incumbent, the tuple.} Lemma~\ref{lem:beliefs}(d) gives the posteriors \((m,\pi,M)\) after
the orders \((-1,0,1)\). Step 0 gives \(\Delta_1>0\) and \(g_{H,1}>g_{L,1}\), so Lemma~\ref{lem:prices} applies.
Since \(B_1(M)>c_H\), \(B_1(\pi)<c_H\) and \(B_1(m)<B_1(\pi)\), Lemma~\ref{lem:prices}(d) gives \(e(1)=1\) and
\(e(0)=e(-1)=\rho\), with no tie. Every equilibrium at \(F_1\) is therefore the tuple
\[
\mathcal E_{\rm F}(F_1):\quad
\begin{aligned}
&\sigma_H=\delta_1,\quad \sigma_L=\delta_{-1},\quad (\mu(-1),\mu(0),\mu(1))=(m,\pi,M),\\
&P(x)=t_{0,1}+e(x)\,w_1(\mu(x)),\quad \bar\mu(P(x))=\mu(x),\quad \zeta(P(x))=\1\{x=1\},
\end{aligned}
\]
with \((e(-1),e(0),e(1))=(\rho,\rho,1)\) and truthful bids.

\emph{Equilibrium conditions for \(\mathcal E_{\rm F}(F_1)\).} (E1): the likelihoods \(f_H=(b,b_0,b+\lambda)\) and
\(f_L=(b+\lambda,b_0,b)\) give \((m,\pi,M)\). (E2): the stated entry is nondecreasing in the posterior and lies in
\([\rho,1]\), and \(\Delta_1>0\). By the paragraph on constructed tuples in Appendix~\ref{app:beliefs}, the three
prices are strictly increasing in the posterior, so \(P(-1)<P(0)<P(1)\). Each price has one order in its
preimage, so \(\bar\mu(P(x))=\mu(x)\). (E3): \(B_1(M)>c_H\), \(B_1(\pi)<c_H\) and \(B_1(m)<c_H\), all strict.
(E4): \eqref{eq:a2-terminal} with the stated entry. (E5): at \(\mathcal E_{\rm F}(F_1)\), \(K_H=\Delta_1(1-M)\ge\rho\kappa\Delta_1>k\)
and \(K_L=\rho\Delta_1m\ge\rho\kappa\Delta_1>k\). Hence \(U_H(1)>0=U_H(0)>U_H(-1)\) and
\(U_L(-1)>0=U_L(0)>U_L(1)\). (E6): Appendix~\ref{app:payoffs}.

\emph{Step 4: entry, ownership and the takeover share at \(F_1\).} Class \(H\) places the order \(1\) with
probability \(f_H(1)=b+\lambda\), through a liquidity purchase or an informed purchase. Class \(L\) places it with
probability \(f_L(1)=b\). Only the order \(1\) recruits the high-cost challenger. Hence
\[
e_H=\rho+(1-\rho)(b+\lambda),\qquad e_L=\rho+(1-\rho)b,\qquad
\Ent_1=\pi e_H+(1-\pi)e_L=\rho+(1-\rho)(b+\pi\lambda)>\rho ,
\]
since \(b>0\); also \(e_H-e_L=(1-\rho)\lambda>0\). Preparation depends on the order and the cost, and both are
independent of \((\theta,R)\) given the class. A prepared challenger of class \(j\) acquires the target when
\(\theta>R\), with probability \(W_j(F_1)\). Write \(\Own_{j,i}\) for the probability that the challenger is of class
\(j\) and acquires the target at \(F_i\), with \(\pi_H=\pi\) and \(\pi_L=1-\pi\). Then
\(\Own_{j,1}=\pi_je_jW_j(F_1)\), and Step~1 gives \(\Own_{j,0}=\pi_j\rho\,W_j(F_0)\). Division by \(\pi>0\) shows
that high-class ownership rises exactly when \([\rho+(1-\rho)(b+\lambda)]\,W_H(F_1)>\rho\,W_H(F_0)\).

The challenger acquires the target with positive probability against both incumbents. By (H1),
\(B_1(M)>c_H>0\), so \(g_{j,1}>0\) for some class \(j\); a class whose gross profit is positive beats the incumbent
with positive probability, so \(W_j(F_1)>0\). Since \(F_1\) strengthens \(F_0\), \(F_1\le F_0\) pointwise, and
\(W_j(F_0)\ge W_j(F_1)\) for both classes. Class \(H\)'s takeover share at \(F_i\) is
\(\Own_{H,i}/(\Own_{H,i}+\Own_{L,i})\). For \(x,y,x',y'\ge0\) with \(x+y>0\) and \(x'+y'>0\), the inequality
\(x/(x+y)>x'/(x'+y')\) holds exactly when \(xy'>x'y\). With \(x=\Own_{H,1}\), \(y=\Own_{L,1}\), \(x'=\Own_{H,0}\) and
\(y'=\Own_{L,0}\), and after division by \(\pi(1-\pi)\rho>0\), the share rises from \(F_0\) to \(F_1\) exactly when
\[
e_H\,W_H(F_1)\,W_L(F_0)>e_L\,W_H(F_0)\,W_L(F_1).
\]

Let class \(H\) outbid \(F_1\). Then \(1\ge W_H(F_0)\ge W_H(F_1)=1\), so class \(H\) outbids \(F_0\), and
\(\Own_{H,1}=\pi e_H>\pi\rho=\Own_{H,0}\). Condition~\eqref{eq:a3-share} reads \(e_H\,W_L(F_0)>e_L\,W_L(F_1)\). If
\(W_L(F_0)>0\), then \(e_H\,W_L(F_0)>e_L\,W_L(F_0)\ge e_L\,W_L(F_1)\), and the share rises strictly. If
\(W_L(F_0)=0\), then \(W_L(F_1)=0\), so \(\Own_{L,0}=\Own_{L,1}=0\), and the share equals one against both
incumbents.

\emph{Step 5: the very strong incumbent.} Fix any candidate at \(F_2\). By \eqref{eq:a2-bounds},
\(K_H\ge\rho\kappa\Delta_2>k\) and \(K_L\ge\rho\kappa\Delta_2>k\), so every equilibrium has full orders, and
Lemma~\ref{lem:beliefs}(d) gives the posteriors \((m,\pi,M)\). By Step 0, \(B_2(\mu)\le B_2(M)<c_H\) at each
posterior, and every price belief is one of these posteriors or an average of them. So (E3) gives
\(\zeta\equiv0\) with no tie, and \(e\equiv\rho\). Every equilibrium at \(F_2\) is therefore the tuple
\[
\mathcal E_{\rm F}(F_2):\quad
\begin{aligned}
&\sigma_H=\delta_1,\quad \sigma_L=\delta_{-1},\quad (\mu(-1),\mu(0),\mu(1))=(m,\pi,M),\\
&P(x)=t_{0,2}+\rho\,w_2(\mu(x)),\quad \bar\mu(P(x))=\mu(x),\quad \zeta\equiv0,
\end{aligned}
\]
with truthful bids. \emph{Equilibrium conditions.} (E1): the likelihoods \(f_H=(b,b_0,b+\lambda)\) and \(f_L=(b+\lambda,b_0,b)\)
give \((m,\pi,M)\). (E2): since \(\Delta_2>0\) and \(m<\pi<M\), the three
prices \(t_{0,2}+\rho\,w_2(\mu(x))\) differ, so each price has one order in its preimage. (E3): \(B_2(\mu(x))<c_H\)
at each posterior. (E4): \eqref{eq:a2-terminal} with \(e\equiv\rho\). (E5): at \(\mathcal E_{\rm F}(F_2)\),
\(K_H=\rho\Delta_2(1-M)>k\) and \(K_L=\rho\Delta_2m>k\). (E6): Appendix~\ref{app:payoffs}. The prices differ, so the
price stays informative while entry equals \(\rho\). Entry is \(\rho\) after every order, so, as in Step 4,
high-class ownership is \(\pi\rho W_H(F_2)\).

\emph{Step 6: tie rules.} Every investor comparison in Steps 1, 2 and 5 is strict. Every high-cost comparison at an
equilibrium belief is strict. No tie rule is used, so the four pairs of rules give the same unique outcome in each
economy.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Decisive advantage]\label{lem:decisive}
Fix ranked finite-mean classes, a reserve \(p>0\), a high cost \(c_H>0\), and a family of continuous incumbent
laws. The family has \emph{decisive advantage} if, for every law \(F\) in it,
\[
g_H(F)>c_H\quad\Longrightarrow\quad W_H(F)=1.
\]
At every law in this family with \(\DT>0\) and \(g_H>g_L\), any equilibrium that recruits a high-cost challenger
after some order has \(W_H(F)=1\). This holds at every interior prior, under all four tie rules, both at fixed
presence and with research at a cap below one. Under (H1)--(H3), decisive advantage at \(F_1\) gives the
ownership and takeover-share conclusions under outbidding in Proposition~\ref{prop:ccc}(ii).

For point classes \(h>\ell\ge p\) and the full family \(F_r=U[0,r]\), \(r>0\), decisive advantage holds exactly
when
\[
c_H\ge\frac{h^2-p^2}{2h}.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Full liquidity support and an interior prior give every price an interior posterior, at fixed presence and
also when research is bounded by a cap below one. If a high-cost challenger prepares after an order, then
\[
c_H\le B_F(\mu)=g_L(F)+\mu[g_H(F)-g_L(F)]<g_H(F),
\]
where the last inequality uses \(\mu<1\) and the positive profit gap. Decisive advantage therefore gives
\(W_H(F)=1\). Applied at \(F_1\), this is the outbidding hypothesis of Proposition~\ref{prop:ccc}(ii).

For a point value \(h\) and \(r>h\), direct integration gives
\[
W_H(U[0,r])=\frac hr<1,\qquad g_H(U[0,r])=\frac{h^2-p^2}{2r}.
\]
The latter decreases strictly in \(r\), with supremum \((h^2-p^2)/(2h)\) as \(r\downarrow h\).
If \(c_H\) is at least this supremum, \(g_H>c_H\) forces \(r\le h\), where \(W_H=1\).
If \(c_H\) is below it, continuity gives some \(r>h\) with \(g_H>c_H\) and \(W_H<1\).
These two implications prove the equivalence for the full family.
\end{proof}


\paragraph{Each hypothesis is used once.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
\item \(B_0(\pi)<c_H\) gives entry \(\rho\) at \(F_0\). Through Proposition~\ref{prop:returns}(a), it also stops
recruitment after the zero order at \(F_1\).
\item \(c_H<B_1(M)\) gives recruitment after a purchase at \(F_1\).
\item \(\Delta_0<k\) defeats every informed order at \(F_0\), against every candidate.
\item \(k<\rho\kappa\Delta_1\) forces full orders at \(F_1\), and through Proposition~\ref{prop:returns}(b) at \(F_2\),
against every candidate.
\item \(B_2(M)<c_H\) stops recruitment at \(F_2\).
\end{itemize}
The nonzero-order bounds hold for every \(0<\beta<1/2\).

\paragraph{The open set.}
For uniform incumbents, (H1) to (H3) are strict inequalities between continuous functions of
\((r_0,r_1,r_2,c_H,k,\rho,\pi,\lambda,\beta)\). They define an open set in the domain \(0<\beta<1/2\).
The sufficient condition
\((1+\rho)W_H(F_1)\ge2\rho W_H(F_0)>0\) for higher ownership implies the exact condition of Step 4 whenever
\(b+\lambda>1/2\), because then \(e_H>(1+\rho)/2\).

\fixedlabel{prop:region}{1}\fixedlabel{cor:ccc-example}{1}
Let the classes be point masses at \(h=\reg{prim_h}\) and \(\ell=\reg{prim_ell}\), the incumbent be uniform on
\([0,r]\), and
\[
(p,\rho,c_H,k)=(\reg{prim_p},\reg{prim_rho},\reg{prim_cH},\reg{prim_k}),\qquad
(r_0,r_1,r_2)=(\reg{prim_r0},\reg{prim_r1},\reg{prim_r2}).

Take a slot with \(0<\lambda<1\), \(0<\beta<1/2\), \(u=\lambda/[(1-\lambda)\beta]\).
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=2em]
\item At prior \(\pi=\reg{prim_pi}\), the high-cost window, trading-cost window and exclusion condition hold

\[
\begin{gathered}
\reg{u_lo_frac}<u<\reg{u_hi_frac},\\
\text{equivalently}\quad
\tau(r_1)=\reg{M_lo_frac}<M(\pi)<\reg{M_hi_frac}=\tau(r_2).

\item For \(u<\reg{u_lo_frac}\), entry at \(r_1\) is \(\rho\). At \(u=\reg{u_lo_frac}\), orders are uniquely zero

with endpoints \(\reg{prior_lo_B}\) and \(\reg{prior_hi_B}\) to six places. The high-cost and trading-cost windows

The example's cost \(c_H=\reg{prim_cH}\) lies in this window, with margins

whatever the slot, prior, \(\rho\), \(k\) and research.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{prop:region}]
Take the example's primitives \((h,\ell,p)=(\reg{prim_h},\reg{prim_ell},\reg{prim_p})\),
\((\rho,c_H,k)=(\reg{prim_rho},\reg{prim_cH},\reg{prim_k})\) and the strengths
\((r_0,r_1,r_2)=(\reg{prim_r0},\reg{prim_r1},\reg{prim_r2})\). For \(\ell\le r\le h\),
\(\tau(r)=[2r c_H-(\ell^2-p^2)]/(2rh-r^2-\ell^2)\), with
\(\tau(r_0)=\reg{tau_r0_frac}\), \(\tau(r_1)=\reg{tau_r1_frac}\) and \(\tau(r_2)=\reg{tau_r2_frac}\). Also
\(\DT(r_0)=\reg{margin_Delta_pos_B_frac}\), \(\DT(r_1)=\reg{a236_Delta_r1_frac}\) and \(\DT(r_2)=\reg{a236_Delta_r2_frac}\) by \eqref{eq:payoffs}.

\emph{Part (a).} At the prior \(\pi=\reg{prim_pi}\), \(M=(1+u)/(2+u)\) and \(\kappa=m=1-M=1/(2+u)\). The left side of (H1) has the
margin \(c_H-B_{r_0}(1/2)=\reg{margin_weakcost_B}\), and the left side of (H2) has the margin
\(k-\DT(r_0)=\reg{margin_kweak_B}\). Neither depends on the slot. Since
\[
M>\tau\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad u>\frac{2\tau-1}{1-\tau},
\]
the right side of (H1) is \(u>\reg{u_lo_frac}\), and (H3) is \(u<\reg{u_hi_frac}\). The right side of (H2) is
\[
\rho(1-M)\DT(r_1)>k\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad u<\frac{\rho\DT(r_1)}{k}-2=\reg{u_kstrong_frac}.
\]
The bound \(u<\reg{u_hi_frac}\) implies it; at
the example's slot \(\reg{u_kstrong_frac}-u=\reg{margin_u_kstrong_B}\).
Hence (H1) to (H3) hold if and only if \(\reg{u_lo_frac}<u<\reg{u_hi_frac}\), that is \(\tau(r_1)<M<\tau(r_2)\). At
fixed \(\beta\), \(\lambda=u\beta/(1+u\beta)\) increases strictly in \(u\), so the band is an open interval of
\(\lambda\). Proposition~\ref{prop:ccc}(i)--(iii) then give the entry values under all four tie rules, and since
\(h>r_2\), \(W_H=1\) at the three strengths, which gives the ownership values.

\emph{Part (b).} For \(u<\reg{u_kstrong_frac}\), Step 2 of the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:ccc} holds at \(r_1\),
and at \(r_2\) because \(\DT(r_2)>\DT(r_1)\). Orders are full at both strengths, and the posteriors are
\((m,1/2,M)\). If \(u<\reg{u_lo_frac}\), then \(M<\tau(r_1)\), no order recruits at \(r_1\), and entry at \(r_1\) is
\(\rho\). If \(\reg{u_hi_frac}\le u<\reg{u_kstrong_frac}\), then \(M\ge\tau(r_2)\), and a purchase recruits at \(r_2\)
when an indifferent high-cost challenger prepares. For \(u\ge\reg{u_kstrong_frac}\), the right side of (H2) fails at \(r_1\).

At \(u=\reg{u_lo_frac}\), the left side of (H1), both sides of (H2) and (H3) still hold strictly. At \(r_0\), every
correct order earns at most \(\DT(r_0)-k=-\reg{margin_kweak_B}<0\), so orders are uniquely zero. At \(r_1\) and \(r_2\), orders are
uniquely full by Step 2, and parts (i) and (iii) hold. A purchase at \(r_1\) leaves the high-cost challenger indifferent, and
an indifferent high-cost challenger prepares and gives entry \(\Ent_1\). When an indifferent high-cost challenger may prepare with any probability, entry after a purchase ranges over \([\rho,1]\), so entry at \(r_1\)
can take any value in \([\rho,\Ent_1]\).

\emph{Part (c).} Fix \(1+u=\eu\) and the example's other inputs, and vary \(\pi\). The inequality \(M(\pi)>\tau\) is
\(\pi/(1-\pi)>\tau/[(1-\tau)(1+u)]\), that is \(\pi>\tau/[\tau+(1-\tau)\eu]\). The right side of (H1) gives the
lower endpoint of the corollary, and (H3) gives its upper endpoint. The left side of (H1) is
\(B_{r_0}(\pi)<c_H\), that is \(\pi<\tau(r_0)=\reg{tau_r0_frac}\). The left side of (H2) does not depend on \(\pi\).
For the right side of (H2), let \(\kappa_0=k/[\rho\DT(r_1)]=\reg{a236_kappa_floor_frac}\). Then
\[
m(\pi)>\kappa_0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\pi>\frac{\kappa_0\eu}{1-\kappa_0+\kappa_0\eu}.
\]
This gives the lower endpoint, and \(1-M(\pi)>\kappa_0\) gives the upper endpoint, so
\(\pi\in(\reg{prior_kappa_lo_B},\reg{prior_kappa_hi_B})\)
approximately. This interval contains the other two. The intersection is the interval of the corollary, with
endpoints \(\approx\reg{prior_lo_B}\) and \(\approx\reg{prior_hi_B}\). Without (H3), the upper endpoint is
\(\tau(r_0)\approx\reg{prior_ii_hi}\).

\emph{Part (d).} At the stated slot,
\(b=\beta/[1+(\eu-1)\beta]\) and \(\lambda=(\eu-1)b\). Substitution in part (a) gives the entry and ownership
values. Since \(h>r_2\), \(W_H=1\) at every stated strength and \(W_L=\ell/r\). Thus the takeover share is
\[
\frac{\pi e_H}{\pi e_H+(1-\pi)e_L\ell/r}.
\]
At \(r_0\) and \(r_2\), both class entries equal \(\rho\); at \(r_1\), they are the two expressions in
Proposition~\ref{prop:ccc}(ii). These substitutions give the three displayed shares.

\emph{Part (e).} Lemma~\ref{lem:decisive} makes decisive advantage equivalent to
\(c_H\ge(h^2-p^2)/(2h)=\reg{cH_DA_floor_B}\); affordability is
\(c_H\le h-r_2=\reg{cH_afford_ceiling_B}\). Their intersection is the displayed cost window.
The distances from the stated cost to the two endpoints give the displayed margins. On \(p\le r\le h\),
\(g_H(r)=h-r/2-p^2/(2r)\) decreases strictly for \(r>p\).
Solving \(g_H(r)=c_H\) gives the upper root in the statement; the lower algebraic root lies below \(p\).
For \(r\le p\), gross profit is \(h-p>c_H\), and for \(r\ge h\), it is at most
\((h^2-p^2)/(2h)<c_H\). Hence this upper root is the unique actual crossing.
Recruitment implies \(g_H>c_H\) by Lemma~\ref{lem:decisive}'s first argument, which gives the strict bound on \(r\).
\end{proof}
