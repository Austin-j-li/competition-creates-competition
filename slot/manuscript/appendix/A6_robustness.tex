\subsection{Robustness}\label{app:robustness}

This subsection states and proves Proposition~\ref{prop:control} and the results that Section~\ref{sec:robustness}
summarizes. Each proof uses the conventions of Appendix~\ref{app:beliefs}.

\subsubsection*{The fixed-information control}

\begin{proposition}[Fixed information]\label{prop:control}
Fix the joint law of the class \(j\) and a signal \(S\) that the challenger observes.
Fix the class value laws \(G_j\) and the cost law. In each economy \(i\), require
\(\mathcal L_i(\theta,R\mid j,S)=G_j\otimes F_i\). The cost is independent of the class, both values and \(S\). If
the incumbent strengthens from \(F_0\) to \(F_1\), entry does not rise under rule C1. Its decrease is exactly
\[
\Ent_0-\Ent_1=\Pr\bigl\{B_{F_1}(\mu(S))<C\le B_{F_0}(\mu(S))\bigr\},\qquad \mu(S)=\Pr(j=H\mid S),
\]
and the decrease is strict exactly when this probability is positive.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:control}]
The cost is independent of the class, the values and the signal \(S\), so \(\mu(S)=\Pr(j=H\mid S)\) is the
challenger's complete class belief at its decision. The condition
\(\mathcal L_i(\theta,R\mid j,S)=G_j\otimes F_i\) gives gross profit \(B_{F_i}(\mu(S))\) in economy \(i\).
Under rule C1, the challenger prepares exactly when
\(C\le B_{F_i}(\mu(S))\). Proposition~\ref{prop:returns}(a) gives \(B_{F_1}(\mu)\le B_{F_0}(\mu)\) at every belief.
Hence, pointwise,
\[
\1\{C\le B_{F_0}(\mu(S))\}-\1\{C\le B_{F_1}(\mu(S))\}=\1\{B_{F_1}(\mu(S))<C\le B_{F_0}(\mu(S))\}\ge0 .
\]
The joint law of \((S,C)\) is the same in both economies. Take expectations: \(\Ent_0-\Ent_1\) equals the
probability in the proposition, which is nonnegative, and it is positive exactly when the decrease is strict. A
tie probability \(q\) that is the same in both economies replaces each indicator by
\(\1\{C<B_{F_i}\}+q\,\1\{C=B_{F_i}\}\). With \(B_{F_1}\le B_{F_0}\), the difference of the two expressions is \(0\) for
\(C<B_{F_1}\) and for \(C>B_{F_0}\), \(1-q\) at \(C=B_{F_1}<B_{F_0}\), \(1\) strictly between, \(q\) at \(C=B_{F_0}>B_{F_1}\),
and \(0\) when the two profits are equal; it is nonnegative, so entry does not rise with such a tie probability
either.
\end{proof}

The experiment fixes \(\lambda\), \(\beta\), \(\pi\), the cost law and the full orders
\((\sigma_H,\sigma_L)=(\delta_1,\delta_{-1})\). Lemma~\ref{lem:beliefs}(d) gives the posteriors \((m,\pi,M)\). Let
\(e_r(\mu)=\rho+(1-\rho)\1\{B_r(\mu)\ge c_H\}\) and \(P(x)=t_0+e_r(\mu(x))\,w(\mu(x))\). This entry is nondecreasing
in the posterior, so by the paragraph on constructed tuples in Appendix~\ref{app:beliefs} the three prices differ,

depend on \(r\). Given the class and order, the value law is \(G_j\otimes F_r\), as Proposition~\ref{prop:control} requires.
At the benchmark, \(\tau(r)=(48r-3)/[4(20r-r^2-1)]\) rises and \(\tau(1)=5/8>1/2=\pi\), so only the

\subsubsection*{Preparation cost laws}

The two-point cost law of Section~\ref{sec:values} is not needed. Let the preparation cost \(C\) be independent of
the class, the value, the incumbent's value and the slot, with the law
\[
H_C=\rho H^0+(1-\rho)H^1,\qquad \operatorname{supp}H^0\subset[0,L],\qquad \operatorname{supp}H^1\subset[c_-,c_+].
\]
Either component may be atomless or have atoms. A challenger prepares when gross profit at its belief exceeds its
cost, and it stays out when gross profit falls short. The low component plays the role of the zero cost, and the high
component plays the role of \(c_H\).

With a general cost law, the preparation rule assigns \(\zeta(\bar P,C)\in[0,1]\) to each price and cost, and (E3)
reads: \(\zeta(\bar P,C)=1\) if \(B(\bar\mu(\bar P))>C\), and \(\zeta(\bar P,C)=0\) if \(B(\bar\mu(\bar P))<C\). Entry
after the order \(x\) is \(e(x)=\int\zeta(P(x),C)\,dH_C(C)\). Every step of Appendix~\ref{app:beliefs} that does not
use the two-point law carries over; in particular, Lemma~\ref{lem:trading} holds for any entry in \([\rho,1]\).

\begin{proposition}[Preparation cost laws]\label{prop:atomless}
Let the classes satisfy the class ranking~\eqref{eq:classes}, and let \(F_1\) strengthen \(F_0\). Suppose
\[
0<L<B_1(m),\qquad B_0(\pi)<c_-<c_+<B_1(M),\qquad \Delta_0<k<\rho\kappa\Delta_1.
\]
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=2em]
\item At \(F_0\), the unique orders are zero, and entry is \(\rho\).
\item At \(F_1\), the unique orders are full, and only a purchase recruits the high component. Entry is
\(\rho+(1-\rho)(b+\pi\lambda)\). High-class ownership rises if and only if
\([\rho+(1-\rho)(b+\lambda)]\,W_H(F_1)>\rho\,W_H(F_0)\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:atomless}]
\emph{Step 0: consequences of the hypotheses.} The class ranking~\eqref{eq:classes} gives \(\Delta_i\ge0\) and
\(w_L\ge0\). Since \(k>0\), \(k<\rho\kappa\Delta_1\) gives \(\Delta_1>0\). The support gaps and
Proposition~\ref{prop:returns}(a) give \(B_1(M)>c_+>c_->B_0(\pi)\ge B_1(\pi)\). Since \(M>\pi\),
\(B_1(M)-B_1(\pi)=(M-\pi)(g_{H,1}-g_{L,1})>0\), so \(g_{H,1}>g_{L,1}\). The proof needs no condition \(\Delta_0>0\).

\emph{Step 1: the floor under every profile.} Fix any candidate in either economy. By Lemma~\ref{lem:beliefs}, every
price belief lies in \([m,M]\); this step uses \(\beta\le1/3\) for a price that pools the zero order. Hence
\(B_{F_i}(\bar\mu)\ge B_{F_i}(m)\ge B_1(m)>L\), so every cost in the support of \(H^0\) prepares strictly, at every
price. Entry is therefore at least \(\rho\) after every order.

\emph{Step 2: orders.} At \(F_0\), the upper bound in \eqref{eq:a2-bounds} gives correct gross returns at most
\(\Delta_0<k\), and wrong-signed orders earn at most \(-k\); if \(\Delta_0=0\), every nonzero order earns \(-k\). So
both classes abstain in every equilibrium. At \(F_1\), Step 1 and \eqref{eq:a2-bounds} give correct gross returns
at least \(\rho\kappa\Delta_1>k\), so full orders are forced in every equilibrium. These bounds hold under both
investor rules.

\emph{Step 3: the tuples.} At \(F_0\): zero orders, \(\mu\equiv\pi\), \(\bar\mu\equiv\pi\), the single price
\(t_{0,0}+\rho\,w_0(\pi)\), and \(\zeta(\bar P,C)=\1\{C\le B_0(\pi)\}\). At \(F_1\): full orders, posteriors
\((m,\pi,M)\), \(\zeta(P(x),C)=\1\{C\le B_1(\mu(x))\}\), entry \((\rho,\rho,1)\), prices
\(t_{0,1}+e(x)\,w_1(\mu(x))\) and \(\bar\mu(P(x))=\mu(x)\); truthful bids in both. Verification: (E1) and (E2) hold as
in Steps 1 and 3 of the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:ccc}; at \(F_1\), the stated entry is nondecreasing in the
posterior and \(\Delta_1>0\), so the prices differ. (E3): at \(F_0\), \(B_0(\pi)<c_-\) excludes the whole high
component, and \(B_0(\pi)>L\) includes the whole low component. At \(F_1\), \(B_1(M)>c_+\) includes the whole high
component, and \(B_1(m)<B_1(\pi)\le B_0(\pi)<c_-\) excludes it at the other two posteriors. Every realized
comparison is strict, so rules C1 and CM agree. (E4): \eqref{eq:a2-terminal}. (E5): at \(F_0\), the correct gross
returns are \(\rho\Delta_0(1-\pi)\) and \(\rho\Delta_0\pi\), both below \(k\); at \(F_1\), the purchase earns
\(\Delta_1(1-M)-k>0\) and the sale \(\rho\Delta_1m-k>0\). (E6): truthful bids.

\emph{Step 4: outcomes.} A purchase has probability \(b+\lambda\) in class \(H\) and \(b\) in class \(L\), and only
a purchase admits the high component. So \(\Ent_1=\rho+(1-\rho)(b+\pi\lambda)\), class entry is
\(\bar e_H=\rho+(1-\rho)(b+\lambda)\), and ownership is \(\pi\bar e_HW_H(F_1)\) against \(\pi\rho W_H(F_0)\), as in
Step 4 of the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:ccc}.
\end{proof}

No step uses atomlessness or a density. The two-point law of the benchmark is the case \(H^0=\delta_0\),
\(H^1=\delta_{c_H}\).

\paragraph{The third economy and the benchmark band.}
For \(F_2\), the conditions \(L<B_2(m)\), \(B_2(M)<c_-\) and \(k<\rho\kappa\Delta_2\) give full orders by the same
bound, include the low component everywhere, and exclude the high component at every posterior, so entry is
\(\rho\). Suppose instead that the low component prepares everywhere, that \(B_r(\pi)<c_-\), and that the orders are
full. The high component then prepares only after a purchase, with probability \(H^1(B_r(M))\), so
\[
\Ent(r)=\rho+(1-\rho)(b+\pi\lambda)\,H^1\bigl(B_r(M)\bigr).
\]
This is the only place where atomlessness enters: for atomless \(H^1\), \(H^1\) is continuous, and \(B_r(M)\) falls
continuously in \(r\), so entry is continuous and nonincreasing in strength. In the benchmark, take
\(L=\reg{atom_L}\) and \(H^1\) uniform on \([c_H-\varepsilon,c_H+\varepsilon]\) with \(\varepsilon=\reg{atom_eps}\).
The margins are \(B_1(m)-L=\reg{margin_p4_atomless_floor_B}\), \(c_--B_0(\pi)=\reg{margin_p4_atomless_weak_B}\) and
\(B_1(M)-c_+=\reg{margin_p4_atomless_strong_B}\). Entry at \(r_1\) is \(\reg{p4_atomless_r1_entry}\) and ownership
is \(\reg{p4_atomless_r1_own}\). At \(r_2\), \(c_--B_2(M)=\reg{margin_p4_atomless_return_B}\): the top profit lies
inside the band, at distances \(\reg{margin_p4_atomless_top_lower_r2_B}\) and \(\reg{margin_p4_atomless_top_upper_r2_B}\)
from its ends, and the formula gives entry \(\reg{p4_atomless_r2_entry}\) and ownership \(\reg{p4_atomless_r2_own}\) by
direct evaluation of the uniform law. The return to \(\rho\) at \(r_2\) holds for every half-width below
\(c_H-B_2(M)=\reg{p4_atomless_max_width_floor_r2}\); at \(\varepsilon=\reg{atom_eps_small}\) its margin is
\(\reg{margin_p4_atomless_small_width_return}\).

\subsubsection*{Value classes and priors}

\begin{proposition}[Value classes and priors]\label{prop:classes}
Fix classes that satisfy the class ranking~\eqref{eq:classes} with \(\E_H[\theta]>\E_L[\theta]\), a reserve \(p>0\), and
any \(\pi,\rho\in(0,1)\) and slot \((\lambda,\beta)\). For uniform incumbents, a nonempty open set of strengths and
costs \((r_0,r_1,r_2,c_H,k)\) satisfies (H1)--(H3) and the ownership condition of Proposition~\ref{prop:ccc}. On this
set every conclusion of Proposition~\ref{prop:ccc} holds.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:classes}]
Let \(\Gamma(x)=\int_p^xD(y)\,dy\), and write \(\bar\Gamma=\E_H[\theta]-\E_L[\theta]>0\) for the total mean gap. Under
the class ranking~\eqref{eq:classes}, \(D\ge0\), so \(\Gamma\) is continuous and nondecreasing with limit \(\bar\Gamma\).
Let \(v_*=\inf\{x\ge p:\Gamma(x)>0\}\); finite means and \(\bar\Gamma>0\) give \(p\le v_*<\infty\).

\emph{Step 1: the uniform incumbent.} For \(R\sim U[0,r]\), \eqref{eq:spread} and integration by parts give
\[
\DT(r)=\int_p^r\Bigl(1-\frac yr\Bigr)D(y)\,dy=\frac1r\int_p^r\Gamma(y)\,dy ,
\]
since \(\Gamma(p)=0\) and the boundary term at \(r\) vanishes. Since \(\Gamma\) is continuous,
\[
\DT'(r)=\frac{r\Gamma(r)-\int_p^r\Gamma(y)\,dy}{r^2}\ge\frac{p\,\Gamma(r)}{r^2}>0\qquad(r>v_*),
\]
because \(\int_p^r\Gamma\le(r-p)\Gamma(r)\). Also \(\DT(r)\to0\) as \(r\downarrow v_*\), since \(\Gamma=0\) on
\([p,v_*]\). The split identity of Section~\ref{sec:profits} gives
\(g_H(r)-g_L(r)=\bar\Gamma-\DT(r)\ge\bar\Gamma-(r-p)\bar\Gamma/r=p\bar\Gamma/r>0\), and at \(r=v_*\), \(g_H-g_L=\bar\Gamma\). Dominated
convergence, with the integrable bound \(1-G_j\), gives continuity of \(g_j(r)\) and \(W_H(r)\) in \(r\), and
\(g_j(r)\to0\) as \(r\to\infty\). Since \(\theta\ge p\), \(W_H(r)=\E_H[\min\{\theta/r,1\}]\ge p/r>0\).

\emph{Step 2: the construction.} Let \(e_H=\rho+(1-\rho)(b+\lambda)>\rho\). At \(v_*\),
\(B_{v_*}(M)-B_{v_*}(\pi)=(M-\pi)\bar\Gamma>0\). By continuity choose \(r_1>v_*\) close enough to \(v_*\) that
\(B_{r_1}(M)>B_{v_*}(\pi)\) and \(e_HW_H(r_1)>\rho W_H(v_*)\). Then choose \(r_0\in(v_*,r_1)\) close enough to
\(v_*\) that \(\DT(r_0)<\rho\kappa\DT(r_1)\). Since \(B_r(\pi)\) and \(W_H(r)\) fall in \(r\),
\(B_{r_0}(\pi)\le B_{v_*}(\pi)<B_{r_1}(M)\) and \(e_HW_H(r_1)>\rho W_H(v_*)\ge\rho W_H(r_0)\). Choose \(c_H\) in
\((B_{r_0}(\pi),B_{r_1}(M))\) and \(k\) in \((\DT(r_0),\rho\kappa\DT(r_1))\). Since \(g_j(r)\to0\), choose
\(r_2>r_1\) with \(B_{r_2}(M)<c_H\). These choices satisfy (H1) to (H3) and the ownership condition.

\emph{Step 3: openness and the conclusions.} Each condition is a strict inequality between continuous functions of
\((r_0,r_1,r_2,c_H,k)\), so a neighborhood of the constructed point satisfies all of them. On this set,
Proposition~\ref{prop:ccc} supplies the three tuples, checks every condition against them, and proves uniqueness. In
the point-mass case \(D=\1\{\ell\le y<h\}\), so \(v_*=\ell\).
\end{proof}

\subsubsection*{More than two classes}

Let the classes \(j=1,\dots,J\) have priors \(\pi_j>0\) and values above the reserve, and write \(t_{j,i}\) and
\(g_{j,i}\) for the class objects at \(F_i\). Every posterior vector \(\nu\) that an order can produce lies in the set
\[
\mathcal M_\pi=\Bigl\{\nu:\ \frac{\nu_j}{\pi_j}\le(1+u)\frac{\nu_l}{\pi_l}\ \text{for all } j,l\Bigr\}.
\]
The price reveals only the posterior mean of class proceeds, \(\nu\cdot t\). Partition the classes into a low group
\(\mathcal L\) and a high group \(\mathcal H\), with high-group prior mass \(\bar\pi\). Write \(\bar g_{\mathcal
H}\) and \(\bar g_{\mathcal L}\) for the prior-weighted group means of \(g_{j,1}\).

\begin{proposition}[Many classes under separation]\label{prop:manyclasses}
Let the incumbent strengthen from \(F_0\) to \(F_1\). Suppose
\[
\max_jt_{j,0}-\min_jt_{j,0}<k,\qquad \sum_j\pi_jg_{j,0}<c_H,
\]
\[
\min_{j\in\mathcal H}t_{j,1}-\max_{\nu\in\mathcal M_\pi}\nu\cdot t_1>\frac k\rho,\qquad
\min_{\nu\in\mathcal M_\pi}\nu\cdot t_1-\max_{j\in\mathcal L}t_{j,1}>\frac k\rho,
\]
and \(\bar g_{\mathcal H}>\bar g_{\mathcal L}\) with \(\bar g_{\mathcal L}+M(\bar\pi)(\bar g_{\mathcal H}-\bar
g_{\mathcal L})>c_H\). Then at \(F_0\) the unique orders are zero, and entry is \(\rho\). At \(F_1\) the unique orders
are a purchase in every high-group class and a sale in every low-group class, only a purchase recruits, and entry is
\(\rho+(1-\rho)(b+\bar\pi\lambda)>\rho\). These conclusions hold under either challenger rule.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:manyclasses}]
The tuple now lists every \(\sigma_j\), the posterior vector \(\mu(q)=(\mu_j(q))_j\) after every order, the posterior
vector at every price, the prices, \(\zeta\) and the bids. Each class reaches each order through a liquidity order,
so \(f_j(q)\in[(1-\lambda)\eta(q),\,(1-\lambda)\eta(q)+\lambda]\), and with \(\beta\le1/3\) every ratio \(f_j(q)/f_l(q)\)
lies in \([1/(1+u),1+u]\). Bayes' rule gives \(\mu_j(q)/\pi_j=f_j(q)/\sum_l\pi_lf_l(q)\), so
\(\mu_j(q)/\pi_j\le(1+u)\,\mu_l(q)/\pi_l\) for all \(j,l\), and every posterior vector lies in \(\mathcal M_\pi\). A price
belief is a mixture of order posteriors, and \(\mathcal M_\pi\) is convex, so it also lies in \(\mathcal M_\pi\).

\emph{Payoffs.} Competitive prices give \(P(q)=t_0+e(q)\,[\mu(q)\cdot t-t_0]\), and
\(\E[V_T\mid j,q]=t_0+e(q)(t_j-t_0)\) as in \eqref{eq:a2-terminal}. Hence, against the fixed price and entry,
\[
U_j(q)=q\,e(q)\,[t_j-\mu(q)\cdot t]-k|q| ,
\]
with \(e(q)\in[\rho,1]\) because the zero-cost challenger prepares after every price. This step needs no price
sufficiency.

\emph{The weak economy.} Every residual satisfies \(|t_{j,0}-\mu\cdot t_0|\le\max_lt_{l,0}-\min_lt_{l,0}<k\), so
every nonzero order loses, and zero is the unique order of every class. Then \(f_j=f_l\) for all \(j,l\), every
posterior vector equals the prior vector \(\pi\), and gross profit is \(\sum_j\pi_jg_{j,0}<c_H\), so entry is \(\rho\).
The tuple has zero orders, the prior posterior at every order and price, the single price
\(t_{0,0}+\rho\,(\pi\cdot t_0-t_{0,0})\), \(\zeta\equiv0\), and truthful bids. Each condition holds by these
computations.

\emph{The strong economy, orders.} Under every candidate, a high-group class \(j\) earns from a purchase at least
\(\rho\,[\min_{l\in\mathcal H}t_{l,1}-\max_{\nu\in\mathcal M_\pi}\nu\cdot t_1]-k>0\), and from a sale at most \(-k\)
minus a nonnegative term, since \(t_{j,1}-\mu\cdot t_1>0\). A low-group class earns from a sale at least
\(\rho\,[\min_{\nu\in\mathcal M_\pi}\nu\cdot t_1-\max_{l\in\mathcal L}t_{l,1}]-k>0\), and from a purchase at most
\(-k\). Zero pays zero. So every equilibrium has purchases in \(\mathcal H\) and sales in \(\mathcal L\).

\emph{The strong economy, the tuple.} With these orders, the group posterior \(z\) of \(\mathcal H\) follows the
two-class formulas with prior \(\bar\pi\): \(z=(m(\bar\pi),\bar\pi,M(\bar\pi))\) after \((-1,0,1)\). Within each
group every class has the same order law, so the class proportions stay at the prior proportions:
\(\mu_j(z)=z\pi_j/\bar\pi\) for \(j\in\mathcal H\) and \(\mu_j(z)=(1-z)\pi_j/(1-\bar\pi)\) for \(j\in\mathcal L\). Gross
profit at \(z\) is \(\bar g_{\mathcal L}+z(\bar g_{\mathcal H}-\bar g_{\mathcal L})\), increasing in \(z\). At
\(z=\bar\pi\) it is \(\sum_j\pi_jg_{j,1}\le\sum_j\pi_jg_{j,0}<c_H\) by Proposition~\ref{prop:returns}(a); at
\(z=M(\bar\pi)\) it exceeds \(c_H\) by hypothesis. So only a purchase recruits, and entry is \((\rho,\rho,1)\). The
prices are \(P=t_{0,1}+e(z)[\bar t_{\mathcal L}-t_{0,1}+(\bar t_{\mathcal H}-\bar t_{\mathcal L})z]\) with group means
\(\bar t\). Separation gives \(\bar t_{\mathcal H}>\max_{\nu}\nu\cdot t_1\ge\min_\nu\nu\cdot t_1>\bar t_{\mathcal L}\),
so with \(e\) nondecreasing in \(z\) and \(e\ge\rho\), the three prices differ, each price reveals \(z\) and the whole
vector, and \(\bar\mu(P)=\mu(z)\). Each condition then holds by direct computation: (E1) and (E2) by the formulas
above, (E3) by the strict profit comparisons, (E4) by the price formula, (E5) by the strict payoff bounds, (E6) by
truthful bids. A purchase has probability \(b+\bar\pi\lambda\), so entry is \(\rho+(1-\rho)(b+\bar\pi\lambda)>\rho\).
Every comparison is strict, so both challenger rules give this outcome.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection*{Complementary private signals}

A buyer may know its integration plans, while investors who follow the target know its customers or its product
market. Two conditionally independent binary signals represent the two sources. The investor observes
\(T\in\{+,-\}\) with accuracy \(a_T\), and the challenger observes \(Y\in\{+,-\}\) with accuracy \(d\):
\[
\Pr(T=+\mid H)=\Pr(T=-\mid L)=a_T,\qquad \Pr(Y=+\mid H)=\Pr(Y=-\mid L)=d,
\]
with \(1/2<a_T,d<1\).
The investor orders on its signal, and the challenger sees the price and its signal before it decides. Write
\(m=m(1/2)\), \(M=M(1/2)\), \(\mu_-=(1-a_T)+(2a_T-1)m\) and \(\mu_+=(1-a_T)+(2a_T-1)M\). A challenger with signal
\(+\) holds the belief \(\phi_+(\mu)=d\mu/[d\mu+(1-d)(1-\mu)]\). Write
\(D^+(r)=\DT(r)+(1-\rho)(2d-1)[t_H(r)-t_0(r)]\) for the largest price response to the public posterior.

Throughout, the values are the point masses \(h\) and \(\ell\), the prior is \(1/2\), and \(T\) and \(Y\) are
independent given the class and independent of the slot, the cost and \(R\). The investor's order distributions are
\(\sigma_+\) and \(\sigma_-\) after its two signals. Write \(f_t(x)=(1-\lambda)\eta(x)+\lambda\sigma_t(x)\) for the
probability of the order \(x\) given \(T=t\), and \(\nu(x)=f_+(x)/[f_+(x)+f_-(x)]\) for the posterior of \(T=+\); the
two signals have prior \(1/2\).

\paragraph{Beliefs, entry and prices.}
The order depends on the class only through \(T\), so \(\mu(x)=a_T\nu(x)+(1-a_T)(1-\nu(x))=(1-a_T)+(2a_T-1)\nu(x)\).
Lemma~\ref{lem:beliefs}(b)--(c), applied to \(T\) in place of the class, gives \(m\le\nu(x)\le M\), so
\(\mu_-\le\mu(x)\le\mu_+\); the same holds for every price belief. The signal \(Y\) is independent of the order given
the class, so a challenger with \(Y=y\) at the price \(\bar P\) holds the belief \(\phi_y(\bar\mu(\bar P))\), with
\(\phi_-(\mu)=(1-d)\mu/[(1-d)\mu+d(1-\mu)]<\phi_+(\mu)\). Let \(I_y(\mu)\in[0,1]\) be the high-cost preparation
probability after \(Y=y\) at the public belief \(\mu\). Since \(B\) rises in the belief, \(I_-(\mu)>0\) implies
\(B(\phi_+(\mu))>B(\phi_-(\mu))\ge c_H\), so \(I_+\ge I_-\) under both challenger rules. Class entry at \(\mu\) is
\[
e_H(\mu)=\rho+(1-\rho)[dI_++(1-d)I_-],\qquad e_L(\mu)=\rho+(1-\rho)[(1-d)I_++dI_-],
\]
so \(e_H\ge e_L\ge\rho\). Competitive prices give \(P(x)=t_0+e_L w_L+\mu(x)\,\Delta_P\), with
\(\Delta_P=e_Hw_H-e_Lw_L=e_L\DT+(e_H-e_L)w_H\). Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:a6-DP}
\rho\DT\le\Delta_P\le\DT+(1-\rho)(2d-1)w_H=D^+ .
\end{equation}
If \(P(x)=P(x')\), class entry at the two orders is the same, because entry depends on the price, the signal and the
cost; then \(\Delta_P\ge\rho\DT>0\) gives \(\mu(x)=\mu(x')\), and \(\bar\mu(P(x))=\mu(x)\). The price reveals the public
posterior under every profile.

\paragraph{Order payoffs.}
After \(T=t\), the class probabilities are \(a_T\) and \(1-a_T\) or the reverse, and entry given the class and the
order does not depend on \(T\). Subtract the price:
\[
U_+(1)=(2a_T-1)[1-\nu(1)]\,\Delta_P(\mu(1))-k,\qquad U_-(-1)=(2a_T-1)\,\nu(-1)\,\Delta_P(\mu(-1))-k,
\]
and the wrong-signed orders earn \(-(2a_T-1)[1-\nu(-1)]\Delta_P-k\) and \(-(2a_T-1)\nu(1)\Delta_P-k\), both at most \(-k\).
Zero pays zero. With wrong-signed orders excluded, \(\nu(1)\) depends only on the purchase rate after \(T=+\), and
\(\nu(-1)\) only on the sale rate after \(T=-\); the zero order can depend on both, but it pays zero. By
\eqref{eq:a6-DP} and the bounds on \(\nu\), every correct gross return lies in \([(2a_T-1)m\rho\DT,\,(2a_T-1)D^+]\)
under every profile.

\begin{proposition}[Complementary private signals]\label{prop:signals}
Let \(0<p<\ell<r_0<r_1<h\), \(0<\rho<1\), and \(1/2<a_T,d<1\), with any slot \((\lambda,\beta)\). Suppose
\[
B_{r_0}(d)<c_H<B_{r_1}\bigl(\phi_+(\mu_+)\bigr),\qquad
(2a_T-1)\,D^+(r_0)<k<(2a_T-1)\,\rho\,m\,\DT(r_1).
\]
At \(r_0\), the unique orders are zero after both signals, and entry is \(\rho\). At \(r_1\), the unique orders are a
purchase after \(T=+\) and a sale after \(T=-\), and entry strictly exceeds \(\rho\). These order conclusions hold
under every pair of tie rules. The hypotheses allow \(d>a_T\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:signals}]
\emph{The weak strength.} Under every candidate at \(r_0\), every correct gross return is at most
\((2a_T-1)D^+(r_0)<k\), so zero is the unique order after both signals, under both investor rules. Then
\(\nu\equiv\mu\equiv1/2\), the private beliefs are \(d\) and \(1-d\), and \(B_{r_0}(d)<c_H\) excludes the high cost
after either signal, so entry is \(\rho\). The tuple has zero orders, these beliefs, the price
\(t_0+\rho(w_H+w_L)/2\) at every order, \(\zeta(\bar P,y,c_H)=0\), and truthful bids. Against it, each correct order
earns \((2a_T-1)\rho\DT(r_0)/2-k\le(2a_T-1)D^+(r_0)-k<0\).

\emph{The strong strength.} Under every candidate at \(r_1\), every correct gross return is at least
\((2a_T-1)m\rho\DT(r_1)>k\), so the purchase after \(T=+\) and the sale after \(T=-\) are the unique best orders. Then
\(f_+=(b,b_0,b+\lambda)\) and \(f_-=(b+\lambda,b_0,b)\), so \(\nu=(m,1/2,M)\) and \(\mu=(\mu_-,1/2,\mu_+)\). Class entry
follows from \(I_y(\mu(x))\) at each order, and the prices from the formula above. The probabilities \(I_y(\mu)\) are
nondecreasing in \(\mu\), because \(\phi_y\) and \(B\) rise, so \(e_H\) and \(e_L\) are nondecreasing in \(\mu\). For
\(\mu_2>\mu_1\),
\[
P(\mu_2)-P(\mu_1)=(\mu_2-\mu_1)\Delta_P(\mu_1)+\mu_2[e_H(\mu_2)-e_H(\mu_1)]w_H+(1-\mu_2)[e_L(\mu_2)-e_L(\mu_1)]w_L>0,
\]
since the first term is positive by \eqref{eq:a6-DP} and both brackets are nonnegative. The three prices therefore
differ, each price has one order in its preimage, and \(\bar\mu(P(x))=\mu(x)\). At the purchase price, \(B_{r_1}(\phi_+(\mu_+))>c_H\), so a challenger with
\(Y=+\) prepares. The event \((X,Y)=(1,+)\) has positive probability in each class, so entry strictly exceeds
\(\rho\). Against the tuple, \(U_+(1)\) and \(U_-(-1)\) are at least \((2a_T-1)m\rho\DT(r_1)-k>0\). These order
conclusions use only bounds on every profile, so they hold under every pair of tie rules.

Keep the values, costs, strengths and accuracies of \(S(B)\). Set \((\lambda,\beta)=(1/3,3/10)\).
Then \((a_T,d)=(7/10,3/4)\), \(M=8/11\) and \(m=3/11\). The five margins are
\[
\begin{aligned}
c_H-B_{r_0}(d)&=\frac{21}{400}>0,\\
B_{r_1}(\phi_+(\mu_+))-c_H&=\frac{1363}{36800}>0,\\
k-(2a_T-1)D^+(r_0)&=\frac{2}{1375}>0,\\
(2a_T-1)\rho m\DT(r_1)-k&=\frac{603}{31625}>0,\\
c_H-B_{r_1}(\phi_-(\mu_+))&=\frac{76753}{18400}>0.
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus the hypotheses allow \(d>a_T\).
\end{proof}

At \(r_0\), the investor mixes between trade and abstention after either signal, and it trades with probability
\(\reg{p4_b_signal_r0_buy_rate}\). At \(r_1\), it buys with probability one after a favorable signal, and after an
unfavorable signal it sells with probability \(\reg{p4_b_signal_r1_sell_rate}\).
Each mixed tuple is unique under rule IM with either challenger rule, by the strict monotonicity of each return and
the endpoint signs. Under rule I0 neither strength has an equilibrium, because the unique admissible rates are
interior. Without the challenger's signal, entry is \(\rho\) under every profile at both strengths, zero orders are
forced at \(r_0\), and at \(r_1\) the unique rates are \(s=t=4b/3\); this control also needs rule IM, since the gross
return at zero, \(\reg{p4_b_no_y_r1_zero_gross}\), exceeds \(k\) and the return at full orders,
\(\reg{p4_b_no_y_r1_full_gross}\), falls short of it. The rational constants above are exact; the script
\nolinkurl{P4_information/derive.py} prints them with every tuple, and
\nolinkurl{XP4/xp4_check.py} enumerates every rate on each order side and finds
no other equilibrium.
