\begin{frame}[t,label=S1]{Does a stronger incumbent always keep the rival out?}
\framesubtitle{The incumbent is prepared. The rival must pay to prepare.}
\hypertarget{main:s1}{}
\vspace{2.2em}
\begin{columns}[T,totalwidth=\linewidth]
\column{0.31\linewidth}
\ColHead{Seller}The seller seeks one more bidder for a listed target. A stronger incumbent can help find the rival, or keep it out.
\column{0.31\linewidth}
\ColHead{Rival}The rival cannot bid until it pays to prepare. The stock price can show whether preparation pays.
\column{0.31\linewidth}
\ColHead{Price}Target shares trade before the rival prepares. The price can change whether the rival prepares.
\end{columns}
\vfill
\FootLine{One extra bidder can change target proceeds at first order (Bulow and Klemperer, 1996). With the sale rule fixed, the question is entry.}
\end{frame}}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- Frame 2
\begin{frame}[t,label=S2]{The received answer: strength cannot raise entry}
\framesubtitle{When what the rival can learn stays fixed}
\hypertarget{main:s2}{}
\vspace{0.6em}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{8pt}
\item The rival must pay to prepare before it learns its value and can bid.
\item A stronger incumbent weakly cuts rival gross profit at each belief about synergy.
\item Preparation can stop paying, so entry cannot rise.
\item Fishman (1988): the first bidder chooses a preemptive bid. Here strength is public; the incumbent's own value stays private.
\end{itemize}
\TakeawayEnd{This answer holds the rival's news fixed. A stock price can change what the rival learns.}
\FootLine{Fishman (1988); Hirshleifer and Png (1989). Auction entry: Samuelson (1985); McAfee and McMillan (1987); Levin and Smith (1994); Gentry and Stroup (2019).}
\end{frame}

\Body

\TakeawayEnd{A stronger incumbent can shift more of the synergy gap from the rival to shareholders, so the informed trader can earn more.}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- Frame 4
\begin{frame}[t,label=S4]{A stronger incumbent can recruit the rival}
\hypertarget{main:s4}{}
\Body
\LargeLine{Strength can widen the shareholder spread, the good-minus-poor gap in target proceeds. The informed trader can then profit, and the price can recruit the high-cost rival.}
\vspace{0.1em}
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
\item \textbf{Non-monotone entry:} entry can rise from the weak to the strong incumbent, then return to its floor at the very strong one. Each case has a unique outcome (Proposition 2).
\item \textbf{Value split between rival and shareholders:} strength weakly cuts rival gross profit and weakly widens the shareholder spread (Proposition 1).
\item \textbf{Fixed-information control:} with the rival's news fixed, a stronger incumbent cannot raise entry.
\end{enumerate}
\vspace{0.2em}
{\raggedright Extensions: costly research by the informed trader; gains for target shareholders from a visible price at the strong incumbent; a purchase-impact prediction.\par}
\vfill
\LightRule
{\sffamily\color{cGrayText}Roadmap: Model $\rightarrow$ Mechanism and main result $\rightarrow$ Fixed-information control $\rightarrow$ Extensions and prediction.\par}
\end{frame}

% the next frame in the foot zone.
\begin{frame}[t,label=S5]{Players and the sale rule}
\hypertarget{main:s5}{}
\ModelStrip{1}
\Body
{\raggedright Values and costs are per share; the target's standalone value is $0$; all agents are risk neutral.\par}
\begin{DefList}[2.7cm]
\DefRow{Incumbent}{\textbf{incumbent bidder, already prepared:} no further preparation cost; value $R\sim F$, private until bids; $F$ is public and continuous on $[0,\bar r]$. \textbf{Stronger:} $\widetilde F(y)\le F(y)$ for all $y$.}
\DefRow{Rival}{\textbf{potential rival bidder:} sees the target stock price $P$ (frame 6), then decides whether to prepare. \textbf{Synergy class} $j\in\{H,L\}$ ($H$ good, $L$ poor synergy), prior $\pi=\Pr(j=H)\in(0,1)$; value $\theta\mid j\sim G_j$, with finite mean.}
\DefRow{Class ranking}{$G_H(y)\le G_L(y)$ for all $y$, and $G_H(p^-)=G_L(p^-)=0$: every value meets the reserve $p$ (Sale row). The sale and the rival's identity are public; $j$ is not.}
\DefRow{Preparation cost $C$}{$\Pr(C=0)=\rho\in(0,1)$, else $C=c_H>0$, the high cost ($H$ for high, not class $H$). A rival who prepares pays $C$, learns $\theta$ and can bid; otherwise it cannot bid.}
\DefRow{Sale}{the seller commits before trade to a cash second-price auction with reserve $p>0$. Target proceeds go to target shareholders.}
\end{DefList}
\vfill
\LightRule
{\footnotesize\raggedright $(j,\theta)$, $R$, $C$ and the trading-slot draws (frame 6) are mutually independent. Neither bidder trades target shares.\par}
\end{frame}

  \node[lab] at (8.85,-0.4) {Rival sees $P$, learns $C$, prepares or stays out};
  \node[lab] at (11.8,-0.4) {Prepared rival learns $\theta$; both bid; payoffs};

  \node[font=\small,inner xsep=0pt] (inc5) at (11.8,-2.1) {Incumbent bids $R$};

\vspace{-0.5em}
\LightRule
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item One trader fills the slot with one unit order $q$; the outcome is $X=q\in\{-1,0,1\}$: stock sale order, no order, stock purchase order.
\item With probability $\lambda\in(0,1)$ (the informed presence), the slot goes to an informed trader who is not a bidder. It observes $j$ and chooses $q$ at cost $k|q|$, $k>0$.
\item With probability $1-\lambda$, the slot goes to a liquidity trader: $\Pr(q=1)=\Pr(q=-1)=\beta$, $\Pr(q=0)=1-2\beta$, with $0<\beta\le 1/3$.
\item Competitive market makers observe $X$ only and set $P(X)=\mathbb{E}[V_T\mid X]$, with $V_T$ target proceeds per share. The expectation includes any entry that the price causes.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\Body
{\footnotesize\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{4.9cm}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{4.3cm}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{5.1cm}@{}}

Player & Observes & Chooses\\
\midrule

{\footnotesize Common knowledge: all frame 5--6 parameters and distributions. `Observes' lists private information beyond that.\par}
\vspace{0.5em}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{3pt}
\item \textbf{Order belief} (market makers): $\mu(x)=\Pr(j=H\mid X=x)$.
\item \textbf{Price belief} (rival): $\bar\mu(\bar P)=\Pr(j=H\mid P(X)=\bar P)$; it pools all $x$ with $P(x)=\bar P$, so it need not equal $\mu(x)$ for one such $x$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- Frame 8
\begin{frame}[t,label=S8]{Payoffs}
\hypertarget{main:s8}{}
\ModelStrip{5}
\Body
\begin{center}\scalebox{0.88}{\SplitLine{6.2}{incumbent's value $R$}}\end{center}
\vspace{-0.5em}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item Prepared rival with value $\theta\ge p$: expected target proceeds $t(\theta)=\mathbb E_R[\max\{p,\min(R,\theta)\}]$ and expected rival gross profit $g(\theta)=\mathbb E_R[\max\{\theta-\max\{p,R\},0\}]$, before $C$. Hence $t(\theta)+g(\theta)=\theta$.
\item Class means: $t_j=\mathbb E_j[t(\theta)]$, $g_j=\mathbb E_j[g(\theta)]$, with $\mathbb E_j$ under $G_j$. With no prepared rival, target proceeds are $p\,\mathbf 1\{R\ge p\}$, so $t_0=p[1-F(p)]$.
\item \textbf{Shareholder spread:} $\Delta_T=t_H-t_L$. At belief $\mu=\Pr(j=H)$, \textbf{rival gross profit} is $B(\mu)=g_L+\mu(g_H-g_L)$; at price $\bar P$, the net gain from preparation is $B(\bar\mu(\bar P))-C$.
\item The informed trader who submits $q$ (so $X=q$) earns $q[V_T-P(q)]-k|q|$.
\item Gross of preparation cost, a winner gets its value minus its payment; a loser gets $0$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t,label=S9]{Equilibrium with competitive prices}
\hypertarget{main:s9}{}
\ModelStrip{6}
\Body
{\raggedright Perfect Bayesian equilibrium with competitive (break-even) prices: orders, prices, beliefs and preparation all fit one pair $P(\cdot)$, $e(\cdot)$.\par}
\vspace{0.15em}
\begin{DefList}[2.7cm]
\DefRow{Orders}{entry $e(x)=\Pr(\text{rival prepares}\mid X=x)$ depends on $x$ only through $P(x)$. An informed trader who sees class $j$ has expected payoff $U_j(q)=q[t_0+e(q)(t_j-t_0)-P(q)]-k|q|$ from order $q$, where $t_0+e(q)(t_j-t_0)=\mathbb E[V_T\mid j,X=q]$. In each class $j$, it puts positive probability only on $\arg\max_q U_j(q)$ at the equilibrium $P(\cdot)$, $e(\cdot)$.}
\DefRow{Competitive prices}{$P(x)=\mathbb{E}[V_T\mid X=x]=t_0+e(x)[t_L-t_0+\Delta_T\,\mu(x)]$. The $C=0$ rival always prepares (last row), so $e(x)\ge\rho$: $\rho$ is the entry floor. A purchase \textbf{recruits} when $e(1)>\rho$, that is, when it brings in high-cost preparation.}
\DefRow{Beliefs}{$\mu(x)$ and $\bar\mu(\bar P)$ (frame 7) follow Bayes' rule given the order strategy. Each outcome has positive probability in each class under liquidity trade, so the order beliefs are pinned down.}
\DefRow{Preparation and bids}{a rival with $C=0$ prepares at each price; with $C=c_H$, it prepares at price $\bar P$ if and only if $B(\bar\mu(\bar P))\ge c_H$. Bids are \textbf{truthful}: a prepared rival bids $\theta$, the incumbent $R$.}
\end{DefList}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t,label=S10]{A stronger incumbent can shift the split toward shareholders}
\framesubtitle{Proposition 1: rival gross profit weakly falls; the shareholder spread weakly rises under the class ranking}
\hypertarget{main:s10}{}
\Body\TightMath
\begin{Statement}{Proposition 1}
\raggedright
Let $F,\widetilde F$ be continuous incumbent distributions, with $\widetilde F$ stronger: $\widetilde F(y)\le F(y)$ for every $y$.
Fix $p$ and the finite-mean class distributions $G_H,G_L$.\par
\vspace{0.1em}
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.46\linewidth}\raggedright
(a) For $j\in\{H,L\}$ and every $\mu\in[0,1]$,
\[
g_j(\widetilde F)\le g_j(F),\qquad
B_{\widetilde F}(\mu)\le B_F(\mu).
\]
Here $g_j(F)$ and $B_F$ are $g_j$ and $B$ (frame 8) under $F$.
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.51\linewidth}\raggedright
(b) Under the class ranking (frame 5), the shareholder spread satisfies $\Delta_T(\widetilde F)\ge\Delta_T(F)$.
\end{minipage}\par
\vspace{0.1em}
Since both classes meet the reserve almost surely,
$\Delta_T(F)+[g_H(F)-g_L(F)]
=\mathbb E_H[\theta]-\mathbb E_L[\theta]$, which does not depend on $F$.
\end{Statement}
\par\centerline{\scalebox{0.5}{\GapLine{5.4}{8.2}}}\par
\Intuition{A stronger incumbent can move part of the good-minus-poor value gap from the rival to target shareholders.}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- Frame 11
% Rows: the definitions full width; the band and the price-reveals-belief part side by side; the
% preparation rule with its cases beside it.
\begin{frame}[t,label=S11]{Beliefs stay in a band}
\framesubtitle{With a positive shareholder spread, the price reveals the order belief}
\hypertarget{main:s11}{}
\Body\TightMath
\begin{Statement}{Belief bounds}
\raggedright
Define\quad $b=(1-\lambda)\beta,\qquad u=\dfrac{\lambda}{b},\qquad
m(\pi)=\dfrac{\pi}{\pi+(1-\pi)(1+u)},\qquad
M(\pi)=\dfrac{\pi(1+u)}{\pi(1+u)+1-\pi}.$\par
\Gloss{Here $b$ is the liquidity mass on one order direction, $u$ the mass ratio, $M$ the top belief and $m$ the band floor.}
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.46\linewidth}\raggedright
For every order strategy (pure or mixed) and every $x\in\{-1,0,1\}$,
\[
0<\beta\le\frac13
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
m(\pi)\le\mu(x)\le M(\pi).
\]
\Gloss{With $\beta\le1/3$, liquidity trade covers all three outcomes.}
The price belief $\bar\mu$ obeys the same bounds.
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.51\linewidth}\raggedright
Suppose $\Delta_T>0$, $g_H>g_L$ and $\rho\in(0,1)$.
Under Bayes' rule, optimal preparation, competitive prices and truthful bids,
\[
\begin{aligned}
&P(x)=P(x')\ \Longrightarrow\ \mu(x)=\mu(x'),\\
&\bar\mu(P(x))=\mu(x).
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{minipage}\par
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.46\linewidth}\raggedright
\textbf{Preparation rule.} Define the preparation threshold $\tau=(c_H-g_L)/(g_H-g_L)$, so $B(\mu)\ge c_H\iff\mu\ge\tau$. If an indifferent rival prepares, entry $e(x)$ after order $x$ is
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.51\linewidth}\raggedright
\[
e(x)=\begin{cases}\rho,&\mu(x)<\tau,\\1,&\mu(x)\ge\tau.\end{cases}
\]
\end{minipage}
\end{Statement}
\Intuition{Liquidity orders look the same as informed orders, so the class stays uncertain after any order.}
\PlaceNav{\hyperlink{app:b2}{\beamergotobutton{B2}}}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- Frame 12
\begin{frame}[t,label=S12]{Gross gain from the correct order lies in $[\rho\kappa(\pi)\Delta_T,\ \Delta_T]$}
\hypertarget{main:s12}{}
\Body\TightMath
\begin{Statement}{Trading bounds}
\raggedright
Fix a shareholder spread $\Delta_T\ge0$ and any order strategy, with Bayes beliefs, competitive prices, truthful bids
and $e(x)\in[\rho,1]$.
Call a purchase ($x=1$) in class $H$ and a sale order ($x=-1$) in class $L$ the correct orders. Their gross gains are
\[
K_H=e(1)\Delta_T[1-\mu(1)],\qquad
K_L=e(-1)\Delta_T\mu(-1).
\]
Define $\kappa(\pi)=\min\{m(\pi),1-M(\pi)\}$.
\Gloss{$\kappa(\pi)$ is a lower bound on the doubt that a correct order leaves.}
\[
\rho\kappa(\pi)\Delta_T\le K_j\le\Delta_T
\qquad(j\in\{H,L\}).
\]
Net of the order cost, wrong-signed orders give $U_H(-1),U_L(1)\le-k<0$; no order earns zero, gross and net.
The informed trader in class $j$ sends the correct order if $K_j>k$ and no order if $K_j<k$.
At $K_j=k$, the correct order, no order and every mixture of the two are optimal.
\end{Statement}
\Intuition{Gross gain is entry probability times shareholder spread times the doubt left after the order. The informed trader needs that gain to cover the order cost.}
\PlaceNav{\hyperlink{app:b2}{\beamergotobutton{B2}}}
\end{frame}

\end{minipage}\par
\end{Statement}

\begin{frame}[t,label=S16]{A wider shareholder spread raises research, up to $\bar\lambda$}

\begin{Statement}{Proposition 4}
\raggedright
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\linewidth}\raggedright

\Delta_T>2k\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tau\le M_{\widehat\lambda}.
\]

\end{Statement}
\Intuition{A wider shareholder spread raises research up to the cap; where research rises, entry rises under good synergy and falls under poor.}

\begin{frame}[t,label=B1]{Proposition 2 in exact form}
\hypertarget{app:b1}{}\BackupId{B1}
\footnotesize
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item Setup: the synergy distributions satisfy the class ranking (good synergy first-order dominates poor synergy; each value meets the reserve $p$) and have finite means. The incumbent's value distribution $F$ is continuous on $[0,\bar r]$, with finite upper bound $\bar r$. Distributions $F_0$, $F_1$, $F_2$ satisfy $F_2\le F_1\le F_0$, so each step is a first-order stochastic strengthening. Fixed: prior $\pi$, entry floor $\rho$ and presence $\lambda$, each in $(0,1)$; liquidity chance $\beta\in(0,1/2)$; high cost $c_H>0$; order cost $k>0$. Write $b=(1-\lambda)\beta$ for the liquidity mass in one direction, and $\kappa(\pi)=\min\{m(\pi),1-M(\pi)\}$ for the common lower bound on residual doubt, with band floor $m(\pi)$ and top belief $M(\pi)$.
\item With $B_i$ rival gross profit and $\Delta_i$ shareholder spread $\Delta_T$ against $F_i$: (H1) $B_0(\pi)<c_H<B_1(M(\pi))$. (H2) $\Delta_0<k<\rho\,\kappa(\pi)\,\Delta_1$. (H3) $B_2(M(\pi))<c_H$.
\item Weak $F_0$: informed orders $(0,0)$ by synergy class (good, poor); belief $\mu(x)=\pi$ after each order $x$; entry $e(x)=\rho$ after each order; total entry $\rho$; one stock price. Strong $F_1$: orders $(1,-1)$; beliefs after a sale, no order, a purchase are $(m(\pi),\pi,M(\pi))$; entry is $(\rho,\rho,1)$; prices rise strictly; total entry is $\rho+(1-\rho)(b+\pi\lambda)>\rho$; entry under good synergy is $\rho+(1-\rho)(b+\lambda)$, and under poor synergy $\rho+(1-\rho)b$. Very strong $F_2$: orders and beliefs as at $F_1$; entry $\rho$ after each order; total entry $\rho$; three distinct prices.
\item Ownership: $W_j(F)=\mathbb{E}_j[F(\theta)\mathbf{1}\{\theta\ge p\}]$ is the win chance of a prepared rival in case $j$, where $\theta$ is its value and the indicator $\mathbf{1}\{\theta\ge p\}$ equals one when the value meets the reserve $p$, and zero otherwise. \begin{frame}[t,label=B2]{Why each outcome is unique}
\framesubtitle{Posterior bounds, entry and prices}
\hypertarget{app:b2}{}\BackupId{B2}
\small
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{6pt}
\item \textbf{Lemma A.1} (order probabilities and beliefs), for any order plans: each order has positive probability in each synergy class. For a sale or a purchase, the chance of that order in class $j$ lies between $b$ and $b+\lambda$, where $b=(1-\lambda)\beta$ is the liquidity mass in one direction and $\lambda$ is presence. With $u=\lambda/b$, each nonzero order has likelihood ratio between $1/(1+u)$ and $1+u$, so the belief $\mu$ after that order lies in $[m(\pi),M(\pi)]$, where $M(\pi)=\pi(1+u)/[\pi(1+u)+1-\pi]$ and $m(\pi)=\pi/[\pi+(1-\pi)(1+u)]$. The no-order case lies in the same band when $\beta\le1/3$. Under full orders, good synergy buys and poor synergy sells, the beliefs after a sale, no order, a purchase are $(m(\pi),\pi,M(\pi))$. A belief at a price lies between the smallest and largest beliefs of the orders with that price.
\item \textbf{Lemma A.2} (entry and prices): entry $e(x)\ge\rho$ after each order $x$, with entry floor $\rho\in(0,1)$. The price after order $x$ is $P(x)=t_0+e(x)[t_L-t_0+\Delta_T\,\mu(x)]$, with proceeds $t_0$ without entry and $t_L$ under poor synergy. When $\Delta_T>0$ and rival gross profit rises with the belief $\mu$, equal prices give equal beliefs, so the price reveals $\mu(x)$; entry is $\rho$ when $\mu(x)<\tau$ and $1$ when $\mu(x)\ge\tau$, with threshold $\tau$ (at $\mu(x)=\tau$, any entry in $[\rho,1]$ can hold when an indifferent high-cost rival may prepare with any probability); prices rise strictly with $\mu$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}[t,label=B2b]{Why each outcome is unique}
\framesubtitle{Trading bounds and the four-step proof}
\BackupId{B2}
\footnotesize
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{4pt}
\item \textbf{Lemma A.3} (informed trading): the gain before order cost $k$ from a correct purchase under good synergy is $K_H=e(1)\,\Delta_T\,[1-\mu(1)]$; from a correct sale under poor synergy, $K_L=e(-1)\,\Delta_T\,\mu(-1)$. Under every order plan, $\rho\,\Delta_T[1-M(\pi)]\le K_H\le\Delta_T$ and $\rho\,\Delta_T\,m(\pi)\le K_L\le\Delta_T$. With $\kappa(\pi)=\min\{m(\pi),1-M(\pi)\}$, each gain lies between $\rho\,\kappa(\pi)\,\Delta_T$ and $\Delta_T$; the purchase bound uses $1-M(\pi)$ and the sale bound uses $m(\pi)$. A wrong-signed order earns at most $-k$. Good synergy buys when $K_H>k$ and stays out when $K_H<k$; poor synergy compares $K_L$ with $k$ in the same way.
\item Four-step proof: 1.~Weak: each gain is at most $\Delta_T$, below $k$, so no informed order; the belief stays at the prior; entry stays at $\rho$. 2.~Strong: each gain is at least $\rho\,\kappa(\pi)\,\Delta_T$, above $k$, so full orders hold under every candidate; beliefs are $(m(\pi),\pi,M(\pi))$. 3.~A purchase reaches $M(\pi)$, rival gross profit there strictly exceeds the high cost, so entry after a purchase is $1$; after other orders entry stays at $\rho$. 4.~Very strong: full orders stay, but rival gross profit at $M(\pi)$ falls short of the high cost, so entry returns to $\rho$ after each order.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}[t,label=B5]{Who the informed trader is}
\hypertarget{app:b5}{}\BackupId{B5}
\vspace{1.0em}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{12pt}
\item The informed trader is an outsider with sector expertise. It holds no net position in the target before trade. It has no control rights, and it cannot acquire the target.

\begin{frame}[t,label=B8]{Payoffs and Proposition 1 in full}
\framesubtitle{Payoff objects · Paper §2.4 and §3.1}
\hypertarget{app:b8}{}\BackupId{B8}
\vspace{0.6em}
{\raggedright Take a prepared rival with value $\theta\ge p$. It pays $\max\{p,R\}$ when it wins. The incumbent pays $\theta$ when it wins.\par}
\[
t(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_R\bigl[\max\{p,\min(R,\theta)\}\bigr],\qquad g(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_R\bigl[(\theta-\max\{p,R\})_+\bigr],\qquad t(\theta)+g(\theta)=\theta
\]
\vspace{-0.4em}
{\small\raggedright $\mathbb{E}_R$ averages over the incumbent's value $R\sim F$.\par}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{3pt}
\item Synergy means: $t_j=\mathbb{E}_j[t(\theta)]$ and $g_j=\mathbb{E}_j[g(\theta)]$ for $j\in\{H,L\}$. Without entry, $t_0=p[1-F(p)]$.
\item Shareholder spread: $\Delta_T=t_H-t_L$. Read it as: how much more shareholders get with good synergy than with poor synergy.
\item At belief $\mu=\Pr(H)$, expected rival gross profit is $B(\mu)=g_L+\mu(g_H-g_L)$.
\item Win chance: $W_j(F)=\mathbb{E}_j[F(\theta)\mathbf 1\{\theta\ge p\}]$. A good-synergy rival outbids $F$ when $W_H(F)=1$.
\end{itemize}
\TakeawayEnd{The traded claim and the rival's claim sum to the same value. Strength moves the split.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t,label=B8s]{Payoffs and Proposition 1 in full}
\framesubtitle{Proposition 1 · Paper §3.1}
\BackupId{B8}
\small
\vspace{0.4em}
\begin{Statement}{Proposition 1}
\raggedright
Let $\tilde F\le F$ be a first-order stochastic strengthening of the distribution $F$.\par\vspace{2pt}
(a) For any class distributions with finite means, gross auction profit $g_H$ and $g_L$ weakly fall. Rival profit $B(\mu)$ weakly falls at each fixed belief $\mu$.\par\vspace{2pt}
(b) Under the class ranking $G_H\le G_L$, the shareholder spread $\Delta_T$ weakly rises.\par\vspace{2pt}
Under the class ranking, $\Delta_T(F)+g_H(F)-g_L(F)=\mathbb{E}_H[\max\{\theta,p\}]-\mathbb{E}_L[\max\{\theta,p\}]$, with the rival's value $\theta$ and reserve $p$.
\end{Statement}
\vspace{0.6em}
\begin{itemize}
\item Intuition: one class value gap is fixed, and strength moves its split between target proceeds and rival gross profit.
\end{itemize}
\BackButton{main:s10}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- B8, page 3
\begin{frame}[t,label=B8b]{Payoffs and Proposition 1 in full}
\framesubtitle{Proposition 1 in full: forms, strict signs, equal admissibility}
\BackupId{B8}
\vspace{0.6em}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{10pt}
\item With class gap $D(y)=G_L(y)-G_H(y)$: $\Delta_T(F)=pF(p)D(p^-)+\int_p^\infty[1-F(y)]D(y)\,dy$ and $g_j(F)=\int_p^\infty[1-G_j(y)]F(y)\,dy$.
\item Profit falls strictly exactly when $\int_p^\infty[1-G_j(y)][F(y)-\tilde F(y)]\,dy>0$. Spread rises strictly exactly when $\int_p^\infty[F(y)-\tilde F(y)]D(y)\,dy>0$.
\item Without the class ranking, the reserve term $pF(p)D(p^-)$ can reverse the spread sign. With no class atom at the reserve, the spread weakly rises under every strengthening exactly when class $H$ dominates above the reserve and the classes have equal admissibility, $G_H(p^-)=G_L(p^-)$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}[t,label=B9]{Equilibrium in full}
\hypertarget{app:b9}{}\BackupId{B9}
\footnotesize
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
\item Tuple: $\mathcal{E}=(\sigma_H,\sigma_L,\mu,P,\bar\mu,\zeta)$, with truthful bids. Order distribution in class $j$: $\sigma_j$ on $\{-1,0,1\}$. Order belief: $\mu(x)=\Pr(j=H\mid X=x)$. Stock price after order $x$: $P(x)$. Belief at a price: $\bar\mu(\bar P)=\Pr(j=H\mid P(X)=\bar P)$. High-cost preparation probability at price $\bar P$: $\zeta(\bar P)$. Cost $C=0$ prepares at each price.
\item Liquidity probabilities: $\eta(-1)=\eta(1)=\beta$ and $\eta(0)=1-2\beta$. Order probability in class $j$: $f_j(x)=(1-\lambda)\eta(x)+\lambda\sigma_j(x)$, with presence $\lambda$. Entry after order $x$: $e(x)=\rho+(1-\rho)\zeta(P(x))$, with entry floor $\rho$.
\item (E1) Order beliefs by Bayes' rule: $\mu(x)=\pi f_H(x)/[\pi f_H(x)+(1-\pi)f_L(x)]$ after each order $x$, with prior $\pi$.
\item (E2) Beliefs at a price by Bayes' rule: $\bar\mu(\bar P)=\pi\sum_{x:\,P(x)=\bar P}f_H(x)\big/\sum_{x:\,P(x)=\bar P}[\pi f_H(x)+(1-\pi)f_L(x)]$ at each price $\bar P$ that occurs.
\item (E3) Preparation: $\zeta(\bar P)=1$ when rival gross profit $B(\bar\mu(\bar P))>c_H$, and $\zeta(\bar P)=0$ when $B(\bar\mu(\bar P))<c_H$, with high cost $c_H$. At equality, the tie rule applies.
\item (E4) Competitive prices: $P(x)=\mathbb{E}[V_T\mid X=x]$ after each order $x$, with sale proceeds per share $V_T$. In payoff objects, $P(x)=t_0+e(x)[t_L-t_0+\Delta_T\,\mu(x)]$, with shareholder spread $\Delta_T$ and class proceeds $t_L$, $t_0$ from frame 8.
\item (E5) Orders: $\sigma_j(q)>0$ only when the order $q$ maximises the informed trader's class-$j$ payoff $U_j(q)=q[t_0+e(q)(t_j-t_0)-P(q)]-k|q|$, with order cost $k$. Prices $P$ and entry $e$ stay at the stated functions.
\item (E6) Truthful bids: each prepared bidder with value at or above reserve $p$ bids its value.
\item Tie rules, default pair: an indifferent informed trader may mix over its best orders, and an indifferent rival at cost $c_H$ prepares. Other rules: an indifferent informed trader abstains, or an indifferent rival at cost $c_H$ prepares with any probability.
\end{itemize}

\item At prior $1/2$, order frequencies match. Compare raw impacts: $P(1)-P(0)$ against $P(0)-P(-1)$.

\item At prior $1/2$, order counts cannot separate slot types. Prices can.
\end{enumerate}}

\begin{frame}[t,label=B13b]{Predictions in full}
\BackupId{B13}
\small
\vspace{0.4em}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{5pt}
\item Mean order under full two-sided trade is $\lambda(2\pi-1)$. Counts name informed presence only when the prior is known and differs from $1/2$.
\item At prior $1/2$, sale impact plus volume name presence when entry sits at its floor after a sale and after no order, with payoff scale $\rho\Delta_T$ known.
\item For a prepared rival, the winner gap after purchase against sale equals $[M(\pi)-m(\pi)](W_H-W_L)$, with win chance $W_j$ in class $j$. The prediction is strict only when $W_H>W_L$.
\item A report that reveals preparation alone carries a positive mean price change exactly when preparation stayed uncertain after some order. Both reports together average to zero.
\item At one middle strength, sale processes can form two clusters: uninformative prices with mean order zero, or informative prices with mean order $\lambda/2$.
\item Count preparation starts, not public offers alone. Date incumbent strength from public records before the preparation decision.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}[t,label=B13c]{Predictions in full}
\framesubtitle{Weighted price impact and the winner · Proposition A.10}
\BackupId{B13}
\small
\vspace{0.6em}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{6pt}
\item Let order frequency be $\bar f(x)=\Pr(X=x)$, purchase impact $I_+=P(1)-P(0)$, and sale impact $I_-=P(0)-P(-1)$. \textbf{Proposition A.10:} under full two-sided trade, class $H$ buys and class $L$ sells with probability one, and prior $\pi<{}$threshold $\tau$, the weighted difference $\bar f(1)I_+-\bar f(-1)I_-$ is positive exactly when entry after a purchase exceeds the floor, $e(1)>\rho$. At prior $\pi=1/2$ the frequencies agree, and the raw impacts $I_+$ and $I_-$ compare directly. The entry part of the price gap $P(1)-P(-1)$ sits in purchase impact $I_+$.
\item Under the same full two-sided trade, where entry $e(x)=\rho$ after each order, the order $X$ does not predict entry. It still predicts the winner exactly when the win chance $W_H$ exceeds $W_L$, with $W_j=\mathbb{E}_j[F(\theta)\mathbf{1}\{\theta\ge p\}]$ for a prepared rival of class $j$; the indicator $\mathbf{1}\{\theta\ge p\}$ is one when the value meets the reserve. For a prepared rival, the win-probability gap after a purchase against a sale is $[M(\pi)-m(\pi)](W_H-W_L)$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}[t,label=B15]{Continuous order sizes}
\hypertarget{app:b15}{}\BackupId{B15}
\small
\vspace{0.4em}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{4pt}
\item The slot set-up uses unit orders $\{-1,0,1\}$, so every order is on path and no off-path belief enters
\item A menu of continuous order sizes needs a convention for the sizes that no synergy class uses, as in Edmans, Goldstein and Jiang 2015
\item Additive liquidity flow also keeps every size on path
\item With Laplace noise of scale $\xi$ and full unit orders, the top belief equals the slot value $M(\pi)$ when $1+u=\mathrm{e}^{2/\xi}$
\item The two set-ups then share the belief bounds. They differ in the probability that each synergy class reaches the top belief
\end{itemize}
\Takeaway{Unit orders keep prices on path at every order.}
