\section{Panel preparation}\label{sec:13_panel}

This chapter collects what you need in the room: seven derivations to do from memory, the likely questions with
answers, the points that a referee will press, the terms to use exactly, and a plan for a 15-minute talk. Each
answer points to the chapter that derives it in full.

\subsection{Seven derivations to do on a whiteboard}\label{sec:13_panel-whiteboard}

Practise these until you can do each one in about two minutes, without notes.

\paragraph{W1. The spread.} If \(R\le\ell\), both classes pay \(\max\{p,R\}\). If \(R>\ell\), class \(H\) wins and
pays \(R\), and against class \(L\) the incumbent wins and pays \(\ell\). So the gap is \((R-\ell)_+\). With
\(R\sim U[0,r]\),
\[
\DT=\frac1r\int_\ell^r(y-\ell)\,dy=\frac{(r-\ell)^2}{2r},\qquad \DT'(r)=\frac{r^2-\ell^2}{2r^2}>0 .
\]
For general classes, the class ranking gives \(\DT(F)=\int_p^\infty[1-F(y)]D(y)\,dy\): a stronger incumbent moves
slices \(D(y)\,dy\) of the value gap to the target (\Cref{sec:03_auction-terms,sec:03_auction-prop1}).

\paragraph{W2. Posterior bounds and \texorpdfstring{\(\beta\le1/3\)}{beta <= 1/3}.} For \(x=\pm1\), every profile gives
\(b\le f_j(x)\le b+\lambda\). So \(1/(1+u)\le f_H(x)/f_L(x)\le1+u\). The odds form of Bayes' rule,
\[
\frac{\mu(x)}{1-\mu(x)}=\frac{\pi}{1-\pi}\cdot\frac{f_H(x)}{f_L(x)},
\]
puts \(\mu(\pm1)\) in \([m(\pi),M(\pi)]\). At zero the liquidity mass is \(b_0=(1-\lambda)(1-2\beta)\), and
\(b_0\ge b\iff1-2\beta\ge\beta\iff\beta\le1/3\). A price belief averages the posteriors of the orders with that
price, so it obeys the same bounds (\Cref{sec:04_slot_beliefs-bounds,sec:04_slot_beliefs-zero,sec:04_slot_beliefs-ceiling}).

\paragraph{W3. The residual advantage and \texorpdfstring{\(\kappa\)}{kappa}.} With \(\E[V_T\mid j,X=q]=t_0+e(q)w_j\) and
\(P(q)=t_0+e(q)[w_L+\DT\mu(q)]\), subtract:
\[
\E[V_T\mid H,q]-P(q)=e(q)\DT[1-\mu(q)],\qquad P(q)-\E[V_T\mid L,q]=e(q)\DT\mu(q).
\]
Since \(1-M(\pi)=m(1-\pi)\) and \(m\) rises, \(\kappa(\pi)=\min\{m(\pi),1-M(\pi)\}=m(\min\{\pi,1-\pi\})\). Its maximum is
\(\kappa(1/2)=1/(2+u)\) (\Cref{sec:05_investor-residual,sec:05_investor-prior}).

\paragraph{W4. The global trading bounds.} Use the floor \(e\ge\rho\) and the bounds of W2:
\[
K_H=e(1)\DT[1-\mu(1)]\ge\rho\DT[1-M]\ge\rho\kappa\DT,\qquad K_L=e(-1)\DT\mu(-1)\ge\rho\DT m\ge\rho\kappa\DT,
\]
and \(K_H,K_L\le\DT\). A wrong-signed order earns at most \(-k\). So \(\DT<k\) forces zero orders, and
\(k<\rho\kappa\DT\) forces full orders, under every candidate (\Cref{sec:05_investor-bounds,sec:05_investor-force}).

\paragraph{W5. The threshold \texorpdfstring{\(\tau\)}{tau}.} \(B(\mu)=g_L+\mu(g_H-g_L)\ge c_H\) exactly when
\(\mu\ge\tau=(c_H-g_L)/(g_H-g_L)\). In the benchmark (\Cref{sec:03_auction-derivatives}),
\(c_H-g_L=(2rc_H-\ell^2+p^2)/(2r)\) and \(g_H-g_L=(2rh-r^2-\ell^2)/(2r)\), so
\[
\tau(r)=\frac{48r-3}{4(20r-r^2-1)},\qquad \tau(r_1)=\frac{141}{200}.
\]

\paragraph{W6. Entry under full orders.} Only a purchase recruits. A purchase has probability
\(\lambda+(1-\lambda)\beta=b+\lambda\) in class \(H\) and \(b\) in class \(L\). So
\[
e_H=\rho+(1-\rho)(b+\lambda),\qquad e_L=\rho+(1-\rho)b,\qquad \Ent=\rho+(1-\rho)(b+\pi\lambda),
\]
which is \reg{entry_r1_B} at the benchmark (\Cref{sec:06_main_result-entry}).

\paragraph{W7. The exact band of \texorpdfstring{\(u\)}{u}.} At \(\pi=1/2\), \(M=(1+u)/(2+u)\) and \(\kappa=1/(2+u)\).
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item (H1), right side: \(M>141/200\iff200(1+u)>141(2+u)\iff59u>82\).
\item (H3): \(M<4245/5804\iff5804(1+u)<4245(2+u)\iff1559u<2686\).
\item (H2), right side: \(1/50<1/[6(2+u)]\iff u<19/3\), which the band already implies.
\end{itemize}
So (H1)--(H3) hold exactly when \(82/59<u<2686/1559\) (\Cref{sec:06_main_result-band}).

\begin{panelnote}[if you have time for one]
Do W4. It is the step that fixes the orders before any candidate is known, so it carries the uniqueness of
Proposition~2.
\end{panelnote}

\subsection{Likely questions, with suggested answers}\label{sec:13_panel-questions}

\subsubsection*{A. Modelling choices}

\paragraph{Q1. Why one order slot?}
``It is the simplest market in which no order reveals the class. The investor fills the slot with probability
\(\lambda\); otherwise a liquidity trader fills it, in either direction, as in Dow and Gorton (1997). So every order
has positive probability under every profile, and no off-path belief enters. And no order has a likelihood ratio
above \(1+u\), so every posterior lies in \([m,M]\) under every strategy, mixed or wrong-signed. The uniqueness
proofs rest on these two facts.'' (\Cref{sec:04_slot_beliefs-law,sec:04_slot_beliefs-bounds}.)

\paragraph{Q2. What replaces Laplace noise, and what is lost?}
``The liquidity trader replaces additive noise. The bound on the likelihood ratio now comes from the liquidity mass
in each direction, not from a noise density. With \(1+u=\eu^{2/s}\) the two set-ups have the same \(m\) and \(M\);
the benchmark \(1+u=\eu\) is the Laplace scale \(2\).'' The slot gains exact algebra: three payoffs, no derivative in
the order size, and the whole equilibrium set along the path. It loses four things. The investor does not choose a
size. Full orders give only three posteriors, so frozen entry is flat up to \(r_C\) and then drops; the Laplace continuum
of posteriors let frozen entry fall as the threshold rose (\Cref{sec:07_controls-frozen}). Class \(H\) reaches the top posterior with
probability \(b+\lambda\), not \(1/2\), so equal bounds do not give equal entry. The logistic-noise extension has no
counterpart. (\Cref{sec:10_robustness-sizes}.)

\paragraph{Q3. Why \texorpdfstring{\(\beta\le1/3\)}{beta <= 1/3}?}
``It is the condition \(b_0\ge b\): a liquidity abstention is at least as likely as a liquidity order in each
direction. Then the zero order obeys the same bounds as a trade, and a price that pools the zero order keeps its
belief in \([m,M]\). Without it, a one-sided profile makes zero the most informative order: with \(\lambda=6/17\),
\(\beta=2/5\), and a profile in which class \(H\) abstains and class \(L\) sells, \(\mu(0)-M=165/1924>0\). The restriction serves the
statements that cover every profile, such as the universal ceiling. Propositions~2 and~3 force full orders first, so
they hold for every \(\beta<1/2\).'' The benchmark lies \reg{margin_beta_B} inside it.
(\Cref{sec:04_slot_beliefs-zero,sec:04_slot_beliefs-ceiling}.)

\paragraph{Q4. Why a trading cost, and why a zero preparation cost with probability \texorpdfstring{\(\rho\)}{rho}?}
``Without \(k\), any positive residual advantage makes a trade pay, so no informed trade is never an equilibrium. The
cost is execution, carry or the cost to borrow for a short sale, not price impact. The zero-cost challenger gives the
entry floor, which does three jobs: with \(\DT>0\) it lets the price reveal the posterior, it gives every correctly
signed order at least \(\rho\kappa\DT\), and it means that information can only add entry. The atoms are not needed:
Proposition~A.4 allows any cost law with a low component below \(B_1(m)\) and a high component strictly inside the
high-cost window.'' (\Cref{sec:05_investor-force,sec:04_slot_beliefs-price,sec:10_robustness-costs}.)

\paragraph{Q5. Why would a buyer learn about its own value from a stock price?}
``Value depends on facts about the target: its customers, its technology, its demand. A specialist investor can know
these before diligence; the buyer knows its own integration plans. These are different facts about one match. In
the example \(S(B)\) of Proposition~A.7, the buyer's signal is the more accurate one, \(d=\reg{sig_d}\) against
\(a_T=\reg{sig_aT}\); entry rises from \reg{p4_s_signal_r0_entry} to \reg{p4_s_signal_r1_entry}, and the rise needs
both signals.'' (\Cref{sec:10_robustness-signals}.)

\paragraph{Q6. Why a cash second-price auction, and why do the bidders not trade?}
``Truthful bids are weakly dominant, so no payoff depends on a conjecture about bid shading. The payment rule
matters: under Nash bargaining with seller weight \(\omega\), competition moves the spread with the sign of
\(1-2\omega\), and the second-price auction is \(\omega=0\). The bidders do not trade, and the sale binds all shares;
this keeps toeholds and free riding out and isolates the information channel.''
(\Cref{sec:03_auction-rules,sec:11_welfare-payment}.)

\subsubsection*{B. The mechanism}

\paragraph{Q7. In one sentence, why does a stronger incumbent raise the spread?}
``A high-class challenger must outbid the incumbent and pays \(R\), but against a low-class challenger the incumbent
wins and pays only \(\ell\); so the gap is \((R-\ell)_+\), and it grows when \(R\) tends to be larger.''
(\Cref{sec:03_auction-cases}.)

\paragraph{Q8. A stronger incumbent also lowers the challenger's profit. Where is that in the result?}
``It is the deterrence effect. It raises the preparation threshold as the incumbent strengthens, and it ends
recruitment above \(r_C\). With the information fixed, it is the only effect. The reversal comes from the change of the price from
uninformative to informative between \(r_0\) and \(r_1\).'' (\Cref{sec:03_auction-derivatives,sec:07_controls}.)

\paragraph{Q9. Market makers anticipate entry. Why is anything left for the investor?}
``Rational prices remove the no-entry proceeds \(t_0\) and the common entry gain \(e(q)w_L\). They cannot remove the
class component, because a liquidity order in the same direction is always possible. The residual is
\(e(q)\DT[1-\mu(q)]\), and \(1-\mu(1)\ge1-M>0\).'' (\Cref{sec:05_investor-residual}.)

\paragraph{Q10. Why does the price reveal the posterior, when entry depends on the price?}
``Two orders with one price face the same challenger, so they have the same entry \(\bar e\ge\rho\). The price formula
gives \(0=\bar e\DT[\mu(x)-\mu(x')]\), and \(\bar e\DT>0\) forces equal posteriors. You never solve the circle.
Recovery needs \(\DT>0\); prices that rise strictly with the posterior also need \(g_H>g_L\).''
(\Cref{sec:04_slot_beliefs-sufficiency}.)

\paragraph{Q11. Why is entry non-monotone in incumbent strength?}
``At \(r_0\) the spread is too small to pay for a trade, so the price carries no information and entry is \(\rho\). At
\(r_1\) trade is informative, and the top posterior \reg{M_half_B} clears \(\tau(r_1)=\reg{tau_r1}\). At \(r_2\) the
price stays informative, but \(\tau(r_2)=\reg{tau_r2}\) lies above the top posterior, so entry returns to \(\rho\).''
(\Cref{sec:06_main_result-ceiling}.)

\paragraph{Q12. Why does research comove with entry?}
``A wider spread raises the return to a purchase that recruits, \(\DT m_\lambda-k\). Interior research solves
\(2a\lambda=\DT m_\lambda-k\), and the implicit-function theorem gives \(\lambda_R'>0\). A purchase has probability
\(\beta+(1/2-\beta)\lambda\): research adds informed purchases in class \(H\) and removes liquidity orders in both
classes, and the first effect is larger because \(\beta<1/2\).'' From \reg{str_r155} to \reg{str_r165}, research
rises from \reg{res_lambda_r155} to \reg{res_lambda_r165} and entry from \reg{res_entry_r155} to
\reg{res_entry_r165}. (\Cref{sec:09_research-prop4}.)

\paragraph{Q13. Is the effect large, or a knife-edge?}
``The set of primitives is open and nonempty, and Proposition~A.5 constructs it for every ranked pair of classes
with different means, at every prior. At the benchmark, entry rises by \reg{entry_jump_B}, a factor of about 2.09, % verify/13_panel.py
and high-class ownership rises from \reg{own_r0} to \reg{own_r1_B}.'' The smallest margin belongs to the ceiling at
\(r_2\); \Cref{sec:13_panel-push} explains why it says nothing about the rise.

\subsubsection*{C. Proof technique}

\paragraph{Q14. What exactly is unique, and what is not?}
``In each economy of Proposition~2, the orders, posteriors, prices, beliefs and on-path entry are unique under
truthful bids. The argument allows mixed and wrong-signed orders, and every comparison is strict, so no tie rule
matters. Uniqueness fails in three places: no trade and an informative equilibrium coexist on \([r_A,r_N]\); zero
research and the research branch coexist at the intermediate strengths; and at boundary cases, such as \(u=82/59\)
or \(r=r_C\), rule CM allows a range of entry.''
(\Cref{sec:06_main_result-scope,sec:08_coexistence-set,sec:09_research-prop4}.)

\paragraph{Q15. What does coexistence mean for comparative statics?}
``Proposition~2 compares three economies with unique outcomes; it is not a derivative and not a path. On
\([r_A,r_N]\) two equilibria exist at one strength, and the model does not select one, so a statement along the path
must name its branch. On the no-trade branch entry is \(\rho\); on the informative branch it rises strictly on
\([r_A,r_F]\) and then stays at \reg{entry_r1_B} up to \(r_C\). The headline needs no selection: \(r_0\) lies below
\(r_A\), and \(r_1\) lies above \(r_J\), where only full orders remain. In data, coexistence predicts two clusters of
processes at one public strength.'' (\Cref{sec:08_coexistence-set,sec:12_empirical-clusters}.)

\paragraph{Q16. How is Proposition~3 proved, and why is there no derivative of a deviation?}
``By algebra, except two monotonicity facts: \(J\) rises on the path, and \(\DT(1-\tau)^2\) rises on \([r_0,2]\). Each
is the sign of an integer polynomial on a rational interval, and an exact Sturm count settles it. Bisection with exact
signs at rational points brackets \(r_A\), \(r_J\) and \(r_*\), and every quantity that depends on \(\eu\) uses
rational bounds on \(\eu\). A deviation by the investor compares three numbers at the candidate's prices and entry:
zero, \(K_j-k\), and at most \(-k\). Nothing is differentiated in the order size.''
(\Cref{sec:08_coexistence-proof,sec:05_investor-sign}.)

\paragraph{Q17. What does the fixed-information theorem require, and what do the examples and controls show?}
``Fix the joint law of the class and the challenger's signal \(S\), the class value laws and the cost law. In each
economy the law of the two values given \((j,S)\) must be \(G_j\otimes F_i\), and the cost must be independent of the
class, both values and \(S\). Then a stronger incumbent cannot raise entry. Each part of the product law matters. In
Example~A the signal reports the incumbent's component in one economy only, and entry rises from \(5/8\) to \(1\)
although the class posterior stays at \(1/2\). In Example~B the signal tells the challenger its value within the
class, and entry rises from \(1/4\) to \(5/8\). The controls apply the theorem: frozen full orders keep entry at
\reg{p4_frozen_r0_entry} at \(r_0\) and \(r_1\), and a hidden price keeps it at \reg{p4_hidden_r0_entry}. The frozen
profile at \(r_0\) is a control, not an equilibrium: both orders lose there.''
(\Cref{sec:07_controls-fixed,sec:07_controls-frozen,sec:07_controls-hidden}.)

\subsubsection*{D. Welfare and sale terms}

\paragraph{Q18. Who gains from price access?}
``At \(r_1\) the orders are full with and without access, so trading costs are the same. Access adds high-cost
preparation after a purchase. Expected proceeds rise by \reg{p2_access_proceeds_gain} and net surplus by
\reg{p2_access_surplus_gain}. A shareholder who holds through the sale receives \(V_T\), so shareholders receive the
proceeds gain; the investor's gross profit, \reg{p2_access_gross_transfer}, is a transfer from liquidity traders.''
Name the domain: where the high cost pays at the prior, as at \(c_H=\reg{access_out_cH}\), access removes
preparation after a sale, and proceeds change by \reg{p2_outside_proceeds_gain}.
(\Cref{sec:11_welfare-access,sec:11_welfare-transfers}.)

\paragraph{Q19. Should the seller set a reserve to make the price informative?}
``A reserve that excludes the low class widens the spread, so trade is informative against both incumbents. At
\(p=\reg{rsv_points_excl}\) the top posterior recruits at both strengths, and entry is flat at
\reg{p2_reserve_points_exclude_r0_entry}. At \(p=\reg{rsv_deter}\) strict deterrence returns. The seller's optimal
terms with the whole trading continuation are the next theoretical step.'' (\Cref{sec:11_welfare-reserves}.)

\subsubsection*{E. Empirics}

\paragraph{Q20. How would you test this, when incumbent strength is endogenous?}
``Take publicly visible sale processes with one prepared bidder. The outcome is the start of substantive diligence by a
further buyer. Measure strength only from information that is public before that decision, and date the price before
it, because target returns before a bid partly reflect anticipation (Schwert 1996). Where the price cannot reach the
buyer in time, a stronger incumbent can only deter. The order is the first prespecified public order opportunity,
such as the opening call auction, and an opportunity with no order stays in the sample as a zero. A second test
needs no auction values: the purchase impact exceeds the sale impact exactly when a purchase recruits. At \(r_1\)
the entry component is \reg{ba_share_r1_B} of the spread.''
(\Cref{sec:12_empirical-spread,sec:12_empirical-measurement}.)

\subsection{Where a panel will push}\label{sec:13_panel-push}

A referee will press six points. Use one formula for each: ``This is a deliberate choice. Here is what it buys. Here
is where the paper relaxes it, or why the result does not depend on it.''

\paragraph{P1. ``The investor knows the class exactly, and the buyer knows nothing.''}
\emph{It buys} one public belief, so the mechanism is a short chain of inequalities. \emph{Relaxed:}
Proposition~A.7 gives both sides private signals about different facts; in \(S(B)\) the buyer's signal is the more
accurate one, and the rise needs both signals. At the benchmark, with \((a_T,d)=(\reg{sig_comp_aT},\reg{sig_comp_d})\), entry rises
from \reg{p4_comp_b_r0_entry} to \reg{p4_comp_b_r1_entry}. Say what fails: at the benchmark costs and strengths the
sufficient conditions cannot hold with \(d\ge a_T\), so \(S(B)\) has its own costs and strengths. Section~5 of the
paper makes the investor's information the result of costly research
(\Cref{sec:10_robustness-signals,sec:09_research}).

\paragraph{P2. ``The windows of (H1)--(H3) are narrow at the benchmark.''}
The band is \(82/59<u<2686/1559\); the benchmark \(u=\reg{u_B}\) lies \reg{margin_u_hi_B} below its top, and the
smallest margin is \(c_H-B_{r_2}(M)=\reg{margin_return_B}\). Answer in four steps.
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item The small margins belong to (H3) and measure where \(r_2\) sits: \(r_2-r_C=\reg{gap_r2_rC_B}\). The ceiling
holds on all of \((r_C,h)\), and its margin is 0.149164 at \(r=4\). % verify/13_panel.py
\item The rise uses only (H1) and (H2): \(82/59<u<19/3\), that is \(141/200<M<22/25\), or presence between
\reg{lambda_lo_B} and 0.651100 at \(\beta=\reg{beta_B}\). % verify/13_panel.py
\item The trading-cost window runs from \reg{Delta_r0} to \(1/[6(1+\eu)]=0.044824\), % verify/13_panel.py
so its upper end is about 2.69 times its lower end. % verify/13_panel.py
\item Proposition~A.5 constructs an open set for every ranked pair of classes with different means, at every
prior.
\end{enumerate}
So the rise is robust; the return to \(\rho\) is close to its boundary because \(r_2\) sits just above \(r_C\)
(\Cref{sec:06_main_result-band,sec:06_main_result-scope}).

\paragraph{P3. ``What happens between the economies, and which equilibrium is selected?''}
\emph{It buys} a headline in which each economy has one outcome, so no selection drives it. \emph{Relaxed:}
Proposition~3 lists the whole equilibrium set at every strength on the path, for every slot in the band;
Table~A.3 shows what changes under the other tie rules; and Corollary~A.2 covers every prior. The model does not select on \([r_A,r_N]\); the empirical
chapter turns this into two clusters. Say plainly what remains open: with research, the set on \((r_N,r_U)\)
(\Cref{sec:08_coexistence-set,sec:08_coexistence-ties,sec:09_research-branch}).

\paragraph{P4. ``The payment-rule result covers only the payment stage.''}
\emph{It buys} the one object that the mechanism takes from the sale rule: how competition moves the spread.
\emph{Scope:} Proposition~A.9 is a payment-stage result; the trading and preparation equilibrium under bargaining is
not solved. What it shows: for \(\omega<1/2\) the spread still rises with strength, at \(\omega=1/2\) it is
\((h-\ell)/2\) for every incumbent, and above \(1/2\) competition narrows it (\Cref{sec:11_welfare-payment}).

\paragraph{P5. ``The seller would choose its terms.''}
\emph{It buys} a clean separation of the information channel from the design problem. \emph{Relaxed:}
Proposition~A.10 changes the reserve: exclusion makes the price informative at both strengths and can flatten entry,
and strict deterrence holds for reserves between about \reg{p2_reserve_ph} and \reg{p2_reserve_weak_top}.
\emph{Open:} the seller-optimal reserve, and whether the seller commits before trade or revises after the price; the
conclusion of the paper names this the next step (\Cref{sec:11_welfare-reserves}).

\paragraph{P6. ``What is not modelled?''}
Toeholds, free riding and manipulation: the bidders do not trade, and the sale binds all shares, so the incumbent
cannot move the price and the investor earns a pure trading residual. Order sizes and repeated trade: one unit
opportunity keeps every order on path and gives exact bounds (\Cref{sec:10_robustness-sizes}). The investor's
position size: the scale of a unit holds the experiment fixed (\Cref{sec:11_welfare-transfers}). The zero-cost
challenger prepares by convention, even at zero rent. Payment is in cash. The empirical results are predictions with
a measurement design, not estimates (\Cref{sec:12_empirical}).

\subsection{A 15-minute talk}\label{sec:13_panel-talk}

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{(1 min) Question.} Does a stronger rival keep a costly entrant out? The standard answer is yes.
\item \textbf{(2 min) Setting.} The decision interval; a prepared incumbent; a challenger that watches the price; one
order slot (Figure~\ref{fig:02_model-timing}).
\item \textbf{(3 min) Two claims on one surplus.} \(\DT=(r-\ell)^2/(2r)\) rises while \(B_r(\mu)\) falls; the split
\(\DT+g_H-g_L=h-\ell\) (Figure~\ref{fig:03_auction-proceeds}).
\item \textbf{(3 min) What one order reveals.} The bounds \([m,M]\); price sufficiency; the residual advantage and the
bounds \(\rho\kappa\DT\le K_j\le\DT\).
\item \textbf{(3 min) Main result.} (H1)--(H3) in words; Table~\ref{tab:01_brief-numbers}; entry rises, then returns
