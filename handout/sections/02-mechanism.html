Two returns to information

2 · The mechanism · 3 minutes

The rival uses information to assess its acquisition profit. The informed trader uses the same class information to value the target's shares. The auction connects these two returns: a stronger incumbent reduces the rival's profit and makes target proceeds more sensitive to its class.

One auction, two claims

The symbols \(g_H\) and \(g_L\) denote expected acquisition profit before the preparation cost, and \(t_H\) and \(t_L\) denote expected target proceeds with preparation. At belief \(\mu=\Pr(j=H)\), the rival prepares when expected profit \(B(\mu)=g_L+\mu(g_H-g_L)\) covers its cost. The target-payoff spread \(\Delta_T=t_H-t_L\) measures how much more shareholders receive with a high-class rival.

Under the second-price rule, a higher incumbent value moves part of the class value gap into target proceeds. At each value level between the reserve \(p\) and incumbent value \(R\), the incumbent outbids the rival and pays its bid. The remainder stays as the rival's profit. Proposition 1 applies to every continuous incumbent distribution. A stronger incumbent never raises the probability that a prepared rival wins. For every pair of classes, it therefore weakly reduces both \(g_H\) and \(g_L\), and gross profit falls weakly at every belief. Under the class ranking it weakly raises \(\Delta_T\), and the two gaps sum to a constant:

\[ \begin{gathered} \Delta_T(F)+g_H(F)-g_L(F)\\ =\mathbb E_H[\max\{\theta,p\}]-\mathbb E_L[\max\{\theta,p\}], \end{gathered} \]

The right side does not depend on the incumbent; in the example it is \(h-\ell\) at every strength \(r\). A stronger incumbent leaves the value gap between the classes unchanged and moves it from the rival's profit to target proceeds. The class ranking requires first-order stochastic dominance of \(H\) over \(L\), with all values in both classes at or above the reserve.

What one order can reveal

A purchase can come from a liquidity trader in either class, so it leaves uncertainty about the class. The same applies to a sale and to no order. Each liquidity purchase or sale has probability \(b=(1-\lambda)\beta\), and the informed trader adds at most \(\lambda\) to an order's probability. With \(\beta\le1/3\), the posterior after every order obeys the same bounds under every pure or mixed strategy of the informed trader:

\[ \begin{gathered} m(\pi)\le\mu(x)\le M(\pi),\qquad u=\frac{\lambda}{b},\\ m(\pi)=\frac{\pi}{\pi+(1-\pi)(1+u)},\\ M(\pi)=\frac{\pi(1+u)}{\pi(1+u)+1-\pi}. \end{gathered} \]

When the high class buys and the low class sells, a sale, no order and a purchase yield posteriors \(m(\pi)\), \(\pi\) and \(M(\pi)\), respectively. In the example's slot at the prior one half, the top posterior is \(M=[[M_half_B]]\). When \(g_H\ge g_L\) and gross profit at the top posterior falls below \(c_H\), no order recruits a high-cost rival, in or out of equilibrium. This is the exclusion bound. In the example it holds against every incumbent stronger than the entry ceiling \(r_C\).

The zero-cost rival prepares after every order, so entry stays at least \(\rho\). With a positive spread, the competitive price \(P(x)=t_0+e(x)[t_L-t_0+\Delta_T\mu(x)]\) reveals the market makers' posterior. To see why, two orders with the same price induce the same entry. Since entry is positive, the price formula then requires the same posterior. When \(g_H\gt g_L\), gross profit covers the high cost exactly when the belief reaches the preparation threshold \(\tau=(c_H-g_L)/(g_H-g_L)\), so high-cost preparation follows exactly the orders whose posterior reaches \(\tau\); an indifferent high-cost rival prepares.

When informed trade pays

Market makers price the preparation response to each order. The informed trader earns from the class information that the price does not reveal. A purchase in class \(H\) and a sale in class \(L\) have gross returns

\[ \begin{gathered} K_H=e(1)\,\Delta_T\,[1-\mu(1)],\\ K_L=e(-1)\,\Delta_T\,\mu(-1),\\ \rho\,\kappa(\pi)\,\Delta_T\le K_H,\,K_L\le\Delta_T, \end{gathered} \]

where \(\kappa(\pi)=\min\{m(\pi),1-M(\pi)\}\). Each return equals entry times the spread times residual uncertainty about the class. Both bounds hold under every candidate profile, and the lower one is the trading bound. If the trading cost is below \(\rho\kappa\Delta_T\), a purchase in \(H\) and a sale in \(L\) are strictly optimal. If the cost exceeds \(\Delta_T\), both classes abstain. A sale in \(H\) or a purchase in \(L\) always loses after costs. Each return depends only on the posterior and entry after its own order, so the informed trader's problem splits by class.

When the spread is positive and \(g_H\gt g_L\), the informed trader's orders, the posteriors and the rival's preparation depend on the auction only through two numbers: the target-payoff spread \(\Delta_T\), which sets what an informed order earns, and the preparation threshold \(\tau\), which sets which posteriors recruit a high-cost rival. Two auctions with the same spread and threshold, and the same other primitives, have the same equilibrium orders, posteriors and entry. A stronger incumbent never narrows the spread, and it never lowers the threshold while a rival known to be in the low class earns at most the high cost, \(g_L\le c_H\). The two numbers can therefore pull in opposite directions: a wider spread raises what a recruiting purchase earns, and a higher threshold makes recruitment harder.

The example's payoffs and the bounds in detail

A prepared rival with value \(\theta\ge p\) pays \(\max\{p,R\}\) if it wins. If it loses, the incumbent pays \(\theta\). Target proceeds are therefore \(\max\{p,\min(R,\theta)\}\), and expected proceeds plus the rival's profit satisfy \(t(\theta)+g(\theta)=\theta\). In the example, with point-mass classes and a uniform incumbent, the payoffs are

\[ \begin{aligned} t_0&=p\Bigl(1-\frac pr\Bigr),& \Delta_T(r)&=\frac{(r-\ell)^2}{2r},\\ t_H&=\frac r2+\frac{p^2}{2r},& t_L&=\ell-\frac{\ell^2-p^2}{2r},\\ g_H&=h-\frac r2-\frac{p^2}{2r},& g_L&=\frac{\ell^2-p^2}{2r}, \end{aligned} \]

The derivatives are \(\Delta_T'(r)=\tfrac12-\ell^2/(2r^2)\gt0\), \(g_H'(r)=-\tfrac12+p^2/(2r^2)\lt0\) and \(g_L'(r)=-(\ell^2-p^2)/(2r^2)\lt0\), and the preparation threshold is \(\tau(r)=[2rc_H-(\ell^2-p^2)]/(2rh-r^2-\ell^2)\). For a general continuous \(F\), define \(D=G_L-G_H\). Then

\[ \begin{gathered} \Delta_T(F)=pF(p)D(p^-)+\int_p^\infty[1-F(y)]D(y)\,dy,\\ g_j(F)=\int_p^\infty[1-G_j(y)]F(y)\,dy . \end{gathered} \]

The reserve term vanishes under the class ranking. Without value atoms at the reserve, two conditions characterize a weak increase in the spread under every first-order stochastic increase in incumbent value. The high class must dominate above the reserve, and both classes must have the same probability of a value below it.

Expected proceeds without entry, \(t_0\), cancel from the informed trader's returns because they enter both the price and the terminal payoff. These proceeds are positive in the example; the zero preparation cost is a separate assumption. The likelihood ratio of each order lies between \(1/(1+u)\) and \(1+u\), which gives the posterior bounds. Since \(1-M(\pi)=m(1-\pi)\), the lower bound uses \(\kappa(\pi)=m(\min\{\pi,1-\pi\})\). This quantity is largest at the prior one half, where it equals \(1/(2+u)\). Away from one half, the price already favors one class and reduces that class's advantage.
