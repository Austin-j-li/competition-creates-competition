Two returns to information

2 · The mechanism · 3 minutes

The challenger uses information to assess its acquisition profit. The investor uses the same class information to value the target's shares. The auction connects these two returns: a stronger incumbent reduces the challenger's profit and makes target proceeds more sensitive to its class.

One auction, two claims

Let \(g_H,g_L\) denote expected acquisition profit before the preparation cost, and let \(t_H,t_L\) denote expected target proceeds with preparation. At belief \(\mu=\Pr(j=H)\), the challenger prepares when expected profit \(B(\mu)=g_L+\mu(g_H-g_L)\) covers its cost. The target-payoff spread \(\Delta_T=t_H-t_L\) measures how much more shareholders receive with a high-class challenger.

Under the second-price rule, a higher incumbent value moves part of the class value gap into target proceeds. At each value level between the reserve \(p\) and incumbent value \(R\), the incumbent outbids the challenger and pays its bid. The rest stays in challenger profit. Proposition 1 applies to every continuous incumbent distribution: a first-order stochastic increase in incumbent value weakly reduces both \(g_H\) and \(g_L\). Under the class ranking, it weakly raises \(\Delta_T\), and the two gaps sum to a constant:

\[ \begin{gathered} \Delta_T(F)+g_H(F)-g_L(F)\\ =\mathbb E_H[\max\{\theta,p\}]-\mathbb E_L[\max\{\theta,p\}], \end{gathered} \]

The constant is \(h-\ell\) at every benchmark strength \(r\). The class ranking requires first-order stochastic dominance of \(H\) over \(L\), with all values in both classes at or above the reserve.

What one order can reveal

A purchase can come from a liquidity trader in either class, so it leaves uncertainty about the class. The same applies to a sale and to no order. Each liquidity purchase or sale has probability \(b=(1-\lambda)\beta\), and the investor adds at most \(\lambda\) to an order's probability. With \(\beta\le1/3\), the posterior after every order obeys the same bounds under every pure or mixed investor strategy:

\[ \begin{gathered} m(\pi)\le\mu(x)\le M(\pi),\qquad u=\frac{\lambda}{b},\\ m(\pi)=\frac{\pi}{\pi+(1-\pi)(1+u)},\\ M(\pi)=\frac{\pi(1+u)}{\pi(1+u)+1-\pi}. \end{gathered} \]

When the high class buys and the low class sells, a sale, no order and a purchase yield posteriors \(m(\pi)\), \(\pi\) and \(M(\pi)\), respectively. At the benchmark prior, the upper bound is \(M=[[M_half_B]]\). If expected profit at this bound falls below \(c_H\), even the most favorable price cannot recruit a high-cost challenger. This is the entry ceiling.

The zero-cost challenger prepares after every order, so entry stays at least \(\rho\). With a positive spread, the competitive price \(P(x)=t_0+e(x)[t_L-t_0+\Delta_T\mu(x)]\) reveals the market makers' posterior. To see why, two orders with the same price induce the same entry. Since entry is positive, the price formula then requires the same posterior. When \(g_H\gt g_L\), high-cost preparation requires \(\mu(x)\ge\tau=(c_H-g_L)/(g_H-g_L)\), with preparation at equality under the baseline tie rule.

When informed trade pays

Market makers price the preparation response to each order. The investor earns from the class information that the price leaves unresolved. A purchase in class \(H\) and a sale in class \(L\) have gross returns

\[ \begin{gathered} K_H=e(1)\,\Delta_T\,[1-\mu(1)],\\ K_L=e(-1)\,\Delta_T\,\mu(-1),\\ \rho\,\kappa(\pi)\,\Delta_T\le K_H,\,K_L\le\Delta_T, \end{gathered} \]

where \(\kappa(\pi)=\min\{m(\pi),1-M(\pi)\}\). Each return equals entry times the spread times residual uncertainty about the class. These bounds hold under every candidate profile. If the trade cost is below \(\rho\kappa\Delta_T\), a purchase in \(H\) and a sale in \(L\) are strictly optimal. If the cost exceeds \(\Delta_T\), both classes abstain. A sale in \(H\) or a purchase in \(L\) always loses after costs.

Benchmark payoffs and the bounds in detail

A prepared challenger with value \(\theta\ge p\) pays \(\max\{p,R\}\) if it wins. If it loses, the incumbent pays \(\theta\). Target proceeds are therefore \(\max\{p,\min(R,\theta)\}\), and expected proceeds plus challenger profit satisfy \(t(\theta)+g(\theta)=\theta\). With point-mass classes and a uniform incumbent, the payoffs are

\[ \begin{aligned} t_0&=p\Bigl(1-\frac pr\Bigr),& \Delta_T(r)&=\frac{(r-\ell)^2}{2r},\\ t_H&=\frac r2+\frac{p^2}{2r},& t_L&=\ell-\frac{\ell^2-p^2}{2r},\\ g_H&=h-\frac r2-\frac{p^2}{2r},& g_L&=\frac{\ell^2-p^2}{2r}, \end{aligned} \]

The derivatives are \(\Delta_T'(r)=\tfrac12-\ell^2/(2r^2)\gt0\), \(g_H'(r)=-\tfrac12+p^2/(2r^2)\lt0\) and \(g_L'(r)=-(\ell^2-p^2)/(2r^2)\lt0\). For a general continuous \(F\), define \(D=G_L-G_H\). Then

\[ \begin{gathered} \Delta_T(F)=pF(p)D(p^-)+\int_p^\infty[1-F(y)]D(y)\,dy,\\ g_j(F)=\int_p^\infty[1-G_j(y)]F(y)\,dy . \end{gathered} \]

The reserve term vanishes under the class ranking. Without value atoms at the reserve, two conditions characterize a weak increase in the spread under every first-order stochastic increase in incumbent value. The high class must dominate above the reserve, and both classes must have the same probability of a value below it.

Expected proceeds without entry, \(t_0\), cancel from the investor's returns because they enter both the price and the terminal payoff. These proceeds are positive in the benchmark; the zero preparation cost is a separate assumption. The likelihood ratio of each order lies between \(1/(1+u)\) and \(1+u\), which gives the posterior bounds. Since \(1-M(\pi)=m(1-\pi)\), the lower bound uses \(\kappa(\pi)=m(\min\{\pi,1-\pi\})\). This quantity is largest at the prior one half, where it equals \(1/(2+u)\). Away from one half, the price already favors one class and reduces that class's advantage.
