Implications and next steps

4 · Implications · 2–3 minutes

Why sale terms matter

Holding incumbent strength at the strong benchmark, letting the challenger observe the price raises target proceeds and acquisition surplus net of preparation costs relative to withholding the price. The comparison holds the sale rule and the incumbent distribution fixed.

A reserve changes what a buyer must pay and what target shares reveal. If it excludes a low-value challenger, it changes how target proceeds differ across challenger types, and therefore changes the investor's incentive to trade. Bargaining can divide the surplus differently again; the paper characterizes its acquisition-stage payments.

Choosing sale terms requires more than selecting the largest revenue in a table. When preparation can fall to zero, a single price may pool several order-flow observations. Different pooling rules can then give the buyer different information even with the same investor orders. The paper establishes a family of such outcomes. A seller must account for the resulting orders, prices, beliefs and preparation decisions together.

Welfare, bargaining and reserve comparisons

Sale terms reach participation through the buyer's expected payment and through the information the market supplies. This section varies the second channel at fixed competition, then asks which payment rules produce the opposition behind Proposition 2.

Access to prices

Hold incumbent strength at \(r_1\). Compare the feedback equilibrium with an economy in which the challenger cannot observe the price. This represents a buyer able to see the market's response to a public opportunity versus one that must commit without that information.

Full investor orders arise in both economies, so trading costs are the same. Price access changes which high-cost challengers prepare, and each additional entrant expects a profit that covers its preparation cost. Proposition A.9 establishes higher target proceeds, [[base_revenue_feedback]] rather than [[base_revenue_hidden]], and a net acquisition-surplus gain of [[base_net_surplus_gain]]. The comparison also holds under Propositions A.5 and A.6.

Separating information from the price level

A deterministic dividend of [[base_matched_dividend]] added to the traded claim in the price-hidden economy matches mean prices across the two economies while leaving \(V_T-P\), trading incentives, and entry unchanged. Entry still differs, so the gap is information, not the price level. The dividend is a diagnostic payment, outside acquisition surplus and outside the seller's feasible terms.

The payment rule

The payment rule determines whether a stronger incumbent increases the target-payoff spread. In the benchmark auction, the competing bid sets the payment when both bidders clear the reserve. Now consider a negotiated sale with verifiable values and no reserve.

The highest-value buyer acquires the target. A sale to the next-best buyer at its value provides an enforceable fallback. The seller and winner split the surplus above that fallback through Nash bargaining, with seller share \(\eta\):

\[ \begin{aligned} T_\eta(R,\theta)&=(1-\eta)\min\{R,\theta\}+\eta\max\{R,\theta\},\\ G_{\theta,\eta}&=(1-\eta)\mathbb E[(\theta-R)_+],\\ \Delta_\eta&=\eta(h-\ell)+(1-2\eta)\mathbb E[(R-\ell)_+]. \end{aligned} \tag{14} \]

Proposition A.8 shows that a stronger incumbent weakly reduces challenger profit for every seller share \(\eta\). It weakly raises the target-payoff spread when \(\eta\lt1/2\), leaves it unchanged at \(\eta=1/2\), and weakly lowers it when \(\eta\gt1/2\); the comparison is strict when the change in the incumbent distribution has positive integral.

For \(R\gt\ell\), the high-value winner's payment rises with \(R\) at rate \(1-\eta\). After a low-value challenger loses, the incumbent's payment rises at rate \(\eta\). The spread widens precisely when the first rate is larger. The second-price rule with no reserve corresponds to \(\eta=0\). The trading and preparation game under bargaining is not solved here.

Bulow and Klemperer (1996) compare an auction with one more bidder against an optimally structured negotiation by the seller's expected revenue; here the comparison is by how competition moves the target-payoff spread.

Reserve comparisons

A reserve is the minimum payment the seller will accept. A reserve above the low challenger value excludes that type from bidding. Target proceeds in the low-value state then equal proceeds without preparation, increasing the difference between high- and low-value states. This can make informed trading worthwhile even against a weak incumbent.

The numerical comparison allows values to vary within bands of half-width \(\varepsilon_V=[[value_band_halfwidth]]\) around \(\ell\) and \(h\). Raising the reserve from [[base_p]] to [[value_reserve_high]] increases revenue against both incumbents and supports informative trading in both economies. Against the weak incumbent, revenue rises from [[value_revenue_weak_low_p]] to [[value_revenue_weak_high_p]] (Table 4).

The seller-optimal reserve remains open (Appendix A.7). Several equilibrium continuations coexist at many reserves, and the Online Appendix reports an exploratory summary over the reserve domain.

A pooled price changes the buyer's posterior and its willingness to prepare, even though investor orders are fixed. The seller therefore cannot identify a continuation by orders alone. What the reversal survives

The information channel does not require investors to be better informed overall than the buyer. In the declared example of the private-information extension, the buyer's signal is more accurate than the investor's, yet the price still adds useful information. A favorable private signal may leave preparation unattractive on its own; combined with a favorable price, it can make the investment worthwhile. This is why the target-specialist interpretation matters: different knowledge can be useful even to a knowledgeable acquirer.

The reversal also survives the paper's logistic-noise example, continuously distributed preparation costs and a narrower gap between acquisition values. The mechanism holds in each; its size changes. The rise from the weak to the strong incumbent survives each change; the later fall is shown for the benchmark only. How often a sufficiently favorable price occurs matters as well as how much the price could reveal.

Robustness: noise, costs and private information

The paper checks the reversal with a different noise distribution, continuously distributed preparation costs, a smaller acquisition-value gap, and a buyer whose private signal is more accurate than the investor's. The argument retains a bound on what orders can reveal and positive preparation at every price. The size of the effect depends on how often a price is favorable enough to justify high-cost preparation.

Noise and preparation costs

For the trading argument, the key restriction on noise is a bound on the derivative of its log density. Logistic noise satisfies this restriction. Proposition A.5 therefore preserves the unique trading outcomes and the preparation comparison.

The magnitude changes. Preparation against the strong incumbent is [[logistic_entry_strong]] under logistic noise, compared with [[base_entry_strong]] under Laplace noise. The logistic threshold lies [[logistic_threshold_noise_sd]] noise standard deviations from zero, so favorable observations are rare. Proposition A.6 also preserves parts (i) and (ii) of Proposition 2 when preparation costs are continuously distributed over bands of half-width [[cost_halfwidth]], under either noise law.

Acquisition values

With \(h=[[moderate_h]]\) and \(\ell=[[moderate_ell]]\), every strict inequality of Proposition 2 holds and entry rises from [[moderate_entry_weak]] to [[moderate_entry_strong]] between \(r_0=[[moderate_r_weak]]\) and \(r_1=[[moderate_r_strong]]\). What the reversal needs is the placement of incumbent strength relative to the low acquisition value, not a large value gap.

Complementary private information

A more accurate private signal need not contain all the information in the price. A buyer may know its integration plans, while a specialist investor knows more about the target's customers or technology. The extension represents this distinction with conditionally independent signals of accuracy \(d\) for the buyer and \(a\) for the investor.

Positive preparation keeps the price informative about the public posterior. Proposition A.7 gives sufficient conditions for the same unique trading and preparation outcomes without requiring \(a\gt d\). With buyer accuracy \(d=[[signal_buyer_accuracy]]\) and investor accuracy \(a=[[signal_trader_accuracy]]\), preparation rises from [[signal_entry_weak]] to [[signal_entry_strong]]. A favorable private signal alone does not justify the high preparation cost against the weak incumbent; combined with a favorable price, it does against the strong one.

What the model predicts

The model gives three predictions for publicly visible sale processes in which one bidder is prepared and a further buyer has not yet committed to diligence. The first and third compare processes that differ in the public strength of the prepared bidder; the second compares buyers within one process.

  1. A stronger bidder can draw a buyer in. When the target's price precedes the further buyer's decision, a stronger prepared bidder can raise the probability that the buyer starts diligence, as in the move from \(r_0\) to \(r_1\) in Proposition 2. When the price does not reach the buyer before its decision, a stronger prepared bidder weakly lowers that probability (Proposition A.3).
  2. Buyers that prepare after good news win more often. When informed trading is active, a further buyer that prepares after a favorable target return wins more often than one that prepares after an unfavorable return. Preparation cost is independent of value, so at each price the share of high-value buyers among those that prepare equals the price-implied posterior, which rises with the price. A high-value buyer always outbids the incumbent; a low-value buyer loses whenever the incumbent's value exceeds \(\ell\).
  3. Against a very strong bidder, entry stops responding. Against a sufficiently strong prepared bidder, further preparation returns to its floor and stops responding to the target's price, while the price stays informative (Proposition 2(iii)).

The empirical counterpart of preparation is the start of substantive diligence or a proposal that requires costly preparation; public offers are a narrower outcome. Target returns before a bid also partly reflect anticipation of that bid (Schwert 1996), so each test dates the price before the buyer's decision. The pilot in Online Appendix D dates these decisions and the public information sets from disclosure records.

What I would pursue next

The next theoretical step is to solve the seller's choice of terms while accounting for the possible trading, pricing and preparation outcomes. A related question is whether committing before trading improves on revising terms after prices are observed. On the empirical side, the test is whether further preparation in publicly visible sale processes responds to incumbent strength as the three predictions say.

The contribution is a link between the payment rule and the information that recruits bidders. The rule a seller picks sets what a challenger would pay and what the stock price can tell it before it decides to prepare.
