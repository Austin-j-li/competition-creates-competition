What the paper establishes

3 · The result · 3 minutes

Main result · Proposition 2 · analytical

For a robust range of values and costs, a stronger incumbent switches the market from no informed trading and low-cost preparation only to informative trading and additional high-cost preparation. The paper proves these are the only trading and preparation outcomes reached in the two comparison economies. Participation rises, and so does the probability that a high-value challenger acquires the target.

Three conditions drive the result. Low preparation costs keep the challenger willing to enter even after bad news. High preparation costs make favorable news necessary. Trading costs make informed orders unprofitable against the weak incumbent but worthwhile against the strong one. These conditions hold over a range of parameter values, so the result does not depend on a single calibration.

Hold the information fixed

To isolate the information effect, hold investor orders fixed at the same informative profile in both economies. A stronger incumbent then reduces preparation, as the usual deterrence argument predicts. In equilibrium, however, the investor does not trade against the weak incumbent and does trade against the strong one. Allowing the information to change reverses the preparation comparison.

Preparation is the decision to pay to learn. Bidding follows if the prepared buyer can submit an admissible offer. Ownership requires winning, and high-value ownership counts only wins by the high-value challenger. These outcomes should not be conflated. In the benchmark, high-value challenger ownership rises from [[base_ownership_weak:percent]] to [[base_ownership_strong:percent]], so the participation gain also changes who acquires the target.

A still stronger incumbent need not attract more preparation. At the third benchmark strength, even the best attainable price cannot justify the high cost, and preparation returns to [[base_entry_collapse:percent]]. The three benchmarks establish a rise followed by a fall. They do not establish a smooth hump or a monotone path between those points.

At intermediate strengths, informative and uninformative outcomes can coexist. The paper certifies selected examples and reports numerical searches more broadly. The main result avoids choosing a preferred branch by establishing unique trading and on-path preparation outcomes in its comparison economies. Pricing rules for events that do not occur in equilibrium need not be unique.

Formal conditions and the proof of the participation reversal

Proposition 2

Proposition 2 (competition creates competition).

Fix \(0\lt p\lt\ell\lt r_0\lt r_1\lt h\), \(0\lt\rho\lt1\), \(b\gt1\), and \(k\gt0\), and suppose that

\[ 0\le c_L\lt B_{r_1}(m), \tag{A1} \]
\[ B_{r_0}(1/2)\lt c_H\lt B_{r_1}(M), \tag{A2} \]
\[ \Delta_T(r_0)\lt k\lt\left(1-\frac1b\right)\rho m\Delta_T(r_1). \tag{A3} \]

(i) At \(r_0\) the unique trading and on-path preparation outcome has no informed trading and entry \(\rho\). (ii) At \(r_1\) the unique trading outcome has full orders \((1,-1)\), a price that Blackwell dominates the weak-incumbent experiment, entry above \(\rho\), and a higher probability that the high-value challenger acquires the target. (iii) At any \(r_2\in(r_1,h)\) with \(c_L\lt B_{r_2}(m)\), \(B_{r_2}(M)\lt c_H\), and \(k\lt(1-1/b)\rho m\Delta_T(r_2)\), trading stays informative but entry returns to \(\rho\). The comparisons hold on a nonempty open set of primitives.

Reading the conditions

(A1) keeps the low-cost challenger in at every feasible belief; (A2) keeps the high-cost challenger out at the weak prior but lets favorable strong-incumbent prices bring it in; (A3) puts the trading cost above every informational return in the weak economy and below a global bound on marginal trading profits in the strong one.

Full statement

(i) With the weak incumbent \(r_0\), the unique equilibrium trading outcome is \(q_H=q_L=0\), the price carries no information, and entry equals \(\rho\). (ii) With the strong incumbent \(r_1\), the unique equilibrium trading outcome is \((q_H,q_L)=(1,-1)\), the price is informative, entry strictly exceeds \(\rho\), and the probability that the high-value challenger acquires the target is strictly higher than at \(r_0\). The strong-incumbent price experiment strictly Blackwell dominates the weak-incumbent experiment at the same noise law. (iii) For any \(r_2\in(r_1,h)\) with \(c_L\lt B_{r_2}(m)\), \(B_{r_2}(M)\lt c_H\), and \(k\lt(1-1/b)\rho m\Delta_T(r_2)\), the unique trading outcome is again \((1,-1)\), but entry returns to \(\rho\). These comparisons hold on a nonempty open set of primitives. Uniqueness refers to trading and on-path entry under truthful bidding and allows arbitrary mixed orders and every continuous deviation.

The proof bounds trading profits over every candidate strategy, including mixed orders. Against the weak incumbent, no informed order covers its cost. Against the strong incumbent, a full order is better than every smaller order. This establishes uniqueness without selecting among numerical solutions.

With full orders, the price crosses the high-cost preparation threshold \(\tau\) more often when challenger value is high: \(\alpha_H\gt\alpha_L\). Additional preparation therefore favors high-value challengers. Equation (12) gives the probabilities used by the explorer. The comparison applies to the specified economies and does not impose a monotone path between them.

The full-order candidate (12)
\[ \begin{aligned} \tau&=\frac{c_H-g_L}{g_H-g_L},& x^*&=\frac b2\log\frac\tau{1-\tau},\\ \alpha_H&=1-\frac12e^{(x^*-1)/b},& \alpha_L&=\frac12e^{-(x^*+1)/b},\\ \mathsf E&=\rho+\frac{1-\rho}{2}(\alpha_H+\alpha_L),& \mathsf O_H&=\frac12[\rho+(1-\rho)\alpha_H]. \end{aligned} \tag{12} \]

\(\tau\) is the posterior at which the expensive challenger is indifferent, \(x^*\) the order flow at which the full-order posterior reaches it, and \(\alpha_H,\alpha_L\) the probabilities that flow exceeds \(x^*\) given a high- or a low-value challenger.

Proof sketch

The proof has six steps.

  1. Bound beliefs. The likelihood-ratio bound in (8) places every market-maker posterior in \([m,M]\). The price posterior inherits these bounds.
  2. Recover information from prices. Low-cost preparation keeps the denominator in (10) positive, so the challenger can recover the market maker's posterior from the price.
  3. Rule out trading against the weak incumbent. Any correctly signed order of size \(s\) earns at most \(s[\Delta_T(r_0)-k]\lt0\). A wrong-signed order also loses money. Zero trade is the only best response.
  4. Establish full orders against the strong incumbent. The Laplace derivative bound and (A3) make profit strictly increasing in order magnitude across the feasible interval.
  5. Construct the informative equilibrium. Full orders imply a posterior and a strictly increasing price function. The preceding bounds verify pricing and investor optimality.
  6. Compare preparation and ownership. Condition (A2) puts the high-cost threshold between the prior and the upper posterior bound. Favorable prices induce additional preparation, more often for a high-value challenger. Strict inequalities make the result robust to small parameter changes.

For the still stronger incumbent in part (iii), trading remains informative, but \(B_{r_2}(M)\lt c_H\) rules out high-cost preparation at every price. Appendix A.3 contains the full proof.
