Competition creates competition

Three economies differ only in incumbent strength. Against the weak incumbent the two challenger types leave target proceeds almost the same, the spread \(\Delta_T(r_0)\) is below the trading cost, and no order can pay for itself, so the price is flat, the market maker's belief stays at the prior, and only the cheap challenger prepares. Against the strong incumbent the spread is wide enough that every unit of a correctly signed order earns more than it costs, so the investor trades to its limit whatever anyone else does. The price now separates good news from bad, and after good news the expensive challenger prepares too, even though its profit at any fixed belief is lower than before. Against a still stronger incumbent the investor still trades, but the most favorable belief the market can reach no longer makes expensive preparation pay, and entry falls back to the floor.

Proposition 2

Proposition 2 (competition creates competition).

Fix \(0\lt p\lt\ell\lt r_0\lt r_1\lt h\), \(0\lt\rho\lt1\), \(b\gt1\), and \(k\gt0\), and suppose that

\[ 0\le c_L\lt B_{r_1}(m), \tag{A1} \]
\[ B_{r_0}(1/2)\lt c_H\lt B_{r_1}(M), \tag{A2} \]
\[ \Delta_T(r_0)\lt k\lt\left(1-\frac1b\right)\rho m\Delta_T(r_1). \tag{A3} \]

(i) At \(r_0\) the unique equilibrium has no informed trading and entry \(\rho\). (ii) At \(r_1\) it has full orders \((1,-1)\), a price that Blackwell dominates the weak-incumbent experiment, entry above \(\rho\), and a higher probability that the high-value challenger acquires the target. (iii) At any \(r_2\in(r_1,h)\) with \(c_L\lt B_{r_2}(m)\), \(B_{r_2}(M)\lt c_H\), and \(k\lt(1-1/b)\rho m\Delta_T(r_2)\), trading stays informative but entry returns to \(\rho\). The comparisons hold on a nonempty open set of primitives.

Reading the conditions

(A1) keeps the low-cost challenger in at every feasible belief; (A2) keeps the high-cost challenger out at the weak prior but lets favorable strong-incumbent prices bring it in; (A3) puts the trading cost above every informational return in the weak economy and below a global bound on marginal trading profits in the strong one.

Full statement

(i) With the weak incumbent \(r_0\), the unique equilibrium trading outcome is \(q_H=q_L=0\), the price carries no information, and entry equals \(\rho\). (ii) With the strong incumbent \(r_1\), the unique equilibrium trading outcome is \((q_H,q_L)=(1,-1)\), the price is informative, entry strictly exceeds \(\rho\), and the probability that the high-value challenger acquires the target is strictly higher than at \(r_0\). The strong-incumbent price experiment strictly Blackwell dominates the weak-incumbent experiment at the same noise law. (iii) For any \(r_2\in(r_1,h)\) with \(c_L\lt B_{r_2}(m)\), \(B_{r_2}(M)\lt c_H\), and \(k\lt(1-1/b)\rho m\Delta_T(r_2)\), the unique trading outcome is again \((1,-1)\), but entry returns to \(\rho\). These comparisons hold on a nonempty open set of primitives. Uniqueness refers to trading and on-path entry under truthful bidding and allows arbitrary mixed orders and every continuous deviation.

Uniqueness comes from dominance, not from a fixed point. In each economy the trading cost is compared with a global bound on the investor's residual advantage, so there is no conjectured equilibrium to check around. Under full orders the price crosses the expensive challenger's threshold \(\tau\) with probabilities \(\alpha_H\gt\alpha_L\), so the added entry is tilted toward high-value challengers; the closed forms in (12) are what the explorer computes. The proposition establishes a rise and a subsequent fall across the specified economies and imposes no monotone path between them.

The full-order candidate (12)
\[ \begin{aligned} \tau&=\frac{c_H-g_L}{g_H-g_L},& x^*&=\frac b2\log\frac\tau{1-\tau},\\ \alpha_H&=1-\frac12e^{(x^*-1)/b},& \alpha_L&=\frac12e^{-(x^*+1)/b},\\ \mathsf E&=\rho+\frac{1-\rho}{2}(\alpha_H+\alpha_L),& \mathsf O_H&=\frac12[\rho+(1-\rho)\alpha_H]. \end{aligned} \tag{12} \]

\(\tau\) is the posterior at which the expensive challenger is indifferent, \(x^*\) the order flow at which the full-order posterior reaches it, and \(\alpha_H,\alpha_L\) the probabilities that flow exceeds \(x^*\) given a high- or a low-value challenger.

Proof sketch

Six steps. First, beliefs are bounded under every trading strategy: positivity of \(f\) and the likelihood-ratio bound (8) give \(m\le\mu_X\le M\), and the price posterior inherits the interval by conditional expectation. Second, the buyer recovers the market maker's posterior from the price: the low-cost type enters at every feasible price, so entry is at least \(\rho\), the denominator in (10) is positive, and \(\Pr(H\mid P)=\mu_X\) almost surely. Third, in the weak economy a correctly signed order of size \(s\) earns at most \(s[\Delta_T(r_0)-k]\lt0\), and a wrong-signed order has negative gross payoff and still pays its cost, so zero is the only best response against every candidate. Fourth, in the strong economy the translation formula for the Laplace kernel gives \(|F'|\le F/b\), the residual bound gives \(F\ge\rho m\Delta_T\), and (A3) makes \(U'(s)\ge(1-1/b)\rho m\Delta_T-k\gt0\) on the whole interval, so the full order dominates every smaller magnitude. Fifth, the informative equilibrium exists: full orders give \(\mu_X(x)=[1+\exp\{-(|x+1|-|x-1|)/b\}]^{-1}\), the strictly increasing price \(P_r(\mu)\) has a measurable inverse across its jump, and the bounds above verify investor optimality and pricing. Sixth, (A2) places \(\tau\) in \((1/2,M)\), the Laplace survival function evaluated at \(x^*-1\) and \(x^*+1\) gives (12), no state-independent transformation of a constant experiment reproduces a nonconstant one (Blackwell), and strict margins make the region open. Part (iii) repeats the fourth and fifth steps at \(r_2\) and uses \(B_{r_2}(M)\lt c_H\) to exclude every expensive entrant. Appendix A.3 of the paper has the full argument.

The benchmark and its information controls

From \(r_0=[[base_r_weak]]\) to \(r_1=[[base_r_strong]]\), entry rises from [[base_entry_weak]] to [[base_entry_strong]] and high-value ownership from [[base_ownership_weak]] to [[base_ownership_strong]]; at \(r_2=[[base_r_collapse]]\) entry is back at [[base_entry_collapse]]. Two controls show where the rise comes from. Freeze the investor's orders at their informative level in both economies and stronger competition cuts entry from [[base_frozen_entry_weak]] to [[base_frozen_entry_strong]], as deterrence predicts (Proposition A.3). Hide the price from the challenger and entry is [[base_hidden_entry_weak]] and [[base_hidden_entry_strong]]. The reversal is the information that trading generates, not the payment.

Coexisting equilibria at intermediate strength

Between the two analytical regions, Proposition 3 establishes by computer-assisted proof three equilibria with full purchases and partial sales \((q_H,q_L)=(1,-v_j)\) that coexist with no trade:

\[ \begin{array}{ccc} r_j&\qquad v_j&\qquad\mathsf E_j\\ [[cert_a_r]]&\qquad[[cert_a_v_interval]]&\qquad[[cert_a_entry_interval]]\\ [[cert_b_r]]&\qquad[[cert_b_v_interval]]&\qquad[[cert_b_entry_interval]]\\ [[cert_c_r]]&\qquad[[cert_c_v_interval]]&\qquad[[cert_c_entry_interval]] \end{array} \tag{13} \]

The entry intervals are strictly ordered upward. These are computer-assisted proofs. Interval arithmetic on exact decimal inputs encloses each equilibrium, and the sketch says what is certified. The asymmetry is economic. Near the high-cost threshold an order moves both the posterior and the probability of entry, so the two investor types face different residual schedules. No trade survives alongside these equilibria because, against a flat price with entry \(\rho\), a lone informed order cannot cover its cost; an informative price changes the entry response and with it the return to trading, so both outcomes are self-consistent at the same parameters.

Full statement of Proposition 3

Proposition 3 (coexisting informative equilibria at intermediate strength).

At the benchmark parameters of Appendix A.6, there exist equilibria \((q_H,q_L)=(1,-v_j)\) at strengths \(r_j\) whose certified enclosures are

\[ \begin{array}{ccc} r_j&\qquad v_j&\qquad\mathsf E_j\\ [[cert_a_r]]&\qquad[[cert_a_v_interval]]&\qquad[[cert_a_entry_interval]]\\ [[cert_b_r]]&\qquad[[cert_b_v_interval]]&\qquad[[cert_b_entry_interval]]\\ [[cert_c_r]]&\qquad[[cert_c_v_interval]]&\qquad[[cert_c_entry_interval]] \end{array} \]

The entry intervals are strictly ordered upward. Each of the three economies also admits no trade with entry \(\rho\).

Proof sketch

Fix \((q_H,q_L)=(1,-v)\). The low-value investor's residual is bounded, nonconstant, and nondecreasing, so it defines a finite positive Stieltjes measure and \(F_L'(s)\lt0\) with \(|F_L''|\le-F_L'/b\); hence \(U_L''\lt0\) on \([0,1]\) and a root of the marginal-profit equation is the unique global short magnitude. Let \(\Psi(r,v)\) be that marginal profit evaluated at \(v\) with the candidate schedule held fixed. Outward interval evaluation shows \(\Psi\gt0\) at the left endpoint of each bracket in (13) and \(\Psi\lt0\) at the right endpoint, so an exact root lies inside. For the high-value investor, the Laplace identity \(f''=(f-\delta_0)/b^2\) bounds \(|U_H''|\) by \(L_U=2\Delta_T/b+\Delta_T/b^2\); interval evaluation of \(U_H'\) on a mesh, minus \(L_U/(2n)\), gives a positive uniform lower bound, with certified margins [[cert_a_high_derivative_lower]], [[cert_b_high_derivative_lower]], and [[cert_c_high_derivative_lower]] at the three strengths. Wrong-signed trades have negative gross profit. The entry enclosures are disjoint and ordered upward. Appendix A.4 of the paper and Online Appendix B give the elementary primitives and the replication recipe.

The search found no mixed equilibrium, a search result rather than a nonexistence theorem; the complete intermediate correspondence is open.

Thresholds on the strength axis

No trade remains an equilibrium up to \(r_N=[[base_r_no_trade_exact]]\) and is uniquely optimal below \(\mathfrak r(k)=[[base_r_pool_unique_sufficient]]\); full orders are uniquely optimal above \(r_U=[[base_r_full_unique_sufficient]]\); high-cost entry is infeasible above \(r_C=[[base_r_high_cost_ceiling]]\), where entry drops from [[base_laplace_entry_ceiling_left_limit]] to \(\rho=[[base_rho]]\). An existence boundary, a sufficient uniqueness bound, and a participation ceiling answer different questions and need not coincide (Proposition A.4).
