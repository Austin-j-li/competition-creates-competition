The claim

A stronger incumbent bidder can attract a challenger. I study a takeover auction in which a prospective buyer observes the target's stock price before paying to learn its own acquisition value. Stronger competition lowers that buyer's acquisition profit at every belief, but it also raises the sensitivity of target proceeds to the buyer's value, and that second effect makes informed trading in target shares worth its cost. On an open set of parameters, strengthening the incumbent switches the unique equilibrium from an uninformative price to an informative one, and entry rises.

The received wisdom runs the other way: a challenger must pay to prepare before it can bid, and a stronger prepared rival lowers the return to preparing (Fishman 1988; Hirshleifer and Png 1989). That force never switches off in my model. What changes is what the challenger knows when it decides. In a second-price sale the loser sets the price. Against a strong incumbent, a high-value challenger wins and pays the incumbent's value, and a low-value challenger loses and the incumbent pays the challenger's value. The stronger the incumbent, the further apart those two payments sit, so the target's proceeds hinge on which kind of challenger is coming. An investor who knows the answer has more to trade on, the price moves further with the news, and the challenger reads that price before it pays to prepare. When the information effect outweighs deterrence, competition creates competition.
