Inside a takeover process

1 · The setting · 2 minutes

An illustrative sale process

A listed software company announces a strategic review. One buyer is ready to bid. A second sees a possible fit but must investigate the technology, check the contracts and arrange financing first. Before it pays for that work, it watches the target's shares.

Preparation is the costly work that turns interest into an executable offer. A buyer that does not prepare stays out of the sale; it cannot bid on a guess. Preparation costs are sunk, and they differ across buyers: a buyer with capacity in place or prior familiarity with the target prepares cheaply, and others do not.

Who knows what

The prepared incumbent knows its acquisition value \(R\). Its strength is public: \(R\) has a distribution \(F\), uniform on \([0,r]\) in the benchmark, and a higher \(r\) is a stronger incumbent, not a higher announced bid. The challenger belongs to a high or a low quality class, \(j\in\{H,L\}\), and its value \(\theta\) has a class distribution \(G_j\). It knows neither its class nor its value. After it sees the stock price, it learns its preparation cost:

\[ \Pr(j=H)=\pi,\qquad C=\begin{cases}0&\text{w.p. }\rho,\\c_H&\text{w.p. }1-\rho.\end{cases} \]

Paying \(C\) reveals \(\theta\) and permits a bid. The seller has committed to a cash second-price auction with reserve \(p\), so each prepared bidder bids its value.

An outside specialist investor knows the challenger's class, though not its value. It holds no position and has no control rights. Trade happens in one public order slot before the challenger decides. With probability \(\lambda\) the investor fills the slot with a sale, no order or a purchase of one unit, at cost \(k\) for a nonzero order. Otherwise a liquidity trader fills it:

\[ \begin{gathered} \Pr(\text{sale})=\Pr(\text{purchase})=\beta,\\ \Pr(\text{no order})=1-2\beta,\qquad 0\lt\beta\le\tfrac13 . \end{gathered} \]

No one sees who filled the slot. Competitive market makers see the order and set the price at the expected terminal value of a share. The challenger sees the price and its own cost, and nothing else.

  1. The sale opportunity is public. The seller commits to a cash second-price auction with reserve \(p\). One bidder is prepared; the challenger has not decided.
  2. The investor learns the challenger's class \(j\). The challenger, the market makers and the incumbent do not.
  3. With probability \(\lambda\) the investor places its order; otherwise a liquidity trader sells, abstains or buys. Only the order is public, not who placed it.
  4. Market makers price the order. They anticipate how the price will change the challenger's decision and therefore the sale proceeds.
  5. The challenger reads a belief about its class from the price, learns its cost, and prepares when expected acquisition profit covers that cost.
  6. Prepared bidders learn their values and bid them. The highest admissible bidder wins and pays the larger of the reserve and the other admissible bid.

Select a stage to see who knows what at that point.

The sale must become public before the set of buyers is fixed; only then can a prospective buyer watch the price while it decides. A disclosed approach, an announced review or an open contest can create that window. Imprivata's 2016 definitive proxy shows the stage the model describes: an unsolicited approach, outreach to other buyers, and indications of interest that still depend on diligence.

The model in full, the benchmark and related literature

All agents are risk neutral, the target's standalone value is zero, and values and costs are per share. The pair consisting of class and challenger value, the incumbent's value, the preparation cost, and the slot draws are mutually independent. Bids are truthful and weakly dominant. Without a challenger, the incumbent buys at the reserve when its value meets it, so expected proceeds without entry are \(t_0=p[1-F(p)]\). They are positive when the incumbent can meet the reserve, as in the benchmark. A zero-cost challenger prepares after every price, because its gross profit is an auction rent and never negative. The classes satisfy the class ranking: \(G_H\) dominates \(G_L\), and both lie above the reserve.

An equilibrium gives each class a distribution over the three orders, a posterior and a price after each order, the challenger's belief and preparation at each price, and truthful bids. The investor may mix and may place a wrong-signed order. A deviation by the investor is evaluated at the candidate's prices and entry; a comparison across incumbent strengths solves for the equilibrium again in each economy. An indifferent investor may mix over its best orders, and an indifferent high-cost challenger prepares.

The benchmark

Point-mass classes put the high class at \(h\) and the low class at \(\ell\), so class and value coincide, and \(0\lt p\lt\ell\lt r\lt h\). Money is measured in units of the low value. The slot has \(1+u=\mathrm e\), where \(u=\lambda/b\) is the informed presence relative to the liquidity mass \(b=(1-\lambda)\beta\) in one direction.

Related literature

The paper belongs to the literature on the real effects of financial markets (Bond, Edmans and Goldstein 2012). The closest antecedent is Dow, Goldstein and Guembel (2017), where a firm's investment decision feeds back into the incentive to produce information about its stock; here the sale rule divides acquisition surplus between a traded claim and a buyer that decides whether to enter. Edmans, Goldstein and Jiang (2015) show that corrective real decisions can discourage trade on bad news; here the real decision is the buyer's entry. Firms learn from peers' prices (Foucault and Frésard 2014), and target prices affect takeover activity (Edmans, Goldstein and Jiang 2012).

The auction-entry literature makes the bidder pool endogenous (Samuelson 1985; McAfee and McMillan 1987; Levin and Smith 1994; Gentry and Stroup 2019). Under selective entry, a bidder's information before entry indexes the distribution of its value, and paying the entry cost reveals the value (Roberts and Sweeting 2013). Here that information is the target's stock price, which moves with the orders of an investor who knows the challenger's class. The slot follows the alternative-arrival structure of Dow and Gorton (1997), and the unit order menu, the presence probability and the unit cost follow Edmans, Goldstein and Jiang (2015).
