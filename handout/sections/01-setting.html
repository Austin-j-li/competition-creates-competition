Inside a takeover process

1 · The setting · 2 minutes

An illustrative sale process

A listed software company announces a strategic review. One bidder is ready to bid. A second sees a possible fit but must investigate the technology, check the contracts and arrange financing first. Before it pays for that work, it watches the target's shares.

Preparation is the costly work that turns interest into an executable offer. A bidder that does not prepare stays out of the sale; it cannot bid on a guess. Preparation costs are sunk, and they differ across bidders: a bidder with capacity in place or prior familiarity with the target prepares cheaply, and others do not.

Who knows what

One bidder is already prepared; I call it the incumbent bidder, or the incumbent. It is the party that approached the target or a bidder that has completed diligence. The incumbent knows its acquisition value \(R\). Its strength is the public distribution \(F\) of \(R\), and a first-order stochastic shift of \(F\) strengthens the incumbent. A second bidder, the potential rival bidder, or the rival, has not decided whether to prepare. The rival belongs to a high or a low quality class, \(j\in\{H,L\}\), and its value \(\theta\) has a class distribution \(G_j\). It knows neither its class nor its value. After it sees the stock price, it learns its preparation cost:

\[ \Pr(j=H)=\pi,\qquad C=\begin{cases}0&\text{w.p. }\rho,\\c_H&\text{w.p. }1-\rho.\end{cases} \]

Paying \(C\) reveals \(\theta\) and permits a bid. The seller has committed to a cash second-price auction with reserve \(p\), so each prepared bidder bids its value.

An outside informed trader with sector expertise knows the rival's class and remains uncertain about the rival's value. It holds no position and has no control rights. Trade happens in one public order slot before the rival decides. With probability \(\lambda\) the informed trader fills the slot with a sale, no order or a purchase of one unit, at cost \(k\) for a nonzero order. Otherwise a liquidity trader fills it:

\[ \begin{gathered} \Pr(\text{sale})=\Pr(\text{purchase})=\beta,\\ \Pr(\text{no order})=1-2\beta,\qquad 0\lt\beta\le\tfrac13 . \end{gathered} \]

No one sees who filled the slot. Competitive market makers see the order and set the price at the expected terminal value of a share. The rival sees the price and its own cost, and nothing else.

  1. The sale opportunity is public. The seller commits to a cash second-price auction with reserve \(p\). One bidder is prepared; the rival has not decided.
  2. The informed trader learns the rival's class \(j\). The rival, the market makers and the incumbent do not.
  3. With probability \(\lambda\) the informed trader places its order; otherwise a liquidity trader sells, abstains or buys. The order is public; the identity of its trader remains hidden.
  4. Market makers price the order. They anticipate how the price will change the rival's decision and therefore the sale proceeds.
  5. The rival reads a belief about its class from the price, learns its cost, and prepares when expected acquisition profit covers that cost.
  6. The incumbent bids its value. A prepared rival learns its value and bids it. The highest admissible bidder wins and pays the larger of the reserve and the other admissible bid.

Select a stage to see who knows what at that point.

The sale must become public before the set of bidders is fixed; only then can a potential rival watch the price while it decides. A disclosed approach, an announced review or an open contest can create that window. Imprivata's 2016 definitive proxy shows the stage the model describes: an unsolicited approach, outreach to other buyers, and indications of interest that still depend on diligence.

The model in full, the example and related literature

All agents are risk neutral, the target's standalone value is zero, and values and costs are per share. The pair consisting of class and the rival's value, the incumbent's value, the preparation cost, and the slot draws are mutually independent. Bids are truthful and weakly dominant. Without a rival, the incumbent buys at the reserve when its value meets it, so expected proceeds without entry are \(t_0=p[1-F(p)]\). They are positive when the incumbent can meet the reserve, as in the example. A zero-cost rival prepares after every price, because its gross profit is an auction rent and never negative. The classes satisfy the class ranking: \(G_H\) dominates \(G_L\), and both lie above the reserve. A path is a family \(F_r\) of continuous incumbent distributions indexed by a strength \(r\), in which a higher \(r\) is a first-order stochastic strengthening.

An equilibrium gives each class a distribution over the three orders, a posterior and a price after each order, the rival's belief and preparation at each price, and truthful bids. The informed trader may mix and may place a wrong-signed order. A deviation by the informed trader takes the candidate's prices and entry as given; a comparison across incumbent strengths solves for the equilibrium again in each economy. When ties arise, an indifferent informed trader may mix over its best orders, and an indifferent high-cost rival prepares; these are the default tie rules. The other two rules are that an indifferent informed trader abstains and that an indifferent high-cost rival may prepare with any probability.

The example

The example has two point-mass classes, the high class at \(h\) and the low class at \(\ell\), so class and value coincide. The incumbent is uniform on \([0,r]\), so a higher \(r\) is a stronger incumbent, and \(0\lt p\lt\ell\lt r\lt h\) at every strength of its path. Money is measured in units of the low value. The slot has \(1+u=\mathrm e\), where \(u=\lambda/b\) is the informed presence relative to the liquidity mass \(b=(1-\lambda)\beta\) in one direction.

Related literature

The paper belongs to the literature on the real effects of financial markets (Bond, Edmans and Goldstein 2012). The closest antecedent is Dow, Goldstein and Guembel (2017), where a firm's investment decision feeds back into the incentive to produce information about its stock; here the sale rule divides acquisition surplus between a traded claim and a rival that decides whether to prepare. Edmans, Goldstein and Jiang (2015) show that corrective real decisions can discourage trade on bad news under rational pricing; here the real decision is the rival's entry itself. Firms learn from peers' prices (Foucault and Frésard 2014), and target prices affect takeover activity (Edmans, Goldstein and Jiang 2012); here a potential rival learns about its own acquisition value from the target's price before it commits.

The auction-entry literature makes the bidder pool endogenous (Samuelson 1985; McAfee and McMillan 1987; Levin and Smith 1994; Gentry and Stroup 2019). Under selective entry, a bidder's information before entry indexes the distribution of its value, and paying the entry cost reveals the value (Roberts and Sweeting 2013). Here that information is the target's stock price, which moves with the orders of an informed trader who knows the rival's class. The slot follows the alternative-arrival structure of Dow and Gorton (1997), and the unit order menu, the presence probability and the unit cost follow Edmans, Goldstein and Jiang (2015).
